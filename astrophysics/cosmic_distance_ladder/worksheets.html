Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Why a ladder?

What to look for: Hubble's law reaches the edge of the universe but needs H₀, which comes from supernova distances. Supernovae need their brightness calibrated by Cepheids in the same galaxies; Cepheids need parallax; parallax needs the size of Earth's orbit, from radar. Each method works where the one below overlaps it.

Investigation 2: Radar echo from Venus

What to look for: About 276 s there and back, so d = 3.00 × 10⁸ × 276 ÷ 2 ≈ 4.1 × 10¹⁰ m (41 million km). Kepler's laws say Venus is then 0.277 AU away, so 1 AU = 4.1 × 10¹⁰ ÷ 0.277 ≈ 1.50 × 10¹¹ m.

Investigation 3: The gradient is the AU

What to look for: A straight line through the origin: the gradient is the length of 1 AU in km (about 150 million). Kepler's laws give every distance in the Solar System in AU; radar turns AU into metres.

Investigation 4: Measure Proxima Centauri

What to look for: p = 0.768″ (half the total back-and-forth shift). d = 1/p = 1.30 pc = 4.2 light-years. That angle is about the width of a coin seen from 6 km away.

Investigation 5: How far can parallax reach?

What to look for: With an uncertainty of 1 mas, a 10% measurement needs p = 10 mas: d = 100 pc. Gaia (0.025 mas) reaches 10% at 4000 pc, forty times further. Beyond that the shift is lost in the measurement error, so we need another method.

Investigation 6: Leavitt's law

What to look for: Longer period = more luminous. The period gives M (from the law calibrated by parallax). The difference m − M (about 24.4) gives d = 10^((m − M + 5)/5) pc ≈ 0.77 Mpc. In 1925 this proved Andromeda is a separate galaxy.

Investigation 7: Too faint to see

What to look for: At 35 Mpc even the brightest Cepheids are around magnitude 27: invisible from the ground, only the longest-period ones are seen by Hubble, more by JWST. Beyond about 40 Mpc we need something brighter: supernovae.

Investigation 8: Baade's mistake (1952) A level

What to look for: Every distance comes out half as big (10^(−1.5/5) = 0.50) and H₀ twice as big. Before Baade found that there are two kinds of Cepheid, the universe seemed half its size and younger than the Earth. A systematic error climbs the whole ladder.

Investigation 9: Five billion Suns

What to look for: 15 magnitudes brighter = 10^(15/2.5) = a million times a Cepheid, about 5 billion times the Sun. That is why supernovae can be seen in galaxies hundreds of Mpc away.

Investigation 10: Slow fade, bright supernova A level

What to look for: Without it the peaks scatter by about 0.33 mag (15% in distance); using the fact that slow faders are brighter (the Phillips relation) cuts it to about 0.13 mag (6%). The H₀ uncertainty roughly halves.

Investigation 11: Measure H₀

What to look for: v = H₀d, about 70 km/s per Mpc in this model; 1/H₀ ≈ 14 billion years. Now any galaxy's distance follows from its red-shift alone: the top rung of the ladder.

Investigation 12: Why use far-away supernovae?

What to look for: Galaxies also move through space at a few hundred km/s. Nearby that is as big as the expansion speed, so v/d scatters wildly. Far away the expansion speed is thousands of km/s and the extra motion hardly matters.

Investigation 13: Errors climb the ladder A level

What to look for: With poor parallaxes the Cepheid zero point is uncertain by tenths of a magnitude, so H₀ is uncertain by 5–20% however many supernovae you use. With Gaia the limit is the number of galaxies with both Cepheids and a supernova.

Investigation 14: The Hubble tension A level

What to look for: 73/67.4 = 1.083, so 5 log₁₀(1.083) = 0.17 mag. A hidden 0.17 mag error in the Cepheid or supernova calibration (or new physics) is needed. JWST has checked the Cepheids and the tension has not gone away.

Batch experiment 1: Radar: does averaging pulses help?

Random timing errors average out: 16 pulses give a quarter of the error of one. A 1 ms error is 150 km in distance; real radar timing is much better, so the AU is now known to a few metres.
Theory (∝ 1 ÷ √N): 1: 432 · 4: 216 · 16: 108 · 64: 54
Result from a test run: 1: 392 ± 70.3 · 4: 235 ± 34.8 · 16: 73.6 ± 17.5 · 64: 58.6 ± 8.21 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Parallax: the error grows with distance

The parallax angle p = 1/d gets smaller while the measurement error stays the same, so the percentage error grows in proportion to distance. That is why parallax only reaches a few thousand parsecs, and why we need standard candles.
Theory (∝ d (0.8σ ÷ p)): 500: 0.998 · 1000: 2 · 2000: 3.99 · 4000: 7.98 · 8000: 16
Result from a test run: 500: 0.914 ± 0.116 · 1000: 2.2 ± 0.275 · 2000: 3.45 ± 0.396 · 4000: 8.88 ± 0.988 · 8000: 15.5 ± 1.94 (mean ± SE, 40 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Calibrating Cepheids: how many do we need?

Each Cepheid scatters about the law by about 0.15 mag, so the zero point improves as 1/√N. 0.02 mag is a 1% error in every distance above this rung.
Theory (∝ 1 ÷ √N): 5: 0.0607 · 10: 0.0429 · 20: 0.0303 · 40: 0.0214 · 80: 0.0152
Result from a test run: 5: 0.0611 ± 0.00916 · 10: 0.0502 ± 0.0104 · 20: 0.0329 ± 0.00499 · 40: 0.0221 ± 0.00367 · 80: 0.0181 ± 0.00239 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: A systematic error climbs the ladder

Every Cepheid distance, every supernova calibration and every supernova distance shifts by the same factor: 0.15 mag is 7% in H₀. No amount of extra data at higher rungs can fix it.
Theory (70 × 10^(error/5)): -0.3: 61 · -0.15: 65.3 · 0: 70 · 0.15: 75 · 0.3: 80.4
Result from a test run: -0.3: 61.6 ± 0.358 · -0.15: 65.8 ± 0.425 · 0: 69.4 ± 0.47 · 0.15: 75.1 ± 0.754 · 0.3: 81.5 ± 1.07 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Does the stretch correction help?

Without the correction peak brightnesses scatter by about 0.33 mag; with it about 0.13. Only ten galaxies calibrate the supernovae, so that scatter matters a lot.
Result from a test run: 0: 3.94 ± 0.471 · 1: 1.46 ± 0.193 (mean ± SE, 30 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: Why far-away supernovae?

Nearby, a galaxy's own motion (a few hundred km/s) is as big as its expansion speed. Far away it is a small fraction, so the fit is better, until the supernovae's own scatter (6% each) sets the limit.
Result from a test run: 20: 4.19 ± 0.682 · 50: 1.44 ± 0.296 · 100: 1.62 ± 0.203 · 200: 0.903 ± 0.143 · 400: 0.72 ± 0.136 · 800: 1.08 ± 0.183 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 7: How much does Gaia help?

Milky Way Cepheids are 300–3000 pc away (parallaxes 0.3–3 mas). Errors of 0.5–1 mas make their distances, and so everything above, very uncertain. Gaia (0.025 mas) makes this rung one of the most accurate.
Result from a test run: 0.025: 1.1 ± 0.181 · 0.1: 2.04 ± 0.256 · 0.25: 6.5 ± 0.923 · 0.5: 16 ± 1.78 · 1: 65.8 ± 6.14 (mean ± SE, 12 runs each)

Batch experiment 8: H₀ from 300 complete ladders

A bell-shaped spread around 70 with SD about 2 (3%). Most of the spread comes from the few galaxies that calibrate the supernovae.
Theory (true): 70
Result from a test run: mean 69.9, SD 1.73, SE 0.0998 from 300 runs
