Teacher notes

Investigation 1: A hot Jupiter

What to look for: The dip is about 1%: the planet blocks (Rp ÷ Rs)² of the star's light. √0.01 = 0.1, so the planet's radius is a tenth of the star's: about Jupiter's size. Hot Jupiters were the first planets found because their dips are deep and frequent.

Investigation 2: Fold the light curve

What to look for: Folding stacks every orbit on top of each other: at the right period, every transit lands in the same place and the dip stands out, even when each one is buried in noise. At the wrong period the dips smear out. The box search tries thousands of periods and picks the one where the dip is deepest and clearest.

Investigation 3: An Earth round a red dwarf

What to look for: The red dwarf is a third the Sun's size, so the same planet blocks about 9 times more light (depth ∝ 1 ÷ Rs²). A 12-day orbit gives many transits to stack. And because red dwarfs are dim, a planet that close can still be in the habitable zone.

Investigation 4: Tilted orbits

What to look for: A transit happens only if the planet passes between us and the star: for a hot Jupiter the orbit must be within a few degrees of edge-on. Most planetary systems are tilted the wrong way, so we miss their transits. The radial velocity wobble remains (reduced by sin i), which is why both methods are used.

Investigation 5: The habitable zone

What to look for: The habitable zone is where a planet gets about as much starlight as the Earth. A brighter star has a zone further out (distance ∝ √L); a dim red dwarf's zone is very close in. TRAPPIST-1 has several Earth-sized planets, some in its habitable zone, all closer than Mercury is to the Sun.

Investigation 6: Weighing a planet A level

What to look for: K ≈ 28.4 m/s × (Mp ÷ Jupiter) × (M★ ÷ Sun)^−2/3 × (P ÷ 1 year)^−1/3 (sin i = 1). About 140 m/s here: about 1 Jupiter mass. With the radius from the transit, mass and radius give the density: rocky, icy or gas.

Investigation 7: Kepler's third law A level

What to look for: For a 3.5-day orbit round a Sun-like star: P = 0.00958 years, so a = (1 × 0.00958²)^(1/3) = 0.045 AU, ten times closer than Mercury. Kepler's law comes from gravity providing the centripetal force: GM/a² = 4π²a/P².
