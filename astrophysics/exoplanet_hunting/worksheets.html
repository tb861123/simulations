Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · How small a planet can TESS find?

What this shows. Small planets make shallow dips (depth ∝ Rp²) that hide in the noise. Detection switches on over a narrow range of sizes, where SNR = depth/σ × √(points in transit) passes 7. The theory curve assumes the measured SNR scatters by ±1 around the expected value.

2 · Does SNR = depth/σ × √n hold?

What this shows. The measured SNR follows the theory line, rising as Rp² (depth). For very small planets the search still finds an SNR of about 4–5: the best-looking noise bump out of thousands of trial periods (false alarms). That is why the threshold is set as high as 7.

4 · Are the radius error bars honest? (68% within 1σ?)

What this shows. The measured radii scatter around the true radius with no bias, and roughly 2 in 3 of the runs' own error bars include the truth. The spread is about 20% wider than the simple theory, because the fit also has to find the transit's timing, length and shape from the same noisy data — so slightly fewer than 68% are inside. The star's radius is assumed known exactly here; in real life its error adds to the planet's.

5 · How precise is the period?

What this shows. Periods are measured to a few minutes, because a small error adds up over many orbits. But the spread is wider than the theory (Carter et al. 2008) and only about half the error bars include the truth: with only about 5 noisy transits, some runs lock onto a period slightly off, where the dips line up nearly as well. The quoted error only looks at the best peak, so it is too optimistic — a real lesson about error bars.

6 · How likely is a transit? (random orientations)

What this shows. With orbits pointing in random directions, only a fraction R★/a line up to transit: about 1 in 9 for a 3-day orbit but only 1 in 215 for an Earth-like orbit. So for every transiting Earth twin there are about 200 we can't see this way.

8 · Minimum mass from the wobble against inclination

What this shows. The wobble only shows the part of the star's motion along our line of sight, so radial velocities give M sin i, a minimum mass. For a face-on orbit (small i) the true mass could be much bigger. A transit tells us i ≈ 90°, so then we get the true mass.
