How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Thin lens: all the bending happens in one plane at the lens distance; the source is a flat picture far behind. Real lenses are thin compared with the distances, so this is excellent.
  • Lens equation: β = θ − α(θ). θ is where we see light arrive, β where it really comes from, α the bending angle (scaled by D_ls/D_s). Several lenses: their bending angles add up (one lens plane).
  • Distances: from redshift, in a flat universe with H₀ = 70 km/s/Mpc, 30% matter and 70% dark energy, as "angular-diameter distances" (how big things look). Note D_ls ≠ D_s − D_l.
  • Point mass: θ_E = √(4GM/c² · D_ls/(D_l D_s)), α = θ_E²/θ.
  • Galaxy (singular isothermal sphere): the mass is spread so that stars orbit at a constant speed σ√2. Mass M means the mass inside the radius R₂₀₀ where the average density is 200× the critical density; then σ² = GM/(2R₂₀₀) and θ_E = 4π σ²/c² · D_ls/D_s, α = θ_E (constant size).
  • Elliptical galaxy: the isothermal ellipsoid (Kormann et al. 1994; Keeton 2001 form). Its four-image "Einstein cross" appears when the source is inside the diamond-shaped caustic.
  • NFW halo: the density profile found in computer simulations of dark matter (Navarro, Frenk & White), with mass M₂₀₀ and concentration c, using the standard formulas for its bending angle and potential.
  • Hot gas: a cored isothermal sphere with core radius r_c (so it is not very concentrated): α = θ_E θ/(r_c + √(θ² + r_c²)).
  • Critical curves (red) are where the magnification 1/det(A) is infinite, with A the matrix ∂β/∂θ. Mapped onto the source plane they become caustics (cyan). A source crossing a caustic gains or loses a pair of images.
  • Images and magnifications of the source centre are found by searching a grid of triangles and refining with Newton's method; μ = 1/det(A). Negative μ means a mirror-image.
  • Time delays: light along different paths takes different times: Δt = (1+z_l)/c · D_l D_s/D_ls · Δ[½|θ−β|² − ψ(θ)], with ψ the lensing potential.
  • Microlensing: lens star plus planet. The binary-lens equation is turned into a 5th-order polynomial (Witt & Mao 1995) and solved for the 3 or 5 images; the source star's size is included by averaging over 25 points across its disc. Single-lens magnification A(u) = (u²+2)/(u√(u²+4)). Noise: random scatter on each measurement.
  • Eddington 1919: bending α = 4GM/(c²b) = 1.75″ × (R☉/b) for light passing a distance b from the Sun's centre. Newton's physics (light as particles) gives half as much.
  • Picture quality (Low/Medium/High/Ultra) only changes how many rays are traced for the picture (and how fine the grid for curves and images is), not the physics. Batch runs always use fixed resolutions.
  • Left out: light from the lens galaxy itself; dust; blurring by the atmosphere or telescope; lenses at several different distances; the source's own motion; microlensing by stars inside lens galaxies; "strong" bending near black holes (we assume small angles).

Set up your own

Batch runs skip the drawing. Microlensing runs treat the source star as a point, sample the light curve 1600 times, and only work out the planet's effect near the star and the planet's caustics. Ring measurements always use a fixed 100 × 100 pixel image, and image searches a fixed 120 × 120 grid, whatever the picture-quality setting, so batch results are reproducible.
