Teacher notes

Investigation 1: How big is the shadow?

What to look for: The shadow is 2.6 rs in radius: over two and a half times bigger than the hole. Any light aimed closer than that is bent round and captured, so the hole casts a shadow much larger than itself. This is the shadow the Event Horizon Telescope photographed.

Investigation 2: Seeing behind the hole

What to look for: The arch is the far side of the disc: light leaving its top face is bent over the hole towards you. The band underneath is the far side's underneath, bent round below. Drag upwards to look from above and watch the arch flatten into an ordinary ring.

Investigation 3: Why is one side brighter?

What to look for: Gas rushing towards you has its light squeezed to bluer, brighter colours and beamed forwards (brightness goes up as the 4th power of the shift). The receding side is dimmed and reddened. Look from directly above (tilt 90°): the asymmetry vanishes, because nothing is moving towards you.

Investigation 4: From black hole to Einstein ring

What to look for: The galaxy is smeared into a ring: light passing on every side of the hole is bent towards you. The dashed ring is the simple weak-bending formula, radius √(2 rs × distance). It works well far out, where bending is small. That formula is all the 'Lensed sky' view uses.

Investigation 5: Rings, arcs and a cross

What to look for: A full ring needs perfect alignment and a round lens. Just off-centre it breaks into two arcs; further out, one bright image and one faint one on the opposite side. A squashed lens cannot make a ring: it makes four images instead (an Einstein cross).

Investigation 6: Weigh the dark matter

What to look for: The arcs shrink to almost nothing. The visible matter is only about 15% of what is needed to bend light this much, so lensing is one of the clearest pieces of evidence for dark matter. The radius of the arcs measures the total mass inside them.

Investigation 7: Catch a planet

What to look for: The background map shows how bright the far star would look at each position. The planet matters only where the map has sharp orange patches (caustics), and they are biggest when the planet is about one Einstein radius from its star. Elsewhere the light curve is indistinguishable from a star alone.

Investigation 8: Einstein or Newton?

What to look for: The shifts are tiny compared with the blur of a photographic plate, so single stars prove little. The fitted value from all the stars together, with its uncertainty, is what counts: it should sit several uncertainties away from Newton. Raise the plate error to 0.5″ and the answer is no longer convincing.

Batch experiment 1: How much is light bent?

No randomness here: every photon aimed the same way does the same thing. The dashed curve is Einstein's weak-bending formula (the one that gives 1.75″ at the Sun). Far out the two agree. Close in, the true bending is much bigger, and at 2.6 rs the photon circles the hole and never escapes.

Batch experiment 2: Measure the shadow

The hole swallows every photon aimed within 2.598 rs, so its 'target area' is far bigger than the horizon. With only 400 photons the count is a matter of chance, so each run gives a slightly different radius; the mean of many runs homes in on √27/2.
Theory (√27/2): 2.6
Result from a test run: mean 2.6, SD 0.138, SE 0.00976 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 3: Einstein or Newton? (1919)

Newton's prediction is 0.87″. Almost no expedition lands that low, so the result is convincing even though each star alone is hopelessly noisy. The real 1919 results were 1.98 ± 0.16″ (Sobral) and 1.61 ± 0.40″ (Príncipe).
Theory (Einstein): 1.75
Result from a test run: mean 1.76, SD 0.215, SE 0.0124 from 300 runs

Batch experiment 4: Weighing with light

A point lens makes two images, at distances θ₊ and θ₋ on either side. Wherever the source is, θ₊ × θ₋ = θ_E², so two measured positions give the ring radius, and the ring radius gives the mass. Four times the mass gives twice the ring.

Batch experiment 6: Where must a planet be?

Each run is a yes or a no, so the dots sit at 0 and 1; the mean is the fraction found. It peaks near 1 Einstein radius (a few AU for a typical star): the 'lensing zone'. Microlensing therefore finds cool planets in wide orbits that other methods miss.
