Gravitational Lensing · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Does the peak brightness follow A(u)?

Each event has a random closest approach u₀ between 0.05 and 1 Einstein radii. Close passes are rare but give huge peaks, so the histogram has a long tail; the blue curve is what A(u) = (u²+2)/(u√(u²+4)) predicts for a uniform spread of u₀. The measured peaks sit very slightly high, because 1% noise on the brightest point can only push the maximum up, and slightly low for the tiniest u₀ because the light curve is sampled only every 1.2 hours.

2 · How often would we spot a Jupiter?

A twin of Jupiter orbits a twin of the Sun (mass ratio q ≈ 0.001) at a random distance in the 'lensing zone' (0.6–1.6 Einstein radii) and at a random angle, and the source passes at random u₀ < 1. The planet counts as detected if it changes the brightness by more than 5% (five times the 1% noise) for three measurements in a row. Gould & Loeb (1992) estimated about 17% for this case. Our simple model, with perfectly regular measurements every 1.2 hours and no gaps for daylight or bad weather, finds a little more. Real surveys miss events in daytime, so they find fewer. The mean of the 0/1 results is the detection probability; its standard error shows how much it would change in another batch.

3 · Is the Einstein radius proportional to √M?

The ring radius is measured from a noisy simulated image at each mass. On log–log axes the points fall on a straight line of gradient 0.5: θ_E ∝ M^0.5. So a lens 100× heavier makes a ring only 10× bigger. The measured radii sit about 1% above the theory line, because an extended source spreads the ring slightly outwards.

4 · Can we weigh a galaxy from its ring?

We measure the ring radius on a noisy image and work out the mass M = θ_E² c² D_l D_s/(4G D_ls). Because M depends on θ_E², a 1% error in the radius becomes a 2% error in the mass. Notice the mean comes out about 4% too high, many standard errors away from 1. That is a systematic error (bias), not bad luck: the source has a size, so its ring is a little wider on the outside, and the brightness-weighted radius is about 2% too big. More runs will not remove a bias; a better method (or a smaller source) would. The mass inside the ring is what lensing actually measures, whether the matter shines or not: this is how dark matter in galaxies is weighed.

5 · How does magnification depend on alignment?

The images of a point source are found by the image-search code and their magnifications added up. They agree with A(u) = (u²+2)/(u√(u²+4)) to better than 0.1%: when the source is lined up to within a tenth of an Einstein radius the total light is 10× brighter. There is no randomness here, so there are no error bars.

6 · How long are the time delays?

The extra travel time between the two images grows in proportion to the lens mass (log–log gradient 1). A galaxy-sized lens gives delays of days to weeks; this is how astronomers measure the expansion rate of the Universe (H₀) from flickering lensed quasars.
