How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Hubble's law: v = H0 d. Velocity v in km/s, distance d in Mpc, H0 in km/s per Mpc. 1 Mpc = 3.09 × 1019 km = 3.26 million light years.
  • Redshift: z = (λobserved − λlab) / λlab. Every line in the spectrum moves by the same factor (1 + z). For small z, v = cz with c = 299,792 km/s. For z above about 0.1 this is only approximate, and for z > 1 it would give speeds faster than light: the redshift is really caused by space stretching while the light travels.
  • Spectral lines used: calcium K and H (393.4, 396.8 nm), hydrogen Hδ, Hγ, Hβ, Hα (410.2, 434.0, 486.1, 656.3 nm), magnesium (517.5 nm) and sodium (589.3 nm).
  • Distances by standard candles: distance modulus m − M = 5 log10(d / 10 pc). Cepheids: M = −2.43 (log10P − 1) − 4.05, P the pulsation period in days (Benedict et al. 2007), usable to about 40 Mpc with the Hubble Space Telescope. Type Ia supernovae: M = −19.3 after standardising, usable to thousands of Mpc. Each galaxy's brightness has a random scatter (the slider), so the distance has a random error of about 46% × the scatter in magnitudes.
  • Calibration error: every distance is multiplied by the same factor (1 + error). This does not average away. Hubble's 1929 distances were about 7 times too small (he mixed up two types of Cepheid and mistook glowing gas clouds for bright stars), so he found H0 ≈ 500.
  • Peculiar velocities: a random speed (normal distribution, chosen SD) along the line of sight, added to the expansion. Redshift measurements also have a 30 km/s random error.
  • Galaxies are placed at random, evenly through a sphere out to the furthest distance (so there are more far ones than near ones), but none closer than 1 Mpc.
  • Fitting: a straight line through the origin, v = H d, by weighted least squares. Each galaxy's weight is 1/(30² + 250² + H²σd²): the fitter allows 250 km/s for peculiar velocities. The quoted uncertainty is scaled by √(χ²/degrees of freedom), so it grows if the points scatter more than expected. Random errors in the distances pull the fitted slope down a little ("regression dilution": about 0.5% for 0.15 mag scatter, 5% for 0.4 mag); the batch theory includes this.
  • Age: the Hubble time 1/H0 = 977.8/H0 billion years. If the expansion had always been at the same rate, this would be the age. With ΛCDM (Ωm = 0.3, ΩΛ = 0.7) the age is 0.964/H0 (13.5 billion years for H0 = 70); with matter only it is (2/3)/H0.
  • Far galaxies (ΛCDM / matter only): the comoving distance is D = (c/H0) ∫0z dz'/E(z'), with E(z) = √(Ωm(1+z)³ + ΩΛ). Standard candles give the luminosity distance dL = (1 + z) D. Plotting cz against dL bends away from a straight line: far supernovae look fainter (further away) than a straight line predicts. In 1998 this showed the expansion is speeding up.
  • The animation is a 2D slice with exponential expansion at a constant rate. Galaxies keep their size: gravity holds them together, only the space between them stretches.
  • Redshift calculator (right panel): one spectral line from a galaxy moving at β = v/c, using the relativistic Doppler formula λobs = λ√((1 + β)/(1 − β)). It compares the exact speed with the small-speed formula Δλ/λ = v/c and estimates the distance with Hubble's law (H0 = 70). The real cluster data are ten galaxy clusters (distance, velocity) fitted with a line through the origin.
  • Not modelled: dust dimming, K-corrections, the Malmquist bias (picking only bright objects far away), gravitational lensing, the "Hubble tension" (local measurements give about 73, the cosmic microwave background about 67), relativistic Doppler formula for peculiar velocities.

📐 More about redshift (A level)
z = Δλ/λ = (λ_obs − λ_rest)/λ_rest
Δλ/λ ≈ v/c (only when v ≪ c)
λ_obs = λ_rest √((1 + β)/(1 − β)), β = v/c (relativistic Doppler)
v = H0 d, H0 ≈ 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹

Light and sound compared: for sound the speed of the source and of the listener through the air both matter (see the Waves simulation). Light needs no medium, so only the relative speed counts. At low speed both give Δf/f ≈ Δλ/λ ≈ v/c (or v/v_sound).

Hubble's law from expansion: if space stretches uniformly, every pair of galaxies moves apart at a rate proportional to their separation: ḋ = H0 d. There is no centre: every galaxy sees the others receding at v = H0 d.

The cosmic microwave background (CMB): light from 380,000 years after the Big Bang has been redshifted by z ≈ 1100, from visible light to microwaves. Its spectrum today is a perfect black body at 2.725 K — the strongest evidence for the Hot Big Bang.

Olbers' paradox: in an infinite, unchanging, eternal universe every line of sight would end on a star and the night sky would be as bright as the Sun. The dark sky shows the universe has a finite age, and light from very distant galaxies is redshifted out of the visible band.

Lookback: light from a galaxy with redshift z set off when the universe was 1/(1 + z) of its present size. A galaxy at z = 1 is seen as it was when the universe was half its present size.
