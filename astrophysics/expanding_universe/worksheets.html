The Expanding Universe · Teacher notesexpanding-universe.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Everything moves away

What to look for: From any galaxy, every other galaxy moves away, and the further one moves faster. There is no centre: space itself is stretching everywhere.

Investigation 2: Red-shift

What to look for: Every line moves to a longer (redder) wavelength by the same factor (1 + z): Δλ/λ is the same for each line. That is the signature of motion away from us (and of stretching space).

Investigation 3: Measure Hubble's law

What to look for: Yes: v = H₀d, a straight line through the origin; the gradient is H₀ (about 70 km/s per Mpc here). Each galaxy's distance comes from a standard candle and its speed from its red-shift.

Investigation 4: Age of the universe

What to look for: 1/H₀ = 1 ÷ (70 km/s per Mpc) = 3.09 × 10¹⁹ ÷ 70 s = 4.4 × 10¹⁷ s ≈ 14 billion years. Real age (with the expansion changing over time): 13.8 billion years.

Investigation 5: Hubble in 1929

What to look for: Nearby, galaxies' own motions (a few hundred km/s) are as big as the expansion speed, and Hubble's distances were too small: he got about 500 km/s per Mpc. With so few, scattered points the uncertainty is large.

Investigation 6: Wrong yardstick

What to look for: No: a systematic error moves every point the same way, so the fit converges on the wrong answer (about 58 instead of 70), with small error bars around the wrong value. More data only beats random errors.

Investigation 7: The accelerating universe A level

What to look for: At large distances the points curve away from the line: distant supernovae are fainter (further) than a constant expansion predicts, because the expansion is speeding up (dark energy). This won the 2011 Nobel Prize.

Investigation 8: Redshift calculator A level

What to look for: Δλ = λv/c = 656.3 × 0.05 = 32.8 nm; z = 0.05. At high speeds use 1 + z = √((1 + β)/(1 − β)): at 0.5c, z = 0.73 (not 0.5).

Batch experiment 1: Do more galaxies shrink the random error?

Random errors average out: four times as many galaxies halve the error.
Theory (∝ 1 ÷ √N): 10: 1.5 · 20: 1.06 · 40: 0.75 · 80: 0.53 · 160: 0.375
Result from a test run: 10: 0.907 ± 0.174 · 20: 0.716 ± 0.102 · 40: 0.789 ± 0.142 · 80: 0.51 ± 0.0618 · 160: 0.299 ± 0.0589 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Can more galaxies fix a calibration error?

No: the answer converges on 58, not 70. A systematic error must be found and corrected, not averaged away.
Theory (70 ÷ 1.2): 10: 58.3 · 20: 58.3 · 40: 58.3 · 80: 58.3 · 160: 58.3
Result from a test run: 10: 58.1 ± 0.212 · 20: 58.2 ± 0.271 · 40: 58.4 ± 0.228 · 80: 58.4 ± 0.161 · 160: 58.5 ± 0.0718 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Peculiar velocities nearby

Nearby, a galaxy's own motion is as big as its expansion speed, so H₀ is very uncertain. Far away the expansion dominates.
Result from a test run: 10: 4.05 ± 0.729 · 20: 2.91 ± 0.391 · 50: 1.21 ± 0.178 · 100: 0.71 ± 0.164 · 200: 0.687 ± 0.138 · 400: 0.698 ± 0.0906 (mean ± SE, 15 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: H₀ from 500 surveys

A bell-shaped spread around the true value: each survey's quoted ±1σ error should contain 70 about two times in three.
Theory (true): 70
Result from a test run: mean 70.1, SD 0.818, SE 0.0366 from 500 runs

Batch experiment 5: Age of the universe

About 14 billion years. The real age (13.8 billion years) is close because the slowing and speeding up of the expansion almost cancel.
Theory (977.8 ÷ 70): 14
Result from a test run: mean 14, SD 0.163, SE 0.00728 from 500 runs
