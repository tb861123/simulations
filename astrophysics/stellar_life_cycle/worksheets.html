Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Do heavy stars die young?

Do heavy stars die young? A star 10 times heavier has about 10 times more fuel but shines about 3,000 times brighter (L ∝ M³·⁵), so it runs out about 300 times sooner: t ∝ M/L ∝ M⁻²·⁵. On log–log axes that is a straight line with gradient −2.5. The model follows it closely from 0.8 to about 25 M☉; below 0.8 M☉ red dwarfs use more of their fuel, and the heaviest stars level off at a few million years because L ∝ M at the top end. The points don't scatter because the model is deterministic.

2 · Is L ∝ M³·⁵?

Is L ∝ M³·⁵? The mass–luminosity relation. The model uses a piecewise version, L ∝ M⁴ between 0.43 and 2 M☉ and 1.4 M³·⁵ above, so the fitted gradient comes out a little above 3.5 over this range. Real stars scatter around such a line because of composition, age and rotation.

3 · What is left behind?

What is left behind? Below about 7.5 M☉ the remnant is a white dwarf whose mass follows the measured initial–final mass relation (0.109 M + 0.394): even an 7 M☉ star leaves only about 1.2 M☉, because winds and the planetary nebula carry the rest away. Above that the core collapses: neutron stars of about 1.3–1.6 M☉, then black holes. Real boundaries are blurry because they depend on rotation, binaries and winds.

4 · How big do stars get?

How big do stars get? Red dwarfs never become giants. Sun-like stars swell to a few hundred solar radii on the asymptotic giant branch (about the size of Earth's orbit). Massive stars become supergiants of up to ~1,500 R☉, but the very heaviest stay hot (too luminous to be red supergiants) and so stay smaller. There is no single formula here: the radius is set by L = 4πR²σT⁴ at each stage.

5 · A cluster of 5,000 stars: how do they end?

A cluster of 5,000 stars: how do they end? Masses are drawn at random from the Salpeter IMF, so most stars are small: over 99% will end as white dwarfs (most haven't got there yet), and only about 1 star in 300 explodes as a supernova. Change the masses to 1–100 M☉ to see the rarer endings more clearly. The theory bars integrate the IMF between the masses where this model's fates change. The bar heights scatter around theory by about ±√(np(1−p))/n — under a tenth of a per cent for neutron stars in 5,000 stars — so rare outcomes like black holes vary most in relative terms.

6 · After 1 billion years, how many still shine?

After 1 billion years, how many still shine? A star is still shining if its life is longer than the cluster's age. Theory uses t = 10 Gyr × M⁻²·⁵ to find the turn-off mass (about 2.5 M☉ at 1 billion years) and integrates the IMF below it. Because so many stars are small, almost all are still shining. The model's stars live a little longer than the main-sequence formula (giant stages add ~10–20%), so its turn-off mass is slightly higher. Try other ages.
