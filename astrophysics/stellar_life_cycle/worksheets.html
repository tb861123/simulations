1 · Do heavy stars die young?

Do heavy stars die young? A star 10 times heavier has about 10 times more fuel but shines about 3,000 times brighter (L ∝ M³·⁵), so it runs out about 300 times sooner: t ∝ M/L ∝ M⁻²·⁵. On log–log axes that is a straight line with gradient −2.5. The model follows it closely from 0.8 to about 25 M☉; below 0.8 M☉ red dwarfs use more of their fuel, and the heaviest stars level off at a few million years because L ∝ M at the top end. The points don't scatter because the model is deterministic.

3 · What is left behind?

What is left behind? Below about 7.5 M☉ the remnant is a white dwarf whose mass follows the measured initial–final mass relation (0.109 M + 0.394): even an 7 M☉ star leaves only about 1.2 M☉, because winds and the planetary nebula carry the rest away. Above that the core collapses: neutron stars of about 1.3–1.6 M☉, then black holes. Real boundaries are blurry because they depend on rotation, binaries and winds.

4 · How big do stars get?

How big do stars get? Red dwarfs never become giants. Sun-like stars swell to a few hundred solar radii on the asymptotic giant branch (about the size of Earth's orbit). Massive stars become supergiants of up to ~1,500 R☉, but the very heaviest stay hot (too luminous to be red supergiants) and so stay smaller. There is no single formula here: the radius is set by L = 4πR²σT⁴ at each stage.

5 · A cluster of 5,000 stars: how do they end?

A cluster of 5,000 stars: how do they end? Masses are drawn at random from the Salpeter IMF, so most stars are small: over 99% will end as white dwarfs (most haven't got there yet), and only about 1 star in 300 explodes as a supernova. Change the masses to 1–100 M☉ to see the rarer endings more clearly. The theory bars integrate the IMF between the masses where this model's fates change. The bar heights scatter around theory by about ±√(np(1−p))/n — under a tenth of a per cent for neutron stars in 5,000 stars — so rare outcomes like black holes vary most in relative terms.

6 · After 1 billion years, how many still shine?

After 1 billion years, how many still shine? A star is still shining if its life is longer than the cluster's age. Theory uses t = 10 Gyr × M⁻²·⁵ to find the turn-off mass (about 2.5 M☉ at 1 billion years) and integrates the IMF below it. Because so many stars are small, almost all are still shining. The model's stars live a little longer than the main-sequence formula (giant stages add ~10–20%), so its turn-off mass is slightly higher. Try other ages.

7 · Does Wien's law hold for real-looking stars?

Does Wien's law hold for real-looking stars? Each run picks a star from the IMF (0.5–50 M☉) and a random moment of its life (red dwarfs, the Sun, giants, supergiants, white dwarfs: 1,000–60,000 K), then finds the brightest channel of its Planck spectrum with a 400-channel spectrometer. λmax × T comes out at Wien's constant, b = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m K, for every star, hot or cool. The runs scatter only because the brightest channel can sit up to half a channel either side of the true peak: the measured SD matches the SD predicted from the channel width, and the mean is within a few standard errors of b. Try 100 channels (coarse) or 1,600 (fine) to see the scatter grow or shrink.

8 · Is L = 4πR²σT⁴?

Is L = 4πR²σT⁴? For each random star the spectrometer adds up the area under its Planck curve (π ∫B dλ = power per m² of surface) and multiplies by the surface area 4πR². Dividing by the model's luminosity gives 1 for every star: L = 4πR²σT⁴ holds whatever the radius and temperature, from a red dwarf to a supergiant 1,000 times the Sun's size. (The model sets each star's temperature from L and R using this law, so this checks that the area under Planck's curve really is σT⁴.) The mean is about 1 part in a million below 1: a small SYSTEMATIC error from adding up the area in steps and missing the far ends of the spectrum (below 10 nm and above 1 mm). The runs hardly scatter at all (SD about 10⁻⁸), so this tiny bias shows up as thousands of standard errors: a good example of why the standard error only measures random scatter, not systematic error.

9 · Wien's law: does λmax ∝ 1/T?

Wien's law: does λmax ∝ 1/T? A pure black body is heated from 1,000 K (peak in the infrared, about 2.9 µm) to 40,000 K (peak in the ultraviolet, about 72 nm). On log–log axes λmax = b/T is a straight line of gradient −1, and the fitted gradient comes out at −1.00. This uses a coarse 100-channel spectrometer (each channel 12% wide), so the 5 repeats at each temperature scatter by a few per cent: the error bars show the instrument's resolution, not a failure of Wien's law.

10 · Stefan–Boltzmann: is P ∝ T⁴?

Stefan–Boltzmann: is P ∝ T⁴? The power per square metre is the area under the Planck curve (times π). Raising the temperature 40 times multiplies it by 40⁴ ≈ 2.6 million, so on log–log axes the points lie on a line of gradient 4, and they sit on σT⁴ with σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴ (measured ÷ theory ≈ 1.000). This is why a star only a little hotter is much more luminous. Repeats agree to about 1 part in 10⁵ because adding up the area hardly depends on where the channels sit.
