How this model works (and what it leaves out)
  • This is a teaching model, not a full stellar-structure code. Each stage of life is described by a few "keyframes" of luminosity, temperature, radius, mass and core conditions, worked out from standard scaling laws, and the model joins them smoothly.
  • Main sequence: luminosity ∝ M2.3–M4 depending on mass, radius ∝ M0.57–M0.8, lifetime ≈ 10 billion years × M−2.5 (levelling off at about 3 million years for the biggest stars). Surface temperature always comes from L = 4πR²σT⁴.
  • Metallicity: metal-poor stars are made slightly hotter and brighter and live slightly shorter; their winds are much weaker. Metal-poor low-mass stars burn helium on a blue horizontal branch; metal-poor massive stars stay blue supergiants.
  • Winds: hot massive stars lose mass ∝ L1.2; cool giants follow a Reimers-type law ∝ L·R/M. If the hydrogen envelope is blown away, the star becomes a Wolf–Rayet star. Low- and intermediate-mass stars lose their envelopes as a planetary nebula.
  • White dwarf masses follow the measured initial–final mass relation (0.109 M + 0.394). White dwarfs cool following Mestel's law (L ∝ t−1.4).
  • Massive stars: helium-core mass ≈ 0.11 M1.33 (reduced by winds). The fate depends on it: under 8 M☉ → neutron star; 8–15 M☉ → black hole after a weak supernova; over 15 M☉ → direct collapse to a black hole; 64–133 M☉ in a metal-poor star → pair-instability supernova with no remnant. Real boundaries are uncertain and "patchy".
  • Core temperatures, densities and durations of carbon, neon, oxygen and silicon burning are scaled from published models of 15 and 25 M☉ stars (Woosley, Heger & Weaver 2002).
  • The size view has two modes. Fixed scale uses one logarithmic scale for the whole life (each ring is many times bigger than the one inside), so growing and shrinking are easy to see, but sizes are not in true proportion. True scale shows real proportions and zooms by ×10 only when it has to. The dashed gold circle is the star's size at the start of the current stage.
  • The cutaway is drawn by mass (how much of the star is in each layer), not to scale in size: in a giant the core holds a lot of the mass but is thousands of times smaller than the star. The "size" view shows the true size.
  • Brown dwarfs (under 0.08 M☉) never fuse ordinary hydrogen; above 0.013 M☉ they briefly fuse deuterium.
  • Rotation, magnetic fields, and most binary-star effects are ignored (except the optional companion that can turn a white dwarf into a Type Ia supernova).
  • Batch run: builds the life of many stars without drawing. "Repeat" picks masses at random from the Salpeter initial mass function (dN/dM ∝ M−2.35) to make a star cluster; "Sweep" steps one starting condition. The model has no randomness, so a sweep gives one exact answer per step unless you switch on the optional random scatter in metallicity and wind. The black-body experiments "observe" each star with a model spectrometer (100, 400 or 1,600 channels spread evenly in log λ from 10 nm to 1 mm, placed at a random offset each run); the channel width is why λmax × T scatters around Wien's constant.
  • Black-body view: the star is treated as a perfect black body at its surface temperature, so its spectrum is Planck's curve. Real stars are close but not perfect: their atmospheres absorb dark lines, they are darker at the edge (limb darkening) and hot stars give out less ultraviolet than a black body. The Sun's real spectrum peaks near 500 nm, as Wien's law says.
  • Wien's law uses b = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m K and the spectrum is drawn per unit wavelength. (Per unit frequency the peak is in a different place, so don't mix the two.) The photons in the glowing-ball animation are picked at random from Planck's law by number (B × λ) and their rate is ∝ T⁴ or ∝ L, limited to 1–60 per second on screen.
  • H–R diagram: the catalogue stars use published values (temperature, luminosity, radius). The main-sequence band is drawn around this model's own main sequence; the other regions are rough boxes. Exam questions use the textbook relations (τ = 10¹⁰ years × M⁻²·⁵) which this model follows only between about 0.8 and 25 M☉.
  • Quality (Low / Medium / High / Ultra) only changes how many points the charts and spectra use and how many particles the animations draw. The canvases are drawn at the screen's full pixel density, so they stay sharp on Surface screens at 200%.
  • Very long stages (red dwarfs, white-dwarf and brown-dwarf cooling) last longer than the age of the Universe (13.8 billion years): what you see there is a prediction no telescope can check yet.

📐 Physics notes

Spec references: WJEC Eduqas A level Physics, Component 3 Topic 5 (Stars and cosmology) and Topic 4 (Thermal physics and quantum ideas).

Key equations

L = 4πR²σT⁴, σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴
λmax = b / T, b = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m K
P = σT⁴ (power per m² of surface)
B(λ,T) = (2hc²/λ⁵) × 1/(ehc/λkT − 1)
L ∝ M³·⁵, τ ∝ M⁻²·⁵ τ☉

The H–R diagram plots luminosity against surface temperature (hot on the left). Most stars lie on the main sequence, a diagonal band from hot and bright (top left) to cool and dim (bottom right). Stars spend most of their lives there, fusing hydrogen in their cores.

Stefan–Boltzmann law: L = 4πR²σT⁴ links luminosity, radius and temperature. A small hot star can be as luminous as a large cool giant. Doubling T multiplies the power by 16.

Wien's displacement law: λmax = b/T. The peak moves to shorter (bluer) wavelengths as the temperature rises: the Sun peaks near 500 nm (visible green), a 2,700 K bulb filament in the infrared. This sets a star's colour, from blue O-type stars (over 30,000 K) to red M-type stars (under 3,700 K).

Black bodies: a black body absorbs all radiation that falls on it and re-emits it with a spectrum that depends only on its temperature. Planck's law (1900) was the first to describe the whole spectrum; it needed energy to be quantised (E = nhf), the birth of quantum physics.

Stellar evolution: lifetime τ ∝ M⁻²·⁵. Massive stars burn their fuel faster (L ∝ M³·⁵) and live far shorter lives. After the main sequence, stars swell into red giants, then end as white dwarfs, neutron stars or black holes, depending on their mass.

Advanced

Mass–luminosity relation: L ∝ M³·⁵ because more massive stars need a higher core pressure and temperature to stay in hydrostatic equilibrium. A 10 M☉ star is about 3,000 times more luminous than the Sun.

Lifetime scaling: the fuel supply ∝ M and L ∝ M³·⁵, so τ ∝ M/L ∝ M⁻²·⁵. A 25 M☉ star lives only about 3 million years; a 0.1 M☉ red dwarf could shine for trillions of years.

H–R regions: the giant branch (cool, luminous) forms when stars run out of core hydrogen and their envelopes expand. Supergiants are the most massive evolved stars. White dwarfs (lower left) are Earth-sized, hot remnants cooling over billions of years.

Spectral classes O B A F G K M ("Oh Be A Fine Girl/Guy, Kiss Me") follow surface temperature and show as colour on the H–R diagram.

Ultraviolet catastrophe: the classical Rayleigh–Jeans law, B = 2ckT/λ⁴, goes to infinity as λ → 0, predicting infinite total power. Planck's quantisation fixes this: short-wavelength (high-frequency) modes need large quanta hf, which are rarely excited at a finite temperature, so the spectrum falls exponentially at short wavelengths.

Stefan–Boltzmann on a log–log graph: log P = log σ + 4 log T, a straight line of gradient exactly 4.

Stellar spectra: real stars are close to black bodies. The Sun (G-type, 5,772 K) has a fairly flat visible spectrum and looks white-yellow.

Cosmic microwave background: the most perfect black-body spectrum ever measured, the afterglow of the Big Bang at T ≈ 2.725 K, peaking in the microwave region (about 1.06 mm in this per-wavelength form).
