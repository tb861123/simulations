Planet Formation Simulator · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · How many planets form?

How many planets form? Ten discs with identical settings but different random starting orbits. The count varies from disc to disc: planet formation is chaotic, so tiny differences early on decide which bodies collide. A planet here must have cleared its orbit (the IAU's rule): bodies closer than 10 mutual Hill radii to a heavier one are not counted, because such crowded orbits are unstable and would collide over the next 10–100 million years — far more orbits than the model can run. Switch 'Record' to the raw count to see every body over 0.05 M⊕ that is left (typically 15–18). Even after clearing, the model usually makes a few more planets than our 8: real giant impacts went on for about 100 million years and spread our rocky planets 25–60 Hill radii apart, and each batch planetesimal starts as big as Mars, so leftovers are already 'planets'.

2 · Bigger disc, bigger planets?

Bigger disc, bigger planets? More solid material means cores grow faster and bigger, so they are more likely to reach the critical mass before the gas disappears and then run away into gas giants. Expect a jump rather than a smooth line: a core that reaches the critical mass in time can grow from 10 to hundreds of Earth masses. Only 2 runs per step, so the error bars are rough.

3 · How much gas makes a gas giant?

How much gas makes a gas giant? With no gas there can be no gas giants at all, however big the cores. With more gas, cores that pass the critical mass can gather more before the disc clears. In test runs: no giants with no gas, about 1–2 with 2 Jupiter masses and about 3 with 6. More gas lets a core that passes the critical mass grow further before the disc clears, and lets later, slower cores catch up. With only 2 runs per step the error bars are large. Note the reduced settings: the gas disc lasts 1 million years here instead of 3, so cores have less time. Try sweeping the critical core mass instead. (Real surveys find giant planets are more common around stars with more heavy elements, i.e. more solids to build cores.)

4 · Does the frost line matter?

Does the frost line matter? A brighter star pushes the frost line outwards (2.7 × √L AU, with L ≈ M⁴): from about 1.3 AU for a 0.7 M☉ star to about 4.6 AU for 1.3 M☉. More of the disc is then warm and rocky, so more rocky planets form inside it (in test runs about 4, 7.5 and 9 for 0.7, 1.0 and 1.3 M☉), while ice-rich giant cores form further out where orbits are slower. A heavier star also changes the orbit speeds, so this is not a perfectly fair test of the frost line alone.

5 · Standing in for the missing time

Standing in for the missing time After the gas has gone the model can only run a few thousand orbits, so it enlarges the collision radius to stand in for the hundred million years of slow orbital chaos it can't run. This sweep shows how much the answer depends on that shortcut: the bigger the boost, the more giant impacts and the fewer, bigger planets are left (raw count shown). A good model makes its shortcuts visible like this, so you can judge how far to trust it.
