Gravity-Assist Sandbox · Teacher notesgravity-slingshots.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Steal some speed

What to look for: Passing behind the planet, the probe is dragged along with it and leaves faster (about 11 km/s faster here). Passing in front (not too close), the planet's pull acts against the probe's motion along the planet's path and it leaves slower. Spacecraft use the second trick to fall in towards the Sun (MESSENGER, Parker Solar Probe).

Investigation 2: It all depends on your point of view

What to look for: No. In the planet's frame the path is a symmetrical hyperbola and the probe leaves at exactly the speed it arrived with: energy is conserved. Only the direction has turned. Back in the Sun's frame the planet's own velocity is added on, and a turned velocity plus the planet's velocity can be longer. The vector diagram (bottom right) shows this: the probe's velocity is the grey arrow plus a cyan arrow that only swings round the circle.

Investigation 3: Is closer always better?

What to look for: Not the closest. The best flyby turns the relative velocity until it points along the planet's own motion. Bend it further and you start turning past that direction and gain less. With these speeds the best is about 2–4 planet radii; skim the cloud tops and you over-turn (or crash!). The Batch run "Speed gain vs flyby distance" maps the whole curve.

Investigation 4: Find the launch window

What to look for: The probe takes about 2.7 years to coast out to Jupiter's orbit, and Jupiter moves about a quarter of the way round the Sun in that time. You must launch when Jupiter is roughly 97° ahead of Earth. That happens once every 13 months (Jupiter's synodic period): these are launch windows.

Investigation 5: Escape with only 10 km/s

What to look for: Get to Jupiter first (launch window!), then pass behind Jupiter, closer than about 0.05 AU. Use the fine-tune buttons (v±, θ±) and watch the predicted closest approach. Jupiter can add 5–10 km/s, more than enough to escape. Pioneer 10, Voyager 1 and 2 and New Horizons all left the Solar System like this.

Investigation 6: Dive towards the Sun

What to look for: Launch backwards (about 180°) so you fall in towards Venus, then pass in front of Venus (on the side it is moving towards). That flyby takes speed away, and your orbit dips closer to the Sun. MESSENGER (to Mercury) and the Parker Solar Probe used repeated Venus flybys exactly like this.

Investigation 7: The Grand Tour

What to look for: Each flyby must aim the probe at the next planet, so tiny errors grow: this is why real missions make small course corrections. Get Jupiter right first (watch where the dashed line goes after it), then use the in-flight burns (0.05 km/s) to steer towards the next planet. Stuck? Press Show me a solution.

Investigation 8: Build your own slingshot

What to look for: A heavier planet bends the path more for the same distance, and an outer planet moves more slowly round the Sun, so it has less speed to lend. The ideal helper is massive and moving fast: that is why Jupiter is the favourite of mission planners.

Batch experiment 1: Speed gain vs flyby distance

The theory line is the slingshot formula: the turning angle sin(δ/2) = 1/(1 + r·v∞²/GM), then speed = |planet velocity + turned relative velocity|. Far flybys barely bend the path; the gain peaks at a few planet radii, then falls a little as the turn overshoots. The scatter comes from the random aiming errors.

Batch experiment 2: Speed gain vs approach angle

The gain is biggest when the flyby swings the relative velocity from pointing against the planet's motion round to pointing with it. Chasing the planet from behind (0°) the probe is turned away from the planet's direction and actually loses speed, even passing behind.

Batch experiment 3: Behind or in front?

Behind the planet: speed gained (the planet slows down by an unmeasurably tiny amount). In front: speed lost, and the planet speeds up. But skim very close in front and the path is swung so far round that the probe can gain again. The theory line has a gap in the middle, where the probe would hit the planet.

Batch experiment 4: In the planet's frame, nothing is gained

In the planet's frame gravity only turns the probe: it speeds up on the way in and slows by exactly the same amount on the way out. The tiny departures from 1 (millionths) are rounding in the step-by-step calculation. All the gain seen in the Sun's frame comes from adding the planet's own velocity.

Batch experiment 5: Heavier planet, bigger kick

A heavier planet turns the path more. But the gain cannot grow for ever: the most a flyby can ever add is twice the relative speed (2v∞), when the path turns right round. Beyond a few Jupiter masses the path is over-turned, past the planet's direction of motion, and the gain falls again.
