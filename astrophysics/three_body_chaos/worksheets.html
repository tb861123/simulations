Three-Body Chaos · Teacher notesthree-body-chaos.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Two universes

What to look for: The gap grows by the same factor in each interval of time: a straight line on the logarithmic chart. That is exponential growth, the signature of chaos. Once the gap is as big as the orbits, prediction has failed.

Investigation 2: Does better measurement help?

What to look for: No. Each factor of 1000 in precision buys only the same fixed amount of extra time, because the error grows exponentially. To predict twice as far ahead you must square your precision. This is why weather forecasts run out after about two weeks.

Investigation 3: A perfect figure-eight

What to look for: It survives small nudges (it is one of very few stable three-body orbits) but a larger push sends it into chaos, usually ending with one star thrown out. Nobody has yet found a real figure-eight system in the sky.

Investigation 4: The triangle that cannot last

What to look for: With equal masses the triangle is unstable: rounding errors in the arithmetic (about 10⁻¹⁶) are enough to break it eventually. Nature has rounding errors too. Now look at the Trojan asteroids, where one mass dominates and the triangle is stable.

Investigation 5: Trojan asteroids

What to look for: Those at 60° ahead and behind (the L4 and L5 Lagrange points) stay put: thousands of real Trojan asteroids share Jupiter's orbit there. The others wander. Chaos and stability can exist side by side in one system.

Investigation 6: Planet of three suns

What to look for: Calm stretches end without warning when another sun swings past: the planet may be stolen by a different sun, frozen in deep space or scorched. This is the setting of Liu Cixin's novel The Three-Body Problem. Real triple stars that last are hierarchical: a close pair with a distant third.

Investigation 7: Build a solar system

What to look for: Stable systems are hierarchical: each orbit is much smaller than the next one out, and small bodies stay well inside their planet's sphere of influence (the Hill sphere). Put orbits too close together and they perturb each other into chaos.

Batch experiment 1: The horizon of predictability

Each run begins from a very slightly different version of the system, and in a chaotic system close encounters (where errors grow fastest) come at different times. A straight line here means time ∝ log(precision): a million times more precision buys only a fixed number of extra time units.
Result from a test run: -12: 50.5 ± 11.7 · -10: 46.1 ± 3.76 · -8: 59.1 ± 12 · -6: 26.9 ± 4.1 · -4: 17.3 ± 1.33 · -2: 9.83 ± 0.124 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Lifetimes of triple stars

There is no typical lifetime: most triples break up quickly, but a few last a very long time (those piled up at 300 were still going when we stopped). The distribution has a long tail. Almost every triple ends as a close binary plus one runaway star.
Result from a test run: mean 51.6, SD 52.7, SE 4.81 from 120 runs

Batch experiment 3: How robust is the figure-eight?

Whether a kicked figure-eight survives depends on the direction of the kick as well as its size, so results near the edge of the stable region are a matter of chance.
Result from a test run: 0: 1 ± 0 · 0.01: 0.917 ± 0.0833 · 0.02: 0.917 ± 0.0833 · 0.04: 0.833 ± 0.112 · 0.07: 0.417 ± 0.149 · 0.1: 0.583 ± 0.149 · 0.15: 0.417 ± 0.149 (mean ± SE, 12 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: A planet with two suns

Close in, the planet feels two separate suns tugging in turn and its orbit is chaotic; far out, the pair acts like one sun. Astronomers find the boundary at roughly 2 to 2.5 times the suns' separation, and real planets with two suns (such as Kepler-16b) orbit just outside it.
Result from a test run: 1.2: 0 ± 0 · 1.5: 0 ± 0 · 1.8: 0 ± 0 · 2.1: 0.4 ± 0.163 · 2.4: 1 ± 0 · 2.8: 1 ± 0 · 3.2: 1 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)
