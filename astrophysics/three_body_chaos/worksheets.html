The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · How much does a finite sample change the outcome fractions?

Each run draws 100 random initial conditions (b uniform in [−3, 3], v in [0.25, 2.5], binary phase in [0°, 360°)) and simulates every one. The spread of the fraction from run to run should match the binomial prediction √(p(1−p)/n) if the draws are independent: a finite sample, not the physics, limits how well a probability is known. The standard error of the mean over runs shrinks as 1/√(runs).

2 · Does the energy error shrink with the tolerance?

The integrator controls its LOCAL error with the tolerance; the GLOBAL energy error accumulated over a whole encounter is roughly proportional to the tolerance, so halving the tolerance should roughly halve the error until round-off dominates. Energy conservation checks the numerics, not the answer: chaotic trajectories can be accurate in energy and still wrong in detail.

3 · How much does a very fast fly-by disturb the binary?

A fast fly-by gives the binary a short kick: the change in its relative velocity is proportional to 1/v. The change in binary energy is first order in that kick, so its sign depends on the binary phase (random here) while its mean MAGNITUDE should fall as 1/v: a log–log slope tending to −1 as v grows. At lower speeds the measured values lie below the line because the binary partly adjusts during the pass. The mean is over runs in which A–B survived and C escaped. A small floor (~10⁻³) comes from starting C at a finite distance (x = −12), where its tidal field already perturbs the circular binary.

4 · How small a change in b still changes the outcome?

Newtonian three-body motion is deterministic: as δ → 0 every nudged run must end like the unnudged one, and the fraction below 1 shows how large a change in the starting offset is needed to change the outcome at this starting point. That size depends on the starting point, the horizon and the integrator tolerance (changes far below the tolerance cannot be resolved meaningfully). This is a finite-time sensitivity measurement, not a proof of mathematical chaos.
