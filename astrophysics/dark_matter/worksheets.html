Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Which halo mass fits best? (χ² against trial mass)

What this shows. χ² is smallest when the trial halo mass equals the true mass (ratio 1). There the minimum is about the number of points (25), because each point is off by about one error bar. Heavier or lighter halos miss the data and χ² climbs steeply. The runs scatter because every run has new random errors.

4 · Noisier measurements → bigger uncertainty?

What this shows. The spread is proportional to the measurement error (gradient +1 on log–log axes) while the errors are small. At large errors the fitted masses become skewed (they cannot go below zero), so the simple theory starts to fail.

5 · How far out must we look?

What this shows. Close to the centre the visible matter dominates, so the halo hardly changes the speeds and its mass is badly measured. Far out the halo dominates and the mass is pinned down. This is why measuring hydrogen gas far beyond the stars (with radio telescopes) was so important.

6 · Is the best fit as good as it should be? (χ² distribution)

What this shows. For a correct model, the best-fit χ² is about N − 1 (25 points, 1 fitted number), with a spread of √(2(N − 1)) ≈ 7. A much bigger χ² would mean the model is wrong — like the no-dark-matter model.

7 · Dark-to-visible ratio vs the cosmic 5.3

What this shows. The Milky Way's halo is about 14 times as massive as its stars and gas, more than the cosmic average of 5.3. Galaxies have lost (or never collected) much of their share of ordinary matter. Dwarf galaxies have ratios in the hundreds.
