Dark Matter and Rotation Curves · Teacher notesdark-matter.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: What should happen?

What to look for: Without the halo, beyond the stars the speed falls (v ∝ 1/√r, like the planets). The measured speeds stay flat at about 220 km/s: the outer stars move far too fast for the visible mass alone.

Investigation 2: Stars would fly off

What to look for: They fly away: at the measured speeds, the visible mass cannot hold them in orbit. Something unseen must provide the extra gravity: a dark matter halo.

Investigation 3: Add the halo

What to look for: Several times more dark than visible inside 30 kpc, and the whole halo is about 5–10 times the visible mass (cosmologically, dark matter is about 5.3 times ordinary matter).

Investigation 4: Vera Rubin's method

What to look for: One side of the galaxy comes towards us (blueshift), the other goes away (redshift). The shift of the hydrogen line, Δλ = λv/c, gives the speed at each point along the slit. Vera Rubin and Kent Ford did this for Andromeda in 1970.

Investigation 5: A dwarf galaxy

What to look for: Almost all of it: the stars and gas are a tiny part, and the curve keeps rising to the last point. Dwarf galaxies are among the most dark-matter dominated objects known.

Investigation 6: Weigh the mystery halo

What to look for: The best fit is where χ² is smallest (about the number of points). Your answer should be within a few percent of the true halo mass.

Investigation 7: Mass from the speed A level

What to look for: For the Milky Way at 30 kpc with v ≈ 220 km/s: M = 220² × 30 ÷ 4.3 × 10⁻⁶ ≈ 3.4 × 10¹¹ M☉, about 5 times the visible mass.

Investigation 8: MOND instead A level

What to look for: For a single galaxy, yes, quite well: MOND boosts gravity where accelerations are tiny. But it struggles with galaxy clusters, gravitational lensing and the cosmic microwave background, which is why most astronomers favour dark matter.

Batch experiment 1: Which halo mass fits best?

A valley with its lowest point at the true mass, where χ² ≈ 25 (the number of points). Its width shows how precisely the data pin the halo down.

Batch experiment 2: How well can we weigh the halo?

The fits cluster around the true value with a spread of a few percent: rotation curves weigh the halo well (inside the region they cover).
Theory (true): 1.2
Result from a test run: mean 1.2, SD 0.0381, SE 0.0027 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 3: More data points

Four times as many points roughly halve the error.
Theory (∝ 1 ÷ √N): 5: 8.21 · 10: 5.8 · 20: 4.1 · 40: 2.9 · 80: 2.05
Result from a test run: 5: 3.03 ± 0.471 · 10: 4.16 ± 0.673 · 20: 4.27 ± 0.699 · 40: 1.69 ± 0.355 · 80: 1.65 ± 0.227 (mean ± SE, 15 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Visible mass against total mass

Big spirals are about a quarter to a third visible inside their discs; the dwarf galaxy is almost all dark.
Result from a test run: 1: 23 ± 0.513 · 2: 28.4 ± 0.613 · 3: 21.6 ± 0.64 · 4: 13.9 ± 0.448 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)
