How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Units: distance in kiloparsecs (1 kpc = 3260 light years), time in millions of years (Myr), mass in Suns (M☉). G = 4.50 × 10⁻¹² kpc³ M☉⁻¹ Myr⁻².
  • Each galaxy (restricted model) is a rigid, smooth ball of mass that moves as a whole: visible matter as a Plummer sphere (radius scale about 2.5 kpc for a Milky Way), plus a dark matter halo as a Hernquist sphere (scale about 30 kpc). Bigger galaxies are made bigger in proportion to (mass)1/3.
  • Gravity: Plummer: g = GMr/(r² + a²)3/2. Hernquist: g = GM/(r + a)². The pull between the two galaxies uses the same shapes with the two sizes combined.
  • Stars are test particles: they feel both galaxies but do not pull on anything. They start on circular orbits in a thin disc, spread out like a real disc (more in the middle).
  • Dynamical friction (Chandrasekhar): a galaxy moving through matter pulls a "wake" of matter behind it, and the wake drags it back: a = −4πG²M lnΛ ρ [erf(X) − 2X e−X²/√π] v / v³, with X = v/(√2 σ). The "Coulomb logarithm" lnΛ is set to 0.5, smaller than the textbook 2–3, because a rigid halo that is never stripped drags too hard; with this value the model gives the published Milky Way–Andromeda timing (first pass about 4 Gyr, merger about 6 Gyr) and equal-mass mergers after two or three passes, as in full simulations. This is what makes galaxies with dark matter halos spiral together. The cores are joined into one when they are closer than their combined core sizes and bound.
  • Leapfrog integration with 1 Myr steps (2 Myr in the N-body mode); gravity is "softened" at short range so nothing blows up.
  • Self-gravitating mode: 4000 particles (halo, bulge and disc) all pull on each other, worked out with a Barnes–Hut tree (θ = 0.7, softening 1 kpc). Friction and merging happen by themselves, but the discs are grainy and slowly thicken.
  • Gas and star formation: about a quarter of the disc particles are gas clouds. Where gas is squeezed, clouds that share a 1.5 kpc box are assumed to collide: their velocities are nudged towards the box's average (over about 100 Myr), so colliding gas loses energy and sinks inwards. Each cloud's density boost is capped at 10×, standing in for young stars blowing the gas apart (feedback). The model counts how crowded the clouds are; where gas is squeezed to 2.5× its starting density it is marked as forming new stars (blue). Rate follows the Schmidt law, rate ∝ density1.5. Real gas also has pressure, shocks and cools; this sticky-cloud rule is only a rough copy.
  • Stars in tails: stars more than twice their galaxy's starting disc radius from every core. Stripped: no longer bound to their own galaxy. Escaping: moving faster than the escape speed of the whole system.
  • Star–star collisions: each star sweeps out a tube with area σ = π(2R☉)², boosted by gravitational focusing (1 + vesc²/v²), through the other galaxy's stars (number density n = ρ / 0.5 M☉). Expected hits = Σ nσv Δt, scaled up to the real number of stars.
  • Left out: gas pressure, shocks and cooling; supernovae and black holes; the halos being stripped and stretched (they stay rigid spheres); the expansion of the Universe; other galaxies nearby (e.g. M33 and the LMC for the Milky Way); stars' masses.
  • Where it disagrees with reality: a rigid halo with a friction formula is only a rough copy of a real, deformable halo, so merger times are uncertain by tens of percent. Toomre's model still gets the shapes of tails and bridges remarkably right.

Why tails?

Gravity gets weaker with distance. The side of a disc nearest the other galaxy is pulled harder than its centre, and the centre harder than the far side. This stretching (a tide, like the Moon's tides on Earth) pulls out a bridge towards the other galaxy and flings a tail out behind.
