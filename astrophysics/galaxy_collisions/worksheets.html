Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Tails from tides

What to look for: Each galaxy pulls harder on the near side of the other's disc than on the far side: a tide. Stars on the near side are dragged into a bridge towards the passing galaxy; stars on the far side are left behind and swing out into a long tail. The tails are made of the outer disc stars, which are the most loosely held. Real examples: the Antennae and the Mice.

Investigation 2: Spin matters

What to look for: When the disc spins the same way as the visitor passes, the stars on the near side keep pace with the visitor for a long time, so its pull has time to build up: a resonance, like pushing a swing in time with its motion. Spinning the other way, each star rushes past the visitor and feels only a brief tug, so the disc is hardly disturbed.

Investigation 3: A ring galaxy

What to look for: As the intruder passes through, its extra gravity pulls every star in the disc inwards for a moment. When it has gone, the stars rebound outwards and then fall back, each at the pace of its own orbit. Their crowding together forms a ring that travels outwards like a ripple. The Cartwheel galaxy is a real one; its ring is lit by newborn stars.

Investigation 4: The Whirlpool

What to look for: The companion's tide stretches the disc into two grand spiral arms: one reaches out to the companion as a bridge, the other swings out on the far side. Many of the most beautiful two-armed spirals, such as the Whirlpool (M51), have a companion like this.

Investigation 5: Why do galaxies merge?

What to look for: With halos, each galaxy ploughs through the other's dark matter, which drags on it (dynamical friction). The orbit loses energy every pass, so they fall back sooner each time and merge. Without dark matter there is almost nothing to push against: the galaxies swing past each other and fly apart. The fact that we see so many merged galaxies is one line of evidence for dark matter.

Investigation 6: Galactic cannibalism

What to look for: Tides strip the dwarf's stars away a little on every orbit and spread them along its path as long streams that wrap round the big galaxy. Eventually the dwarf is gone and its stars are part of the big galaxy's halo. The Milky Way is doing this now to the Sagittarius dwarf, and streams of stars from earlier victims cross our sky.

Investigation 7: Our own future

What to look for: In this model they first pass in about 4 billion years, swing out, fall back and merge within a couple of billion years more, into one large, rounded, featureless galaxy sometimes nicknamed 'Milkomeda'. How direct the first pass is depends on Andromeda's sideways speed, which is still poorly measured. The Sun will most likely still be orbiting inside the merged galaxy, further out than now.

Investigation 8: Do the stars crash?

What to look for: Almost none. Stars are tiny compared with the gaps between them: if the Sun were a grain of sand, the next star would be about 30 km away. A star crossing a whole galaxy has roughly a 1 in 100 billion chance of hitting another, so among 10¹¹ stars you expect about one collision. The stars pass like two swarms of bees; it is gravity, not impact, that reshapes the galaxies. Gas clouds are different: they are huge, so they do collide.

Investigation 9: Starbursts

What to look for: Star formation jumps at each close pass and again at the final merger, when gas is driven into the centre. Colliding galaxies can form stars a hundred times faster than the Milky Way does now: 'starburst' galaxies. After the merger the gas is used up or blown out, and the result is a 'red and dead' elliptical galaxy.

Batch experiment 6: Glancing blow or direct hit?

A close first pass takes the galaxies through the densest part of each other's halo, where friction is strongest, so they lose more energy and fall back sooner. The orbits are not random, so repeat runs agree.
