Galaxy Collisions · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Does a closer pass throw more stars into tails?

Closer passes throw far more stars into tails: tidal stretching depends on the difference in gravity across a disc, which grows steeply as the galaxies get closer (about 1/distance³). The blue theory line is the simple 'impulse approximation': one quick sideways kick to each star. It predicts almost no tails, because these galaxies pass quickly and their dark matter halos hold stars in tightly. The simulated prograde discs beat it by a long way: their stars orbit in step with the passing galaxy, so the pull keeps acting in the same direction (a resonance). Try the retrograde experiment: those runs sit close to the theory. Repeats scatter because stars are placed at random and the discs are tilted slightly differently each time.

2 · Prograde: how many stars are thrown out?

With both discs spinning the same way as the orbit, a large share of stars ends up in long tails, far more than the simple impulse theory (blue line, near zero) predicts. The extra comes from resonance: prograde stars move along with the passing galaxy and feel its pull for longer. Run the retrograde experiment next: this result will stay on the chart as a grey outline for comparison.

3 · Retrograde: how many stars are thrown out?

Retrograde discs make far fewer tail stars than prograde ones, even though the tidal force is the same. A star going the other way sees the tidal pull swing round quickly, so the kicks cancel out. This is why Toomre & Toomre (1972) found that only prograde encounters make long tails. Retrograde results sit close to the impulse theory (blue line), which ignores resonance.

4 · When will the Milky Way and Andromeda merge?

Each run uses a different 'possible' Andromeda orbit, picked from the measurement errors (sideways speed 57 ± 33 km/s, distance, masses). Slow sideways speeds give a near head-on collision and a merger in about 5–6 billion years, as van der Marel et al. (2012) found. Faster sideways speeds give a wide first pass and a much later merger, or none within 10 billion years (those runs are not in the histogram, but are counted). Sawala et al. (2025), who also included the Large Magellanic Cloud and M33, found only about a 50% chance of a merger in the next 10 billion years. Uncertain inputs give an uncertain answer.

5 · Does dark matter make galaxies merge?

Heavier dark matter halos make galaxies merge sooner. A galaxy moving through a halo pulls a wake of dark matter behind it, and the wake's gravity drags it back (dynamical friction). More halo means more drag, so orbital energy is lost faster. Runs with no merger within 4 billion years are marked at the top. Without halos (see the 'No dark matter' preset) the galaxies usually fly apart again.

6 · Do any stars actually crash into each other?

Hardly ever. Stars are tiny compared with the space between them: the Sun is about 1.4 million km across, but the nearest star is 40 million million km away. Shrink the Sun to a grain of sand, and the next grain is 10 km away. So even when two galaxies of a hundred billion stars each pass through each other, only a handful of stars, if any, collide. The simulation's number varies with the orientation of the discs; the theory is a rough straight-line estimate.
