Teacher notes

Investigation 1: A gallery of shapes

What to look for: Symmetric equations give symmetric shapes: Thomas's (x → y → z → x) has three-fold symmetry, Halvorsen's too. Rössler's has one slow loop that occasionally flicks up out of the plane (the z term switches on only when x > c). Nobody can predict the detailed shape by looking: you have to compute it.

Investigation 2: The butterfly effect

What to look for: The gap grows exponentially (a straight line on the log chart) until it is as big as the attractor itself, then it stops growing. For Rössler, λ ≈ 0.07, so each 33 time units the gap grows 10 times: from 10⁻⁶ to 1 takes about 200 time units. Halving the starting error only buys 10 more time units. That is why weather forecasts fail after about two weeks.

Investigation 3: The road to chaos

What to look for: The loop doubles: at about c = 3 it goes round twice before repeating (period 2), at about 4.2 four times (period 4), then 8, 16… faster and faster, until at about 4.5 it never repeats: chaos, and λ becomes positive. This 'period-doubling cascade' happens in dripping taps, heart rhythms and populations too (Feigenbaum).

Investigation 4: Every start, the same shape

What to look for: On the same shape every time: that is what 'attractor' means. The motion along it is unpredictable, but the shape it moves on is completely predictable. Weather is like that too: you cannot say whether it will rain on 3 August next year, but you can say what the climate (the attractor) will be like.

Investigation 5: Turn the friction down

What to look for: Above about 0.33 every path settles to one point. Below it, cycles appear, then (around 0.2) chaos. As b approaches 0 the attractor fills a lattice of cells and the motion becomes like a random walk ('labyrinth chaos'), even though nothing random is in the equations.

Investigation 6: Maps: jumps, not flows

What to look for: Often it changes smoothly, then suddenly collapses into a handful of dots (a periodic orbit: λ < 0) or explodes into a cloud. Chaos and order are finely interleaved in the parameter space. The batch run maps λ against a.

Batch experiment 1: Rössler: the road to chaos

λ is zero (a cycle) through the period doublings up to about c = 4.2, then turns positive: chaos. At c = 5.7 it is about 0.07 (published value 0.0714). Short runs and different starting points give slightly different estimates.
Result from a test run: 2.5: -0.0013 ± 2.01e-4 · 3: 5.61e-4 ± 1.30e-4 · 3.5: -2.79e-6 ± 6.78e-4 · 4: -5.14e-4 ± 1.44e-4 · 4.2: -1.57e-4 ± 3.38e-4 · 4.5: 0.0538 ± 0.0019 · 5: 0.0669 ± 0.00238 · 5.7: 0.0691 ± 6.63e-4 · 7: 0.0759 ± 0.00407 · 9: 0.0817 ± 0.00207 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Two nearby starts

The gap grows roughly exponentially (a rising straight line on this log scale), but the test run rose a little more slowly than the λ = 0.0714 line, about e^(0.054 t) over 150 time units: a fresh gap first has to turn to line up with the direction of greatest stretching, and the stretching is uneven around the attractor (fast in some places, slow or even shrinking in others). Single runs wander a lot. After about 250 time units the gap reaches the size of the attractor and stops growing.

Result from a test run: 0: -6 ± 1.45e-11 · 25: -5.43 ± 0.166 · 50: -4.82 ± 0.234 · 75: -3.9 ± 0.525 · 100: -3.71 ± 0.289 · 125: -2.58 ± 0.357 · 150: -2.48 ± 0.229 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Thomas: friction and chaos

λ is largest for small b (labyrinth chaos), small or zero in windows of order, and zero once b is large enough for the motion to settle into a cycle or a point. The estimates are noisy: Thomas's chaos is slow, and 1500 time units is not long for it.
Result from a test run: 0.05: 0.134 ± 0.00131 · 0.1: 0.0556 ± 0.00841 · 0.15: 0.00145 ± 4.99e-4 · 0.18: 0.035 ± 0.00319 · 0.21: 0.00344 ± 2.73e-4 · 0.25: 2.54e-4 ± 2.34e-4 · 0.3: 0.00326 ± 2.07e-4 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Clifford map: chaos and order

Positive λ means a chaotic, cloud-like attractor; negative λ means the points settle onto a few dots or a smooth closed curve. Order and chaos alternate as a changes. Different starts can land on different attractors when two exist side by side.
