Powers of Ten · Teacher notespowers-of-ten.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: From you to everything

What to look for: From 1.7 m to 8.8 × 10²⁶ m is about 27 powers of ten. Down to a quark (smaller than 10⁻¹⁸ m) is at least another 18. Each power of ten is ten times bigger than the last, so these journeys are not 'a bit bigger' but billions of billions of times.

Investigation 2: Inside your hand

What to look for: Skin cell (30 µm), then its nucleus (6 µm), DNA (2 nm wide), then an atom (0.14 nm). About 300 000 hydrogen atoms fit across one skin cell.

Investigation 3: Atoms are empty

What to look for: About 28 000 nuclei across. A 7 mm pea nucleus would mean an atom about 200 m across: a football stadium with a pea in the middle and a few specks of electron cloud. Almost all the mass is in that pea; the rest is empty space. (Rutherford found this in 1911 by firing alpha particles through gold foil.)

Investigation 4: Hydrogen atoms across the Sun

What to look for: About 1.3 × 10¹⁹: thirteen billion billion. In the Batch run, the uncertainty in each size is used to work out how sure we can be of answers like this.

Investigation 5: The middle of everything

What to look for: Halfway in powers of ten is 10^4.5 m, about 30 km: the size of a city like London, not a person. On a logarithmic scale, 'middle' means the same number of times bigger than the small end as it is smaller than the big end.

Investigation 6: How long would light take?

What to look for: Light crosses the Earth in 0.04 s and the Moon's orbit in 2.6 s. It takes 8.3 minutes from the Sun to the Earth, about a day to cross the Oort cloud's inner parts, 20 years to cross our stellar neighbourhood and 100 000 years to cross the Milky Way. When we look at distant galaxies we see them as they were long ago.

Investigation 7: Where are we?

What to look for: We are about halfway out in the Milky Way's disc (26 000 light-years from its centre), and the Milky Way is one of two big galaxies in the Local Group. We are at the centre of the observable universe only because it is defined by how far light has travelled to us: every observer anywhere is at the centre of their own.

Batch experiment 1: How many fit across?

The answers span 2 to 19 powers of ten, so they are plotted as log₁₀: 4 means 10 000. Pairs involving a cell, a person or a galaxy scatter most, because those sizes vary (people and cells) or are hard to define (where does a galaxy end?). Pairs of Earth and Sun barely scatter: they are measured to a fraction of a per cent.

Batch experiment 2: How many fit inside?

Volume goes as length cubed, so the log is three times bigger, and so is the scatter: a 10% uncertainty in a length becomes about 30% in a volume. About 1.3 million Earths would fit inside the Sun.

Batch experiment 3: Size against mass

If everything had the density of water, the points would lie on the line (mass ∝ size³). People, whales and planets nearly do. Nuclei and neutron stars lie far above it (10¹⁷ kg/m³: matter crushed together). Galaxies and the universe lie far below it: they are almost entirely empty space.
