Life and Cellular Automata · Teacher noteslife-automata.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: A gun that fires gliders

What to look for: One glider every 30 generations, flying off diagonally for ever. Bill Gosper found it in 1970, winning a $50 prize from Conway, who had guessed no pattern could grow without limit. A gun is a pattern whose population grows for ever: the chart climbs in steps.

Investigation 2: Five cells, a thousand generations

What to look for: It churns for 1103 generations before settling into still lifes, blinkers and six gliders that fly away. Tiny starts can have very long, unpredictable futures: there is no shortcut, you have to run it to find out.

Investigation 3: Primordial soup

What to look for: Most cells die within a few hundred generations, leaving 'ash': a few percent of the grid alive. The commonest objects are the block (2×2), the beehive and the blinker (period 2); gliders escape from time to time. Old cells are drawn darker, so still lifes stand out.

Investigation 4: A replicator

What to look for: In HighLife it copies itself again and again, making a growing triangle of replicators. In Life (no birth on 6) the same shape just burns out. One extra number in the rule changes everything.

Investigation 5: Day and Night

What to look for: Islands of 'day' and lakes of 'night' grow and battle, and either can contain the same objects (there are spaceships in both). Because the rule is symmetric, a live blob in a dead sea behaves exactly like a dead blob in a live sea.

Investigation 6: Rule 30, 90 and 110

What to look for: Rule 90 draws the Sierpinski triangle: perfectly regular and fractal. Rule 30 looks random (Mathematica used its centre column as a random number generator). Rule 110 has structures that move and collide; it was proved (Cook, 2004) able to compute anything a computer can: from 8 bits of rule!

Investigation 7: Make your own rule

What to look for: Most rules either die quickly or explode into noise. Interesting behaviour (moving objects, long-lived patterns) tends to sit near the boundary between the two: the 'edge of chaos' idea. The batch run 'Order, chaos and the edge' measures it with random rules.

Batch experiment 1: Soup density and the ash

Very thin soups die out (too few cells to make births) and very thick ones die of overcrowding at once. Anything from about 10% to 70% burns down to roughly the same ash, a few percent of the grid (about 4–6% on these small grids after 600 generations): the soup forgets how it started. Small grids vary a lot because a single glider or blinker is a big share of the ash.
Result from a test run: 0.05: 0.00864 ± 0.00538 · 0.1: 0.0414 ± 0.00553 · 0.2: 0.0518 ± 0.00936 · 0.3: 0.0583 ± 0.0112 · 0.4: 0.047 ± 0.0103 · 0.5: 0.0495 ± 0.00582 · 0.6: 0.0433 ± 0.00584 · 0.7: 0.0292 ± 0.00583 · 0.8: 0.0041 ± 8.58e-4 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Will a small soup die out?

Each run is 0 or 1, so 20 runs give a rough probability (its standard error is up to 0.1). Sparse patches (10%) usually die out completely; at 20% a few do; from 30% upwards none of the test runs died out, because some still life or oscillator almost always forms.
Result from a test run: 0.1: 0.7 ± 0.105 · 0.2: 0.2 ± 0.0918 · 0.3: 0 ± 0 · 0.4: 0 ± 0 · 0.5: 0 ± 0 · 0.6: 0 ± 0 · 0.7: 0 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Order, chaos and the edge

Rules with few births and survivals freeze (nothing changes). Rules with very many fill the grid and freeze full. In between, activity is highest and runs vary hugely: some rules at the same λ freeze while others seethe. Langton (1990) suggested that complex behaviour like Life's (λ = 5/18 ≈ 0.28) lives near the transition between frozen and seething, 'the edge of chaos'.
Result from a test run: 0.06: 0 ± 0 · 0.17: 0.106 ± 0.0694 · 0.28: 0.418 ± 0.0927 · 0.39: 0.369 ± 0.125 · 0.5: 0.458 ± 0.0809 · 0.61: 0.328 ± 0.0824 · 0.72: 0.299 ± 0.0955 · 0.83: 0.161 ± 0.0787 · 0.94: 0 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Rule 30 as a coin

The fractions scatter round 0.5 with a spread close to a real coin's (SD √(0.25/1000) ≈ 0.016), even though rule 30 is completely deterministic. Its centre column passes many statistical tests of randomness.
Theory (a fair coin): 0.5
