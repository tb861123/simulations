Teacher notes

Investigation 1: How few circles?

What to look for: 1 circle: a circle (the average size and position). 2–3: an egg-like oval. By about 5–8 the heart's dimple and point appear; by 20 it is almost perfect. The big, slow circles carry most of the shape; the heart's sharp point and dent need many smaller, faster ones.

Investigation 2: Corners are expensive

What to look for: The circle needs exactly one. The square's corners are sharp, and sharp changes need fast circles: its radii shrink only like 1/k², so even 50 circles leave slightly rounded corners. (Its symmetry helps: only every fourth k is used.) The batch run measures how the error falls.

Investigation 3: Draw your own

What to look for: Smooth, rounded shapes need few circles; sharp corners, zigzags and tiny details need many. The very fast circles are tiny: they only matter for fine detail. Your drawing has at most 256 points here, so 256 circles reproduce those points exactly.

Investigation 4: Which way round?

What to look for: With only anticlockwise circles you can make curves that loop the same way, but most shapes need both. A circle and a clockwise circle of the same size together make a straight back-and-forth line: that is how a real-valued wave (the x-coordinate alone) is built, and why sound only needs k ≥ 0.

Investigation 5: Hear the shape

What to look for: The circle's x is a pure sine wave: a pure note. The heart has a few strong harmonics: a soft, round tone. The star and square have many strong high harmonics (their corners), so they sound bright and buzzy. Turning down the number of circles removes harmonics, so the sound gets duller: a low-pass filter. Inside Sound and Build a Timbre explore this further.

Batch experiment 1: Error against circles: the heart

The error falls quickly at first (about 10% of the heart’s size with 1 circle, about 1.5% with 10) and then more slowly (about 0.1% with 50), because the heart has a sharp point at the bottom and a dent at the top, and sharp features need many fast circles. Parseval's theorem says the mean-square error at the sample points is exactly the sum of the left-out circles' radii squared; measured between the samples the error comes out very slightly smaller.

Batch experiment 2: Error against circles: the square

The square's circles shrink like 1/k² (because of its corners), so its error falls roughly like M^(−1.5): about 0.3% of its size with 10 circles and 0.03% with 50, but its corners stay slightly rounded however many you use. Its four-fold symmetry helps: only every fourth k (1, −3, 5, −7, …) is used at all.

Batch experiment 3: How many circles for 1%?

The circle needs exactly 1. The star (4) and the square (5) need few, because their symmetry means only every fifth or fourth k is used and a few big circles do most of the work. The heart (about 13) and the bird (about 16), with no such symmetry and with sharp points and thin wings, need more. This is the idea behind JPEG pictures and MP3 sound: most of a real signal is carried by a few big Fourier components, so the rest can be thrown away.
