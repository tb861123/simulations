Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Read the bar-gate graph

What to look for: Stock falls about 20 a day. At the re-order level (20 × 5 + 30 = 130) an order of 200 goes in; 5 days later it arrives and stock jumps by 200. 200 tyres last about 10 days.

Investigation 2: Why keep a buffer?

What to look for: Without a buffer, stock only just lasts the lead time on an average day. On a busier-than-average week it runs out roughly half the time: lost sales and annoyed customers.

Investigation 3: Longer lead time

What to look for: Re-order level = usage × lead time + buffer = 20 × 10 + 30 = 230. Order earlier, and the extra uncertainty over a longer wait needs more buffer.

Investigation 4: Just in time

What to look for: Average stock falls from about 130 to about 30: less cash tied up, less space, less waste. But the business depends completely on its supplier.

Investigation 5: JIT with an unreliable supplier

What to look for: With almost no stock, every late delivery empties the shelves. JIT needs reliable suppliers, close relationships, and often nearby suppliers.

Investigation 6: Order big or order often? A level

What to look for: Big orders: few orders but lots of stock held. Small orders: little stock but many orders. EOQ = √(2 × 20 × 40 ÷ 0.05) ≈ 179 tyres balances them (beyond the spec).

Investigation 7: The bullwhip effect A level

What to look for: Each stage reads the orders below as demand and over-corrects. The swing grows at every step: the factory sees far bigger variation than the shoppers ever made.

Investigation 8: A small step in demand A level

What to look for: A permanent 20% rise produces a huge spike at the factory, then a slump: it looks like a boom then a collapse. Sharing sales data along the chain (and shorter lead times) tames the bullwhip.

Batch experiment 1: Buffer stock and stockouts

Each extra σ√L ≈ 13 tyres of buffer cuts the risk sharply. Demand over the lead time varies by σ√L because day-to-day ups and downs partly cancel. Stock is only checked once a day, so on average the order goes in about half a day's sales (d/2 = 10) below the re-order level: the theory allows for that.
Theory (1 − Φ((buffer − d/2) ÷ σ√L)): 0: 77.2 · 5: 64.5 · 10: 50 · 15: 35.5 · 20: 22.8 · 25: 13.2 · 30: 6.8 · 40: 1.27
Result from a test run: 0: 72 ± 3.01 · 5: 60.1 ± 0.589 · 10: 51.2 ± 1.52 · 15: 34.7 ± 1.46 · 20: 24.9 ± 2.02 · 25: 16 ± 1.63 · 30: 9.24 ± 1.85 · 40: 2.76 ± 0.499 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Re-order quantity and cost (A level)

A U-shape: ordering costs fall as 1/Q while holding costs rise with Q. The bottom is at EOQ = √(2dS/H) ≈ 179.
Theory (H(Q/2 + buffer) + S·d/Q): 50: 18.8 · 100: 12 · 150: 10.6 · 200: 10.5 · 300: 11.7 · 400: 13.5 · 600: 17.8
Result from a test run: 50: 18.7 ± 0.0848 · 100: 12.1 ± 0.024 · 150: 10.6 ± 0.0119 · 200: 10.6 ± 0.0365 · 300: 11.7 ± 0.0339 · 400: 13.5 ± 0.0422 · 600: 17.9 ± 0.0581 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: JIT and late deliveries

Even a few late deliveries cost sales, because there is almost no buffer to fall back on. Compare: with buffer stock of 30 and a 5-day lead time the same late deliveries matter much less.
Result from a test run: 0: 2.09 ± 0.154 · 2: 4.4 ± 0.333 · 5: 6.38 ± 0.421 · 10: 11.6 ± 0.486 · 15: 15.4 ± 0.27 · 20: 17.5 ± 0.836 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Bullwhip and lead time (beyond spec)

The amplification grows with the square of the lead time: long lead times mean big safety margins that swing with every forecast change.
Theory (1 + 2L/p + 2L²/p²): 1: 1.63 · 2: 2.5 · 3: 3.63 · 4: 5 · 6: 8.5 · 8: 13
Result from a test run: 1: 1.62 ± 0.0119 · 2: 2.5 ± 0.0368 · 3: 3.53 ± 0.093 · 4: 5.04 ± 0.0628 · 6: 8.53 ± 0.205 · 8: 12.8 ± 0.309 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Up the chain (beyond spec)

The swing multiplies at each stage, even faster than the rough theory (which treats each stage's demand as random and independent: in fact each stage passes on lumpy, correlated orders, which are amplified even more).
Theory ((1 + 2L/p + 2L²/p²)ᵏ (rough)): 1: 2.5 · 2: 6.25 · 3: 15.6 · 4: 39.1
Result from a test run: 1: 2.47 ± 0.0309 · 2: 7.16 ± 0.162 · 3: 23.2 ± 0.675 · 4: 82.4 ± 1.58 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)
