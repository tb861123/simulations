Cash-Flow Forecast · Teacher notescash-flow-forecast.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Read the forecast

What to look for: Each month's closing balance becomes the next month's opening balance. Negative numbers are shown with a minus sign: the business is overdrawn.

Investigation 2: When is cash tightest?

What to look for: It buys materials a month before it sells, and its customers pay 60 days after buying. Cash goes out for the spring rush long before it comes back in: the classic seasonal squeeze.

Investigation 3: Give less credit

What to look for: Cash from credit sales arrives a month sooner, so the overdraft needed falls sharply. But customers may go to a rival who offers longer credit.

Investigation 4: Profitable but broke

What to look for: Profit for the year can be healthy while cash runs out in autumn: stock is bought 2 months ahead and paid for before the Christmas sales arrive. Many firms fail like this.

Investigation 5: Forecast against actual

What to look for: Small monthly differences add up: closing balances carry every earlier error forward. That is why forecasts are updated (variance analysis).

Investigation 6: Take out a loan

What to look for: A lump of cash at the start keeps the balance positive, but repayments (with interest) are a new outflow every month for two years.

Investigation 7: Debt factoring A level

What to look for: The factor pays for credit sales straight away, so the cash squeeze disappears, but 4% of credit sales is lost as a fee: better liquidity, lower profit.

Investigation 8: Bad debts A level

What to look for: Both: a customer who never pays is a lost sale (written off, lowering profit) and lost cash. For a firm already near its overdraft limit, it can be the last straw.

Batch experiment 1: Lowest balance in an uncertain year

Most actual years land near the forecast, but late payers pull the low point down (a systematic shift), and a bad spring takes it much lower. A sensible overdraft limit allows for the worst likely year, not the forecast.
Theory (forecast): -18700
Result from a test run: mean -20200, SD 3390, SE 196 from 300 runs

Batch experiment 2: Credit terms for customers

Each extra month of credit holds back a whole month of credit sales. The cash isn't lost, just late, but the business needs a bigger overdraft to wait for it.
Theory (forecast): 0: -11200 · 30: -13500 · 60: -18700 · 90: -23100
Result from a test run: 0: -12600 ± 609 · 30: -15700 ± 662 · 60: -19400 ± 603 · 90: -24600 ± 840 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Credit from suppliers

Trade credit is a free source of short-term finance: paying suppliers later keeps cash in the business for longer. But look at 90 days: last Christmas's stock is still being paid for in January, when sales are low. Timing matters, not just length.
Theory (forecast): 0: -40400 · 30: -23100 · 60: -6860 · 90: -27100
Result from a test run: 0: -43300 ± 1280 · 30: -23100 ± 1890 · 60: -8010 ± 984 · 90: -28500 ± 1220 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Seasonality and the overdraft

Same annual sales, very different cash needs: the more seasonal the business, the deeper the trough before the busy season pays out.
Theory (forecast): 0: -1500 · 0.25: -3150 · 0.5: -6350 · 0.75: -12500 · 1: -18700 · 1.25: -25000 · 1.5: -31800
Result from a test run: 0: -1750 ± 227 · 0.25: -3300 ± 235 · 0.5: -8060 ± 771 · 0.75: -13500 ± 677 · 1: -20400 ± 1030 · 1.25: -26000 ± 541 · 1.5: -33600 ± 522 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Factoring: cash against profit (A level)

Profit falls by the fee × credit sales (60% of £240k a year): about £1,440 per percentage point. Factoring buys liquidity with profit.
Theory (forecast): 0: 16300 · 2: 13600 · 4: 11000 · 6: 8310 · 8: 5660 · 10: 3000
Result from a test run: 0: 15300 ± 1050 · 2: 13100 ± 1650 · 4: 11000 ± 1190 · 6: 9610 ± 1300 · 8: 5160 ± 1040 · 10: 2600 ± 1290 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)
