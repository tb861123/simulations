How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Shear building: each floor is a lumped mass (all of a floor's weight at slab level). The columns of each storey act as a sideways spring of stiffness k, so the building is a chain of masses and springs on moving ground. Each storey is 3.5 m tall and every storey is identical.
  • Equation of motion for floor i (relative to the ground): m üi = −k(ui−ui−1) + k(ui+1−ui) − damping − m ag. Solved with 4th-order Runge–Kutta, time step 5 ms or less.
  • Materials: steel frame 600 t per floor, 2% damping; concrete 900 t per floor, 5% damping; timber 250 t per floor, 4% damping. Storey stiffness is chosen so the natural period is close to the real-world rule of thumb T ≈ 0.1 × storeys (steel), 0.09 × storeys (concrete) or 0.11 × storeys (timber).
  • Natural frequencies are found exactly from the masses and springs (an eigenvalue calculation). For identical storeys the theory gives ωn = 2√(k/m) sin((2n−1)π / (2(2N+1))), so T1 grows in proportion to the number of storeys N.
  • Damping: "Rayleigh" damping, set so the first two modes have the material's damping ratio ζ (plus any added dampers).
  • Damage: inter-storey drift = relative sideways movement of a storey ÷ its height. Cracking starts at about 0.5% (concrete) or 1% (steel, timber); 2–3% means severe damage or collapse. The model stays linear: a cracked storey is shown, but it does not get softer, so it cannot collapse or change its period as a real damaged building would.
  • Tuned mass damper: an extra mass on the roof on its own spring and damper, tuned close to the building's first natural frequency (Den Hartog's best tuning: fd/f1 = 1/(1+μ), damping √(3μ/(8(1+μ)³)), μ = mass ratio). Taipei 101's 660-tonne pendulum is the famous example.
  • Base isolation: the building sits on a base slab on rubber bearings. Their stiffness is set so the whole building rocks gently with the chosen isolated period (2–3 s), and lead cores give 10–20% damping. Very soft bearings need a wide gap ("moat") round the building — see the bearing movement readout.
  • Earthquakes: random "white noise" shaped by a Kanai–Tajimi filter (the soil layer acts like a damped spring that amplifies its own frequency), a high-pass filter (no slow drift), and an envelope that rises, holds and dies away. Strong shaking lasts longer for bigger magnitudes (about 2 s at M5, 8 s at M7, 20 s at M8). The record is scaled so its peak equals the rock acceleration × the soil amplification (rock ×1, stiff soil ×1.25, soft soil ×1.6, lake-bed clay ×4).
  • Not modelled: twisting, up-and-down shaking, the building rocking or the soil giving way, foundations, non-uniform storeys, cracking softening the frame, collapse, and pounding between neighbouring buildings. Real records also have pulses and long-period surface waves that this random model does not.
