Domino Chain Reactions · Teacher notesdomino-chains.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: One small push

What to look for: Each standing domino stores gravitational potential energy: its centre of mass is high. Your push only has to lift the first one past its tipping point (its centre over its front edge). After that, every domino falling releases its own energy, which tips the next. The wave runs at a steady speed, about 1 m/s for ordinary dominoes.

Investigation 2: How far apart?

What to look for: Close together, each domino only has to tip a little before it hits the next, so the wave is quick. Far apart, each falls further first. More than about one height (plus a thickness) apart, a domino lands flat without reaching the next one and the wave stops dead. The 'Wider and wider' line shows exactly where.

Investigation 3: Where does it stop?

What to look for: It stops where the gap between one domino's front and the next one's back is a little more than the height: the falling domino's top can no longer reach. Before that, the wave slows down as the gaps grow.

Investigation 4: Friction matters

What to look for: With no friction the simulation agrees with van Leeuwen's model to a few per cent. Real dominoes rub as they slide over each other, which wastes energy, so real waves are slower (about 30% here). A good model tells you what you have left out.

Investigation 5: The domino amplifier

What to look for: Yes: the energy that tips each domino is not the push from the one before but its own stored potential energy. A small domino only has to lift a big one slightly to tip it. This is an amplifier: a tiny input releases a huge output. Lorne Whitehead (1983) showed that real dominoes can grow by about 1.5 times each step; in this model the collisions are not springy, so the limit is about 1.4 (see the Batch run).

Investigation 6: Two ways at once

What to look for: They do (within the wobble of the start): the speed of the wave depends on the dominoes and their spacing, not on how hard the first one was pushed. However it starts, the wave settles to the same steady speed. That is the signature of a wave carried by the medium itself.

Investigation 7: Build a contraption

What to look for: Energy is passed on in different forms: gravitational potential energy to kinetic energy of the dominoes, then of the rolling ball, back to the dominoes. Each step loses some energy as heat and sound, so every stage must be 'set' (store its own energy), as dominoes and balls on slopes do.

Batch experiment 1: Wave speed vs spacing (no friction)

The model treats the fallen dominoes as a train leaning on each other, with sticky collisions and no friction. It agrees with the simulation to a few per cent. Closer spacing means a faster wave; at d/h ≈ 1.16 (height plus thickness) the dominoes can no longer reach each other.

Result from a test run: 0.3: 1.31 ± 0.00197 · 0.4: 1.27 ± 2.76e-4 · 0.5: 1.23 ± 0.00445 · 0.6: 1.18 ± 0.0013 · 0.7: 1.12 ± 2.67e-4 · 0.8: 1.07 ± 2.46e-5 · 0.9: 1.02 ± 1.29e-4 · 1: 0.969 ± 0.00322 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Friction slows the wave

Each fallen domino slides down the back of the next one. Friction turns some of the energy into heat, so the train pushes less hard and the wave is slower. Real dominoes (μ about 0.2–0.3) run about 25–35% slower than the frictionless model.
Theory (model without friction): 0: 1.23 · 0.1: 1.23 · 0.2: 1.23 · 0.3: 1.23 · 0.4: 1.23 · 0.5: 1.23
Result from a test run: 0: 1.23 ± 0.00296 · 0.1: 1.04 ± 4.92e-4 · 0.2: 0.925 ± 0.00138 · 0.3: 0.823 ± 3.98e-4 · 0.4: 0.752 ± 0.00137 · 0.5: 0.68 ± 0.00261 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Bigger dominoes, faster wave

Everything about a falling domino scales with √(g h): the wave speed is proportional to the square root of the height. Four times taller, twice as fast. The theory line is the frictionless model scaled by 0.71 (the slowing found in the friction experiment); only its shape is being tested.
Theory (∝ √h (model × 0.71, the friction factor)): 12: 0.438 · 24: 0.62 · 48: 0.877 · 96: 1.24 · 192: 1.75
Result from a test run: 12: 0.423 ± 0.00155 · 24: 0.6 ± 7.41e-5 · 48: 0.876 ± 0.00203 · 96: 1.24 ± 0.00114 · 192: 1.77 ± 0.0015 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Does the wave get through?

A domino can only reach the next one if the gap between them (spacing minus thickness) is less than its height. Near the limit, small placement errors decide it: one slightly-too-wide gap anywhere stops the whole wave, so the success rate falls off before the theoretical limit.
Theory (reaches if gap < height (d − t < h)): 0.6: 1 · 0.8: 1 · 1: 1 · 1.05: 1 · 1.1: 1 · 1.15: 1 · 1.2: 0 · 1.25: 0 · 1.3: 0
Result from a test run: 0.6: 1 ± 0 · 0.8: 1 ± 0 · 1: 1 ± 0 · 1.05: 1 ± 0 · 1.1: 0.75 ± 0.164 · 1.15: 0 ± 0 · 1.2: 0 ± 0 · 1.25: 0 ± 0 · 1.3: 0 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: How fast can an amplifier grow?

The bigger domino is k³ times heavier. With bouncy real dominoes Whitehead reached k = 1.5; here, collisions are not springy and less of the falling domino's momentum is passed on, so the chain fails above about k = 1.4.
Result from a test run: 1.1: 10 ± 0 · 1.2: 10 ± 0 · 1.3: 10 ± 0 · 1.35: 10 ± 0 · 1.4: 10 ± 0 · 1.5: 2 ± 0 · 1.6: 1 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)
