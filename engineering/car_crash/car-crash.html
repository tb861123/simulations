How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Car body: one solid mass. Its crumple zone pushes back with a force that rises over the first 3% of the zone and then stays at the crush force F. Once the zone is used up the car hits its solid passenger cell (very stiff: 30 MN per metre). Crushed metal stays crushed: only a little springs back, so cars bounce very little.
  • Crush distance theory: the crumple zone does work F × d, which equals the car's kinetic energy ½mv², so d ≈ ½mv² / F (+ a small amount for the force build-up). Doubling speed quadruples d. The average deceleration of the car is v² / 2d.
  • Other vehicles: both fronts crush, with the crush happening in the weaker one first. A 20 kg bumper beam joins the two crumple zones. Momentum is shared: if they stay together, vfinal = (m1v1 + m2v2) / (m1+m2).
  • Occupant: a torso (body mass − 4.5 kg) and a 4.5 kg head joined by a springy neck. The head can swing up to about 25 cm ahead of the chest.
  • Seatbelt: a stretchy strap on the torso. Stretch % = how much the belt and chest give under a 10 kN load per metre of belt. Slack must be taken up before it pulls. A pretensioner removes the slack 10–15 ms after impact. The lap belt and the shoulder belt each carry half the load; a load limiter lets the shoulder belt pay out above 4 kN so the force on the ribs cannot go higher.
  • Airbag: fires at the chosen time and fills over 30 ms to 35 cm deep. It pushes on the head and chest like a soft, vented cushion (limited to about 2 kN on the head). A late airbag is still inflating when the occupant arrives — it punches them.
  • Hard contacts: without a restraint (or if the airbag is used up) the chest hits the steering wheel rim and the head hits the wheel or windscreen — stiff, short contacts that give huge decelerations.
  • Head Injury Criterion (HIC15): from the head's deceleration a (in g), find the time window t1–t2 no longer than 15 ms that makes (t2−t1) × [average a]2.5 biggest. Risk of serious head injury (AIS 3+) uses the US safety regulator's curve: P = Φ((ln HIC − 7.45)/0.74). HIC 1000 ≈ 1 in 6 chance of serious injury; legal limit 700 (Euro NCAP aims below 650).
  • Not modelled: the car rotating or pitching, offset or side impacts, the engine and wheels, steel strengthening with speed, the occupant's legs, neck injury, the dashboard collapsing into the cabin, glass breaking, and the person being thrown out of the car. Real dummies have dozens of parts; this one has two.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • 1D: two objects on a frictionless track (like gliders on an air track). Momentum is conserved and Newton's law of restitution sets the speed of separation: v₂ − v₁ = e(u₁ − u₂). These two equations give v₁ = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ + m₂e(u₂ − u₁))/(m₁ + m₂) and v₂ = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ + m₁e(u₁ − u₂))/(m₁ + m₂).
  • The contact: the push between the objects is a smooth half-sine pulse lasting Δt. Its area (the impulse) is fixed by the momentum change, so the peak force is π/2 × J/Δt and the average force is J/Δt. The sizes of the blocks on screen are not to scale and the squash is exaggerated.
  • Explosion: the objects start together and an energy E is turned into kinetic energy; momentum stays the same, so they fly apart with v₁ = u − √(2Em₂/(m₁(m₁+m₂))) and v₂ = u + √(2Em₁/(m₂(m₁+m₂))).
  • 2D: smooth spheres of equal size. The impulse acts along the line joining the centres at the moment of contact; the speeds along the tangent do not change. Positions on the picture are not to scale; speeds and angles are.
  • Newton's cradle: 1 m strings, steel balls (6 cm radius). Each ball is a pendulum solved in 0.5 ms steps; collisions are handled one pair at a time, very quickly, which is how a real cradle with tiny gaps behaves. Real cradles also lose energy to sound and to the balls squashing, and the impulse spreads through the touching balls as a wave.
  • Left out: friction, air resistance, spin and rolling (snooker balls also roll and slide), and the objects deforming permanently.
  • Batch runs: a model air-track practical. Each speed is measured by a light gate with a random error (normal, SD as a % of the speed); angles in 2D are measured with a protractor (SD in degrees).

📐 Physics notes

Spec reference: Component 1, Topic 2 (Kinematics and Dynamics): momentum and Newton's laws.

Key equations:

p = mv
J = Δp = FΔt (impulse–momentum theorem)
m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ = m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ (conservation of momentum)
e = vsep / vapproach = (v₂ − v₁)/(u₁ − u₂)
ΔKElost = ½μ(urel)²(1 − e²), μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂)
v₁ = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ + m₂e(u₂ − u₁))/(m₁ + m₂)
v₂ = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂ + m₁e(u₁ − u₂))/(m₁ + m₂)

Conservation of momentum: the total momentum of an isolated system is constant. In every collision (elastic, inelastic or explosion) the total momentum before equals the total momentum after, provided no external force acts.

Coefficient of restitution (e) measures the 'bounciness' of a collision. e = 1 means elastic (KE conserved). e = 0 means perfectly inelastic (the objects stick). For real collisions 0 < e < 1. The definition e = vsep/vapproach means: how fast the objects separate compared with how fast they approached.

Impulse–momentum theorem: the impulse J = FΔt equals the change in momentum Δp. A longer collision time means a smaller force for the same impulse. This is why airbags, helmets and crumple zones reduce injury: they increase Δt. (Switch to Car crash to see this in a real crash.)

Elastic collision: KE is conserved. For equal masses, the first ball stops and the second moves on with the original speed (Newton's cradle).

Perfectly inelastic: the objects stick together. The most KE possible is lost, but momentum is still conserved: vf = (m₁u₁ + m₂u₂)/(m₁ + m₂).

Advanced context. Reduced mass: for a two-body collision the reduced mass is μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂). The KE lost in a general collision is ΔKE = ½μurel²(1 − e²), where urel = u₁ − u₂. This neatly separates the role of the masses from the coefficient of restitution.

Centre-of-mass (CoM) frame: in the CoM frame the total momentum is zero. Each particle simply reverses its velocity in an elastic collision (and both stop dead in a perfectly inelastic one). The CoM frame makes 2D collisions much simpler.

Derivation of the e formula: Newton's definition uses the component of relative velocity along the line of centres: e = −(v₁ − v₂)/(u₁ − u₂). Together with conservation of momentum this fixes both velocities after the collision.

Why perfectly inelastic loses the most energy: for given starting conditions the KE lost is ½μurel²(1 − e²), which is largest when e = 0. Sticking together always loses the most kinetic energy while still conserving momentum. An explosion (e effectively negative, the objects pushed apart) increases the total KE: energy comes from an internal store (for example chemical energy in an explosive).
