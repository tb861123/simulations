Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Why crumple?

What to look for: The car has the same momentum and kinetic energy to lose either way. Crushing over 65 cm instead of 5 cm makes the stop take about ten times longer and go ten times further, so the force (Δp ÷ Δt, or KE ÷ distance) is about ten times smaller. The front is designed to be destroyed so the people are not.

Investigation 2: No seatbelt

What to look for: The car stops in about 0.1 s but the dummy carries on at 30 mph until it hits the steering wheel and windscreen, which stop it in a few centimetres: hundreds of g. The seatbelt starts slowing the occupant early and spreads the stop over the whole crumple time.

Investigation 3: Seatbelt and airbag

What to look for: A belt holds the torso but the head flings forward on the neck and can hit the wheel. The airbag catches the head over a longer distance, so the head injury criterion (HIC) falls. In this model the belt alone just stops the head reaching the wheel at 30 mph; at higher speeds or with slack it does not, and the airbag matters far more.

Investigation 4: A loose belt

What to look for: Until the slack is taken up the occupant keeps moving while the car slows, so they hit the belt late and fast, with less distance left to stop in: a higher peak force. Pretensioners pull the belt tight in the first few milliseconds for exactly this reason.

Investigation 5: Speed kills

What to look for: Kinetic energy goes as v², so 40 mph has 1.8 times the energy of 30 mph, and 20 mph less than half. The crumple zone crushes about v² further; once it is used up, the stiff passenger cell takes the rest and the forces jump. This is why 20 mph zones save lives.

Investigation 6: Small car meets SUV

What to look for: Momentum is shared: the pair ends up moving in the SUV's direction, so the small car's speed changes by much more (Δv = closing speed × m_SUV ÷ (m_small + m_SUV)). Same force on both (Newton's third law), but the small car has less mass, so more deceleration.

Investigation 7: Collisions lab: momentum is conserved

What to look for: Momentum is conserved in every collision: 6 kg m/s before and after. Kinetic energy is conserved only in an elastic collision; when they stick, a third of the KE (here) becomes heat, sound and deformation.

Investigation 8: Explosions A level

What to look for: Total momentum was zero and stays zero: equal and opposite momenta. Kinetic energy = p² ÷ 2m, so the lighter trolley gets more of the energy. That is why a gun recoils slowly but the bullet flies fast.

Batch experiment 1: Crumple length and deceleration

Double the crumple length and the deceleration halves: the same speed is lost over twice the distance and time.
Theory (v² ÷ 2d): 10: 147 · 20: 73.4 · 30: 48.9 · 45: 32.6 · 60: 24.5 · 75: 19.6 · 90: 16.3
Result from a test run: 10: 136 ± 0.158 · 20: 70.4 ± 0.0473 · 30: 47.6 ± 0.00746 · 45: 32.1 ± 0.00776 · 60: 24.1 ± 0.00761 · 75: 19.4 ± 0.00426 · 90: 16.2 ± 0.00416 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Crush distance grows as v²: twice the speed, four times the crush. At high speed the zone runs out and the passenger cell takes the rest.

Against a lorry, the car's speed changes by nearly 60 mph: the full closing speed. Against a lighter car it changes by less than 30.

Batch experiment 6: Kinetic energy kept in a collision

Momentum is always kept; kinetic energy only when e = 1. The loss is ½ μ u² (1 − e²), with μ the reduced mass.
