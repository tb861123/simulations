Bridge Builder · Teacher notesbridge-builder.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Squares wobble, triangles don't

What to look for: A square of pinned members can fold flat like a parallelogram without any member changing length: it is a mechanism. A triangle cannot change shape unless a member stretches or squashes, so trusses are built from triangles. The page warns 'Wobbly!' when your structure is a mechanism.

Investigation 2: First crossing

What to look for: A timber Warren truss (zig-zag) about 3 m deep does it for about £5,700. Keep timber members short where they are in compression (red): long thin members buckle.

Investigation 3: Blue or red?

What to look for: Like a beam, a truss bends: the top chord is squashed (red) and the bottom chord (the road) is stretched (blue). In a Pratt truss the diagonals slope down towards the middle and are in tension; in a Howe truss they slope the other way and are in compression. Engineers put steel where there is compression and cheaper cables or rods where there is tension.

Investigation 4: Lorry route

What to look for: Timber is too weak for the lorry. A steel Pratt or Howe truss about 3 m deep costs about £23,000. Hover over members to see the force in each and how close it is to breaking.

Investigation 5: How deep should a truss be?

What to look for: Deeper trusses have smaller chord forces (force ≈ bending moment ÷ depth), so at first deeper is stronger. But the vertical members get longer, and long members in compression buckle (Euler: buckling load ∝ 1/L²). There is a best depth, about 5 m here. The Batch run maps it.

Investigation 6: Timber or steel?

What to look for: A squashed (red) top-chord member buckles first: often before the lorry even gets there, under the bridge's own weight. Timber is reasonably strong in tension, but a long timber strut buckles at a low load because timber is not very stiff (small E). Then the forces flow round the gap, the next member is overloaded, and the bridge collapses in a chain.

Investigation 7: Arch over the gorge

What to look for: An arch below the deck pushes outwards and downwards on the cliffs, which are strong. Almost every arch member is in compression (red), and the posts hold the road up. The Example 'Arch below' about 8 m deep costs about £41,000.

Investigation 8: Cables can only pull

What to look for: Cables are strong and cheap for their strength, but only in tension: push on one and it goes slack (shown dashed). A fan of cables from each tower (cable-stayed, like the Queensferry Crossing) carries the deck. The road itself is squeezed (red) by the cables pulling inwards.

Batch experiment 1: Breaking load vs depth

Beam theory treats the truss as a beam: the bending moment at the middle (PL/4) is carried by the two chords, a distance h apart, so the chord force is PL/(4h). That predicts a straight line. It works until about 5 m; deeper than that the long vertical members buckle first (buckling load ∝ 1/length²) and the truss gets weaker.
Theory (beam theory: top chord fails (P = 4hC/L)): 1: 183 · 2: 367 · 3: 550 · 4: 733 · 5: 917 · 6: 1100 · 7: 1280 · 8: 1470 · 10: 1830
Result from a test run: 1: 179 ± 3.89 · 2: 365 ± 7.41 · 3: 528 ± 8.41 · 4: 691 ± 5.61 · 5: 746 ± 13 · 6: 613 ± 14.2 · 7: 505 ± 8.38 · 8: 425 ± 5.17 · 10: 306 ± 5.32 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Breaking load vs number of panels

With few panels the top-chord members are long and buckle at low loads (π²EI/L²). Shorter panels raise the buckling load until it is more than the steel's crushing strength σA; after that, more panels add cost but no strength.
Theory (beam theory with Euler buckling of the top chord): 4: 274 · 6: 550 · 8: 550 · 10: 550 · 12: 550
Result from a test run: 4: 266 ± 4.84 · 6: 543 ± 9.14 · 8: 532 ± 9.35 · 10: 526 ± 11.9 · 12: 549 ± 9.36 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Which shape gives most for the money?

The cable-stayed design wins on this measure because cables use steel in tension, where none of it is wasted resisting buckling, and the towers (free here) do a lot of work. Real towers and anchorages are expensive, which is why short bridges are trusses or arches.
Result from a test run: 1: 28.2 ± 0.669 · 2: 28.8 ± 0.484 · 3: 32.8 ± 0.084 · 4: 11.4 ± 0.0405 · 5: 50.8 ± 1.37 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: The weakest link

A truss fails when its first member fails. Here the two middle top-chord members carry the same, biggest force, so the bridge breaks at whichever of the two is weaker: the breaking load is usually a little below the design value, and the mean sits to the left of the theory line. More equally loaded members, more chances of a weak one: this is one reason engineers apply safety factors.
Theory (every member exactly as designed): 550
Result from a test run: mean 537, SD 22.5, SE 1.59 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 5: Sag vs load

Sag is proportional to load (Hooke's law for the whole bridge), so the points lie on a straight line through the origin. Beam theory only counts the stretching of the two chords; the real truss sags more because the diagonals and verticals stretch too.
Theory (beam theory, δ = PL³/48EI (chords only)): 5: 3.27 · 10: 6.54 · 20: 13.1 · 30: 19.6 · 40: 26.2
Result from a test run: 5: 4.24 ± 0.0131 · 10: 8.49 ± 0.0271 · 20: 17.1 ± 0.0385 · 30: 25.6 ± 0.111 · 40: 33.9 ± 0.0733 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)
