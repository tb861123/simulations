Traffic Flow and Phantom Jams · Teacher notestraffic-flow.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: A jam from nothing

What to look for: Small differences grow: one driver eases off for a moment, the next has to brake a little harder, the next harder still, until someone stops. A 'phantom' jam has formed with no cause that anyone could point to. In the real experiment (22 small cars, driven closer together than this model's drivers allow) it took a few minutes; here it varies each time you restart.

Investigation 2: Which way does the jam move?

What to look for: Cars move forwards through the jam, but the jam itself moves backwards, at 10 to 15 km/h here (15 to 20 on real motorways): cars join at the back faster than they leave at the front. On the space–time diagram each red stripe slopes backwards. This speed is nearly the same on every motorway in the world, because it is set by how long drivers take to pull away.

Investigation 3: How many cars is too many?

What to look for: At low density everyone drives at the limit, so more cars means more flow. Near 25 to 30 vehicles per km per lane the flow peaks at around 2000 vehicles an hour. Add more and jams form: speed collapses and the flow FALLS, so more cars on the road means fewer get through. The road's capacity is a real limit.

Investigation 4: Tap the brakes

What to look for: At this density one sharp brake is enough: the slow-down is passed back and amplified, and a jam is born that outlives its cause by many minutes. Try the same thing with 15 fewer cars: the disturbance dies away. Whether a disturbance grows or fades depends on the density, which is why jams appear at busy times.

Investigation 5: Slow down to speed up

What to look for: At a lower limit drivers leave gaps that are large for their speed and need to brake less sharply, so small slow-downs fade instead of growing. The traffic moves steadily, and steady traffic at 50 can carry a little more than stop-and-go traffic. In this model the gain is modest (see the batch run) and it only appears on a busy road: on a quiet one a lower limit just slows everyone down. This is the idea behind variable speed limits on 'smart' motorways.

Investigation 6: Roadworks

What to look for: Three lanes of traffic must fit through two, so the closure can pass at most about two thirds of what the open road can. If more than that arrives, a queue grows back from the merge. Merging late and taking turns ('zip merging') uses the closing lane's full length and keeps the queue shorter than merging early.

Investigation 7: A few robots

What to look for: The self-driving cars here never lose attention, keep a slightly longer gap and brake gently and early, so they soak up a slow-down instead of amplifying it. A modest share is enough to calm the whole stream: experiments on real roads (Arizona 2017, Nashville 2022) found that one specially driven car among about twenty could smooth a jam and cut fuel use.

Investigation 8: Lorries

What to look for: Lorries are long, slower and slow to accelerate. Cars bunch behind them and pull out to overtake, and every lane change makes the driver behind in the new lane brake. The road carries fewer vehicles per hour, and jams start more easily. This is why some motorways ban lorries from overtaking at busy times.

Batch experiment 1: The fundamental diagram

Up to the peak the runs agree closely: everyone drives at the limit. Beyond it the traffic is unstable, and whether and when a jam forms depends on chance, so the flow varies from run to run.

Batch experiment 3: How fast does a jam travel?

Jams on real motorways travel backwards at 15 to 20 km/h almost everywhere. The speed is set by how quickly drivers pull away from the front of the queue, not by how fast the traffic was going.
Result from a test run: mean -11.9, SD 1.03, SE 0.138 from 56 runs

Batch experiment 4: Does a lower limit help?

On an empty road a lower limit always means a lower flow. On a busy road the highest flow comes at an intermediate limit, which keeps the traffic out of the stop-and-go state.
Result from a test run: 30: 1440 ± 4.61 · 40: 1710 ± 3.16 · 50: 1810 ± 22.8 · 60: 1800 ± 66.3 · 70: 1680 ± 58.2 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)
