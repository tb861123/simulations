2 · Fundamental diagram with dawdling

How does random dawdling (p = 0.25) change the flow–density curve? Dawdling lowers the peak and moves it to lower density. The mean-field estimate pictures cars scattered at random. In light traffic the real cars do better than that (they settle into evenly spaced streams), but in heavy traffic they do worse, because they clump into jams that are slow to clear. So the data cross the mean-field curve.

3 · Journey times at one density

At 25 vehicles/km, how much do lap times vary from run to run? Every run uses the same density but a different random start and different random dawdles, so jams appear in different places and times. The mean-field estimate assumes randomly scattered cars, so it misses by about 10%. Notice how small the run-to-run spread is: on a 3 km ring over 15 minutes, the randomness averages out.

4 · Dawdling and capacity

How does dawdling reduce the most traffic a lane can carry? Capacity falls steeply as p rises: at p = 0 it is 4/5 × 3600 = 2,880 vehicles/hour per lane; real motorway lanes carry about 2,000. (The simulation gets about 2,820 at p = 0 because the densities tried miss the exact critical density.) The mean-field estimate is a poor guide here: it barely changes with p, because it pictures cars scattered at random and misses both the neat streams that form with little dawdling and the jams that form with a lot.

5 · How fast do jams travel backwards?

Does the jam wave move back at −27(1 − p) km/h? Each run tracks the queues of stopped cars and measures how fast they drift backwards. The theory says one car leaves the front of the jam every 1/(1 − p) seconds. Real jams travel back at about 15–20 km/h, which this model matches roughly. The measured jams move back a little slower than the simple theory, because drivers leaving the front sometimes dawdle and stop again, rejoining the jam.

6 · Do variable speed limits help?

At a lane closure, do variable speed limits shorten journeys or make them more reliable? Each pair of runs uses the same random arrivals with and without variable limits. Look at the mean journey time and its spread. In this simple model the gain is small or even negative: the signs slow everyone down, and the main benefit in reality (fewer late, hard lane changes and crashes) is not modelled.
