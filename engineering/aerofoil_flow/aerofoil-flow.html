What's happening

Aerodynamics

Graphs

Potential flow has no viscosity. So it has no stall: the lift keeps rising in a straight line however steep the wing. Real wings stall at about 12–16°, when the air breaks away from the top surface and the lift collapses. It also has no drag at all in steady flow, on any shape (d'Alembert's paradox, 1752). Real objects feel friction drag and pressure drag.
How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Potential flow: inviscid (no viscosity), incompressible (fine below about 100 m/s, a third of the speed of sound), irrotational, steady and two-dimensional.
  • 2D means an infinitely long wing. Real wings have tips: air leaks round them, making tip vortices and induced drag, which lower the lift.
  • Panels: the surface is cut into N straight panels. Each has a constant source strength σ (N unknowns); the body has one vortex strength γ shared by all its panels (1 unknown). Each panel's effect is integrated exactly along its length (not treated as a single point), except that panels more than 6 panel-lengths away from a point in the colour map are lumped as points (error under 0.1%).
  • Equations: no flow through the middle of any panel (V·n = 0), plus the Kutta condition: equal speeds just above and below the trailing edge, so the air leaves the sharp tail smoothly. Without it the answer is not unique and the lift could be anything.
  • Pressure from Bernoulli: Cp = (p − p∞)/(½ρV∞²) = 1 − (V/V∞)². Cp = 1 at a stagnation point; negative Cp is suction.
  • Lift from Kutta–Joukowski: L′ = ρV∞Γ per metre of span, with Γ = γ × perimeter, so CL = 2Γ/(V∞c). The tiles also add up the pressure on every panel ("CL from pressures") as an independent check.
  • Thin-aerofoil theory: CL = 2π(α − α₀), α in radians. The zero-lift angle α₀ comes from the camber line. In potential flow, thickness adds roughly 1 + 0.77t/c to the slope; a real boundary layer takes most of that back.
  • Angle of attack tilts the body about its quarter-chord point; the wind always blows from the left (as in the Python app).
  • Cylinder and square have no sharp trailing edge, so the Kutta condition cannot fix their circulation: you set it with the spin slider (the Python app used a Kutta condition at the back, which gives zero spin). Their reference length is the diameter (or side).
  • Boundary layer (friction drag, transition T, separation S): an integral method run along the surface speeds of the potential flow: Thwaites' method for the laminar layer, Michel's rule for transition, Head's method for the turbulent layer. Re = V∞c/ν. Laminar separation when the shape factor H > 3.5; turbulent when the skin friction reaches zero. After separation the friction is not counted and the model is no longer trustworthy.
  • Viscous coupling (optional): the solver blows air out of the surface at d(Ue·δ*)/ds so the flow sees a body thickened by the displacement thickness δ* (drawn 5× exaggerated), under-relaxed and iterated up to 5 times, as XFOIL does (much simplified). It lowers the lift slightly.
  • Vortex shedding: point vortices are released from the trailing edge so that the total circulation stays the same (Kelvin's theorem): a change ΔΓ on the wing sheds an equal and opposite swirl into the wake. Each vortex has a smoothed (Lamb–Oseen) core that grows with age. The wake is fed back into the panel equations every step, which gives the slow build-up of lift after a sudden start (the Wagner effect). Behind a cylinder or square, vortex pairs are released alternately from the points where the pressure rises fastest, at about the Strouhal frequency, to show a Kármán street: that part is a picture, not a calculation of separation.
  • Pitch oscillation: α = α̅ + A sin(2πft). The reduced frequency k = πfc/V∞ says how unsteady it is. The simulation is quasi-steady (it re-solves the steady flow at each instant, so its CL–α "loop" is a straight line). Theodorsen's theory (1935) for a thin plate pitching about its quarter chord, including the wake and the "added mass" of air, gives an ellipse.
  • Animation: the flow pattern is solved for the real speed, but the particles are slowed down so you can follow them (the factor is shown above the view).
  • Not modelled: stall, turbulence in the flow itself, compressibility and shock waves, 3D effects, buoyancy, heating.
