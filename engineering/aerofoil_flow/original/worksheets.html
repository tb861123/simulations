The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Is CL = 2πα?

What this shows. The lift rises in a straight line with α, as thin-aerofoil theory says. The panel method is about 12% above 2πα (yellow) because in potential flow a 12% thick aerofoil lifts more than a flat plate: the grey dashed line 2π(1 + 0.77t)α allows for that and fits within about 3%. Real wings come out slightly BELOW 2πα, because the boundary layer at the tail weakens the circulation, and they stall at about 12–16°. The model has no viscosity, so it never stalls: the line just keeps going.

2 · What does camber add?

What this shows. A cambered (curved) aerofoil makes lift even at α = 0: the whole line is shifted up. It only stops lifting at the zero-lift angle α₀ = −4.2° for a 4412 (thin-aerofoil theory, from the shape of the camber line). The slope is the same as for the symmetric aerofoil: camber moves the line, angle moves you along it. The points lie slightly above the yellow line because of the thickness (grey dashed).

3 · How many panels are enough?

What this shows. With 10 to 80 straight panels the aerofoil is a rough polygon (especially round its nose) and CL wobbles a few per cent either side of the converged value (yellow, 800 panels). Then it settles: within 1% at 160 panels and 0.2% at 320. That is how you check any numerical model: refine it until the answer stops changing. More panels cost time, because solving N + 1 simultaneous equations takes about N³ steps: 640 panels is 64 times slower than 160.

4 · Does thickness change the lift?

What this shows. Thin-aerofoil theory ignores thickness, so it predicts the same CL = 2πα = 0.548 for every thickness (yellow). In potential flow a thicker aerofoil actually lifts MORE, close to the 2πα(1 + 0.77t) rule (grey dashed). In real air this extra lift is mostly lost: thick sections have thicker boundary layers, and very thick ones separate and stall earlier. Thin sections, on the other hand, have a sharper suction peak at the nose and stall suddenly.

5 · Gusty wind: how much does the lift vary?

What this shows. Each run meets a random gust that changes the angle of attack (normal distribution, SD 1°). Because CL rises in a straight line with α, the lift has the same bell-shaped spread: SD = slope × 1° ≈ 2π(1 + 0.77t) × 0.0175 ≈ 0.12, and the mean sits on the value for 5°. The yellow line is thin-aerofoil theory, which is about 12% low for a 12% thick section (see the first experiment). The scatter is caused by the gusts, not by the solver, which gives exactly the same answer every time for the same angle.

6 · Magnus effect: does spin make lift?

What this shows. The lift here is found by adding up the pressure on every panel, completely separately from the circulation. It agrees with Kutta–Joukowski, CL = 2Γ/(V∞D) = 4π × spin, to better than 1%: lift is proportional to circulation. Backspin (positive) lifts, topspin pushes down: that is why a football or tennis ball curves. (Real spinning cylinders give less than this, because the boundary layer separates.)

7 · d'Alembert's paradox: is there any drag?

What this shows. Adding up the pressure in the direction of the wind gives almost exactly zero drag at every angle (the tiny values come from the straight panels, and shrink with more panels). In steady potential flow NO shape has drag: d'Alembert's paradox (1752). The paradox is solved by viscosity: real air sticks to the surface (friction drag) and breaks away behind the body (pressure drag), which potential flow cannot do.

8 · Friction drag against Reynolds number

What this shows. The boundary-layer model (from the Python app) adds friction drag, which potential flow alone cannot give. At low Reynolds number Re = V∞c/ν the layer stays laminar and Cd falls like 1/√Re, about 5% below the laminar flat-plate (Blasius) line. From about Re = 4 × 10⁵ the layer turns turbulent part of the way along (transition, marked T on the view): the friction stops falling and stays near 0.005, between the laminar and turbulent flat-plate lines, because the front of the wing is still laminar. Real thin aerofoils give about the same. The exact transition point depends on surface roughness and on how turbulent the oncoming air is.
