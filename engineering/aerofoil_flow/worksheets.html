Aerofoil Flow · Teacher notesaerofoil-flow.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: No angle, no lift

What to look for: The flow above and below is a mirror image: the air speeds up equally over both surfaces, so the pressures balance and there is no lift.

Investigation 2: Tilt it

What to look for: The air speeds up most over the top, near the front (red on the speed map); there the pressure is lowest. Underneath it is slower, so the pressure is higher. Higher pressure below, lower above: lift upwards.

Investigation 3: Faster, more lift

What to look for: Lift = ½ρv²cC_L: twice the speed gives four times the lift (C_L stays the same). That is why aircraft need long runways: they must reach a high speed before the lift beats their weight.

Investigation 4: Camber

What to look for: Yes: a curved (cambered) aerofoil turns the air downwards even at 0°, so it makes lift; it has to be tilted slightly nose-down (about −4°) to make none. Most wings are cambered.

Investigation 5: Lift against angle

What to look for: In this model C_L rises in a straight line, about 0.11 per degree (2π per radian). Real wings stall at about 12–16°: the flow separates from the upper surface and lift falls suddenly. Potential flow cannot show that (no viscosity), so the dashed 'real wing' curve is a sketch.

Investigation 6: Spinning ball (Magnus effect)

What to look for: With no spin the flow is symmetric: no lift (and, in this ideal flow, no drag either: d'Alembert's paradox). Spin drags air round with it, so the air is faster on one side and slower on the other: a sideways force. That is how footballs and tennis balls curve.

Investigation 7: Bernoulli and Cp A level

What to look for: Cp ≈ −2 near the leading edge: (v/V∞)² = 3, v ≈ 1.7 × 30 = 52 m/s. Pressure drop = −Cp × ½ρV∞² ≈ 2 × 0.5 × 1.225 × 900 ≈ 1100 Pa below atmospheric.

Investigation 8: Thin-aerofoil theory A level

What to look for: Both have a slope near 2π per radian (0.11 per degree). Thicker aerofoils give a slightly steeper slope in potential flow (real thick wings lose some of it to viscosity). Camber shifts the line left by the zero-lift angle α₀.

Batch experiment 5: Spinning cylinder (Magnus effect)

Lift is proportional to the spin (Kutta–Joukowski: L = ρVΓ). Measured here by adding up the surface pressures, it matches 4πs.
Theory (4πs): 0: 0 · 0.2: 2.51 · 0.4: 5.03 · 0.6: 7.54 · 0.8: 10.1 · 1: 12.6
Result from a test run: 0: 5.53e-16 ± 0 · 0.2: 2.5 ± 0 · 0.4: 4.99 ± 0 · 0.6: 7.49 ± 0 · 0.8: 9.99 ± 0 · 1: 12.5 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: Gusts: how much does the lift vary?

C_L varies by about ±0.18: gusts make the lift jump up and down, which passengers feel as turbulence.
Theory (C_L at 5°): 0.606
Result from a test run: mean 0.607, SD 0.178, SE 0.0126 from 200 runs
