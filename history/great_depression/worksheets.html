The Great Depression · Teacher notesgreat-depression.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: From crash to depression

What to look for: The crash causes a recession, but the steepest fall comes in 1931–33, when waves of bank runs destroy deposits and the money supply shrinks by about a third. In reality about 9,000 of 25,000 US banks failed (in the model about half). Output fell by a quarter and unemployment reached about 25%. The model follows the real figures (dots) roughly.

Investigation 2: What if the Fed had stopped the runs?

What to look for: With no bank runs, money barely falls, and the depression is a sharp recession rather than a catastrophe. This is Friedman and Schwartz's argument (1963): the Federal Reserve turned a recession into the Great Depression by letting the banks fail. Ben Bernanke, later chair of the Fed, agreed in 2002: 'we did it. We're very sorry.'

Investigation 3: The golden fetters

What to look for: Leaving gold lets the central bank create money, so spending and prices recover. Britain left in September 1931 and recovered from 1932; the USA left in 1933 and recovered from then; France stayed on until 1936 and stayed depressed longest. Eichengreen called the gold standard the 'golden fetters'.

Investigation 4: The 1937 recession

What to look for: In 1937 Roosevelt cut spending to balance the budget and the Fed doubled banks' reserve requirements: money and spending fell, and unemployment jumped from about 14% to 19%. It suggests the New Deal recovery depended on the stimulus continuing.

Investigation 5: Germany: Brüning's austerity

What to look for: Brüning cut spending and wages by decree to balance the budget and keep the mark on gold (and to show Germany could not pay reparations). With foreign loans recalled and banks failing in 1931, unemployment rose to about 6 million. Without the cuts, unemployment peaks lower and the estimated Nazi vote is smaller, though Brüning's defenders (Borchardt) argue he had no real choice.

Investigation 6: Hitler's recovery A level

What to look for: Large deficit spending (public works, then rearmament) and exchange controls (off gold in practice) push spending up, so unemployment falls quickly (helped by removing women and Jews from the figures, and conscription, which the model leaves out). It was not a miracle: any government spending that much would cut unemployment; the question is what it was spent on, and the bill (Mefo bills, the 1936 crisis, the drive to war).

Batch experiment 1: Leave gold sooner?

The earlier the country leaves gold, the sooner money stops falling and the shallower the depression. In the model, leaving in 1930 raises output in 1932–34 from about 78 to 86 (1929 = 100). Leaving gold could not stop the bank runs on its own, which is why even the 1930 point is well below 100.
Theory (expected): 1930: 85.9 · 1931: 83.1 · 1931.75: 81 · 1932.5: 79.3 · 1933.25: 78 · 1934: 77.4 · 1936: 77.2
Result from a test run: 1930: 84.1 ± 1.93 · 1931: 84.4 ± 1.35 · 1931.75: 81.1 ± 2.06 · 1932.5: 80 ± 1.42 · 1933.25: 79 ± 1.71 · 1934: 75.9 ± 1.71 · 1936: 78.8 ± 0.815 (mean ± SE, 12 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Stop the bank runs in 1930?

Most failures happened in panics that fed on themselves. Stopping the runs early saves most of the banks, which is the core of Friedman and Schwartz's case against the Federal Reserve.
Theory (expected): 0: 49.7 · 1: 9.21
Result from a test run: 0: 53.2 ± 2.19 · 1: 8.93 ± 0.7 (mean ± SE, 15 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Austerity or spending in Germany?

Cuts deepen the slump; spending softens it. Whether Brüning could have spent more is the 'Borchardt debate': with no foreign lenders, a Reichsbank bound by the Young Plan and memories of the 1923 hyperinflation, his room to borrow was small.
Theory (expected): -1: 6.26 · -0.5: 5.96 · 0: 5.66 · 0.5: 5.36 · 1: 5.06 · 1.5: 4.76
Result from a test run: -1: 6.18 ± 0.25 · -0.5: 5.8 ± 0.136 · 0: 5.57 ± 0.171 · 0.5: 5.47 ± 0.245 · 1: 5.24 ± 0.175 · 1.5: 4.73 ± 0.163 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Unemployment and the Nazi vote

The fit is rough, and a correlation is not proof that unemployment caused the Nazi vote. In fact the unemployed themselves tended to vote Communist; the Nazis did best among the Protestant middle classes and farmers who feared ruin. Historians use such figures with care.
Theory (actual July 1932): 37.3
Result from a test run: mean 38.2, SD 3.98, SE 0.281 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 5: How much was bad luck?

Bank panics are partly a matter of chance: a few early failures can spread or not. But almost every run gives a deep depression: with the policies of 1929–33, a severe slump was very likely.
Theory (real 1933): 73.6
Result from a test run: mean 73.7, SD 5.68, SE 0.402 from 200 runs
