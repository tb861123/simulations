Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The riot that nearly wasn't

What to look for: With thresholds 0, 1, 2, … each new rioter tips the next: all 100 riot. Change one person and the chain breaks at once: only the instigator riots. The two crowds are almost identical, yet newspapers would describe one as 'a mob of radicals' and the other as 'a lone troublemaker'. Small differences in a few people can decide big events.

Investigation 2: Same mood, different outcomes

What to look for: About half the time the riot fizzles at a few people; otherwise almost everyone joins. The average mood is identical: what differs is whether there happen to be enough low-threshold people to carry the chain over the gap. This is why revolutions are so hard to predict, even by the people taking part.

Investigation 3: Grievance and legitimacy

What to look for: It comes in sudden bursts ('punctuated equilibrium'): a local patch with few police tips into open rebellion, it spreads until the police arrest enough people, then quiet returns while prisoners are in jail. The regime looks stable most of the time.

Investigation 4: 1905: Bloody Sunday

What to look for: The loss of legitimacy multiplies everyone's grievance, so a wave of rebellion follows (strikes, mutiny on the Potemkin, peasant risings). Concessions (the October Manifesto: a Duma, civil rights) restore some legitimacy; repression (more police, field courts, 'Stolypin's neckties') raises the risk of arrest. Both reduce open rebellion, but repression does not reduce grievance: it is still there, hidden.

Investigation 5: 1917: war and mutiny

What to look for: In 1905 the army stayed loyal (once the war with Japan ended) and repressed the revolution. In February 1917 the war had raised hardship everywhere (bread queues, inflation) and destroyed legitimacy (defeats, Rasputin), and the Petrograd garrison mutinied: with no one to arrest them, rebels have nothing to fear and the rebellion becomes general. The Tsar abdicated within days.

Investigation 6: Hidden grievance A level

What to look for: Heavy policing keeps open rebellion near zero while grievance stays high: preference falsification (Timur Kuran). Observers, and the regime itself, cannot see how much opposition there is, so when repression weakens, the explosion is a surprise. This helps explain why 1917 (and 1989 in eastern Europe) shocked contemporaries.

Investigation 7: Slow decline or sudden shock? A level

What to look for: A sudden fall gives a far larger peak: everyone's grievance jumps together, so many people cross the threshold at once and overwhelm the police. A gradual fall lets the police pick off the few who rebel at each stage. Epstein found the same: a regime that loses legitimacy slowly may never face a revolution, one that loses it suddenly (Bloody Sunday, a lost war) is in danger.

Batch experiment 1: Spread of opinions and the size of the riot

With little variety, nobody is near the instigators, so the riot fizzles. With more variety there are enough low-threshold people to build a chain, and the crowd tips. Single crowds scatter widely near the tipping point: the mean hides that each crowd is either small or large.
Theory (very large crowd): 2: 2 · 5: 2 · 8: 2.24 · 10: 3.55 · 12: 100 · 15: 100 · 20: 100 · 25: 99.9 · 30: 99.4
Result from a test run: 2: 2 ± 0 · 5: 2 ± 0 · 8: 2.3 ± 0.109 · 10: 13.1 ± 5.39 · 12: 52.1 ± 8.9 · 15: 96.8 ± 3.17 · 20: 100 ± 0.0333 · 25: 99.9 ± 0.0557 · 30: 99.4 ± 0.195 (mean ± SE, 30 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Same average, different crowds

The histogram has two humps: about half the crowds fizzle and half riot almost completely, and almost none end in between. The mean describes no actual crowd. The theory (an enormous crowd) always tips: in a real crowd of 100, chance gaps in the chain of thresholds stop it half the time. Historians face this: knowing the general mood does not tell you whether a particular day will become a revolution.
Theory (very large crowd): 100
Result from a test run: mean 54.4, SD 47.9, SE 2.76 from 300 runs

Batch experiment 3: Legitimacy and rebellion

At 0.9 and above there is no open rebellion. Below that it rises as more people's grievance outweighs their fear, coming in bursts, so single runs vary. At low legitimacy it levels off near 7% because the police keep arresting rebels: by then a large share of the people are in prison.
Result from a test run: 0.95: 0 ± 0 · 0.9: 0 ± 0 · 0.85: 1.32 ± 0.111 · 0.8: 3.31 ± 0.249 · 0.75: 4.89 ± 0.176 · 0.7: 6.39 ± 0.194 · 0.65: 6.84 ± 0.0987 · 0.6: 6.61 ± 0.118 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Police and rebellion

More police raise everyone's estimated risk of arrest, so fewer rebel openly. But grievance is unchanged: the opposition is hidden, not removed (see the investigation 'Hidden grievance').
Result from a test run: 1: 45.5 ± 1.05 · 2: 22.9 ± 1.23 · 3: 7.13 ± 0.192 · 4: 5.9 ± 0.286 · 6: 4.17 ± 0.137 · 8: 0.328 ± 0.173 · 10: 0.0319 ± 0.00221 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Slow decline or sudden shock?

The sudden shock gives a much larger peak: many people cross the threshold at the same moment and the police cannot arrest them fast enough. The gradual fall reaches the same final legitimacy with a smaller peak.
Result from a test run: 0: 24.5 ± 1.91 · 1: 64 ± 0.465 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)
