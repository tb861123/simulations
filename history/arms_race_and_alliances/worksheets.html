Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The dreadnought race

What to look for: Each side builds partly in response to the other (reaction) and partly for its own reasons (Britain's empire and trade routes; Tirpitz's Navy Laws). Because the reactions are strong compared with the cost, the race accelerates: by 1914 Britain had about 30 and Germany about 20. Britain kept a lead because it reacted more strongly (the 'two-power standard', then Churchill's 60% margin).

Investigation 2: Fatigue against fear

What to look for: When fatigue outweighs fear (a·b > k·l), both sides level off at a steady balance instead of racing upwards. The readout shows the balance point. This is the logic of arms limitation: make arms costlier or the reaction weaker.

Investigation 3: Germany drops out

What to look for: Britain's building slows towards a lower balance, but it does not stop at once: Britain still has its own needs (g) and Germany's ships still exist. Anglo-German naval talks (the Haldane mission, 1912) failed, but the race was cooling by 1914, which is why many historians look beyond it for the war's causes.

Investigation 4: The nuclear arms race

What to look for: The model gets the broad rise and levelling off, but not the US peak in the mid-1960s followed by a fall while Soviet numbers kept rising. The US replaced many weapons with fewer, more accurate ones (and MIRVs: several warheads per missile): counting warheads misses changes in technology and strategy. Mutually assured destruction made a stable balance (a·b > k·l) more likely.

Investigation 5: Arms control

What to look for: The treaty weakens both reactions and both sides' ambitions, so the balance point falls. Real treaties capped launchers (SALT I) and removed a class of missiles (INF, 1987); deep cuts came after 1991 (START).

Investigation 6: The July Crisis

What to look for: With the settings as in 1914 (blank cheque, rigid alliances, the Schlieffen Plan, little mediation), a general war follows in roughly half of the runs. War was likely, but not certain: each link in the chain could have broken. Historians argue about which link mattered most.

Investigation 7: Was it the war plan? A level

What to look for: Without a single westward plan, Germany need not attack France at once, Belgium is not invaded and Britain has less reason to fight, and there are more days for diplomacy. The chance of general war falls sharply. This supports Taylor's view that mobilisation plans narrowed the choices, though Fischer would reply that the plan reflected German aims.

Investigation 8: Blank cheque and loose alliances A level

What to look for: Removing the blank cheque cuts the chance at the very first link, so everything downstream becomes less likely: this is Fischer's emphasis on German responsibility. Loosening every alliance spreads responsibility across all the powers, Clark's 'sleepwalkers'. The model shows both matter; which is 'more important' is a historian's judgement.

Batch experiment 1: When does an arms race run away?

The balance point grows faster and faster as k approaches the limit where k·l = a·b: near the edge, a small increase in fear makes a big difference. After 30 years the runs are close to the balance but still below it when k is large, because they take longer to get there.
Theory (balance point): 0: 6 · 0.02: 7.8 · 0.04: 10.3 · 0.06: 13.9 · 0.08: 19.6 · 0.1: 30 · 0.12: 55.3
Result from a test run: 0: 5.99 ± 5.13e-4 · 0.02: 7.7 ± 0.0029 · 0.04: 9.87 ± 0.0199 · 0.06: 12.6 ± 0.0357 · 0.08: 16 ± 0.0627 · 0.1: 20.2 ± 0.109 · 0.12: 25.8 ± 0.17 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: A general war: how likely?

Each batch of 50 gives a slightly different percentage (sampling variation), centred on the theory. A historian has only one July 1914 to study: that single outcome cannot tell us how likely it was.
Theory (chain of chances): 45.5
Result from a test run: mean 46.1, SD 7.29, SE 0.729 from 100 runs

Batch experiment 3: Alliances and the chance of general war

With the Schlieffen Plan in place, rigidity matters mainly through Russia's decision to back Serbia, so the curve is a straight line. The plan made France's and Britain's choices almost automatic.
Theory (chain of chances): 0.1: 20.3 · 0.25: 25.7 · 0.4: 31.1 · 0.55: 36.5 · 0.7: 41.9 · 0.85: 47.3 · 1: 52.7
Result from a test run: 0.1: 20.5 ± 1.39 · 0.25: 29.7 ± 1.54 · 0.4: 29.6 ± 1.56 · 0.55: 36.3 ± 1.01 · 0.7: 41.8 ± 1.33 · 0.85: 50.8 ± 1.18 · 1: 54.1 ± 1.31 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: The Schlieffen Plan

The plan nearly triples the chance of a general war in this model: it turns an eastern war into a western one, invades Belgium (bringing Britain in) and removes days for diplomacy.
Theory (chain of chances): 0: 16.3 · 1: 45.5
Result from a test run: 0: 15.7 ± 0.559 · 1: 46.2 ± 0.698 (mean ± SE, 12 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Can diplomacy save the peace?

Only about eight days in July offered a real chance for diplomacy (the ultimatum was secret and the war plan left little time later). Even fairly effective mediation leaves a large chance of war: the shortage of time was itself a cause.
