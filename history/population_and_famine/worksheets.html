Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The Malthusian trap

What to look for: Population grows to match the extra food, so food per head stays near the equilibrium (about 0.95 on this scale). Six centuries of progress gave England more people, not richer people: the Malthusian trap.

Investigation 2: The Black Death

What to look for: With fewer people on the same land, each worker produces more, so food per head and real wages jump (this happened after 1348: the Statute of Labourers tried to hold wages down). As the population recovers over about a century, wages fall back. Historians link the higher wages to the end of serfdom and the Peasants' Revolt (1381).

Investigation 3: Positive or preventive check?

What to look for: With no preventive check, births only balance deaths when people are close to starving, so bad harvests often cause famine (the positive check). Late marriage holds births down, so the equilibrium is well above famine level. Historians (Wrigley and Schofield) argue this 'European marriage pattern' kept England out of the worst famines after 1600.

Investigation 4: Escape from the trap

What to look for: Technology now grows at 1.5% a year, faster than population can follow, so food per head rises. As people get richer, fertility falls (the demographic transition), so the gains are kept. Malthus wrote in 1798, just as this escape began.

Investigation 5: Ireland: the population before 1845

What to look for: From about 4 million to over 8 million in 60 years. The potato gave more food per acre than any grain, so plots could be split between sons and people married young. By 1841 about a third of the people depended almost entirely on the potato: a population exposed to the failure of one crop.

Investigation 6: Ireland: the blight

What to look for: The worst years were 1846–47 ('Black '47'). In this model about 1.1 million die and 1.1 million emigrate, close to historians' estimates (about 1 million deaths; over 1 million emigrants). Most deaths were from disease spread by hunger and crowded workhouses, not starvation alone.

Investigation 7: Was it nature or choice? A level

What to look for: Halving the blight cuts deaths sharply, but so does doubling relief or keeping exported food at home. The blight was the trigger; how many died depended a great deal on policy. The 'revisionist' view stresses the scale of the natural disaster and the limits of 1840s government; 'nationalist' and post-revisionist historians (e.g. Kinealy) stress choices: laissez-faire, the end of the soup kitchens in 1847, the Gregory clause. A model cannot settle the moral question, but it shows the size of the choices.

Investigation 8: Why did the population keep falling? A level

What to look for: The model recovers towards 7–8 million. The real census fell to 4.5 million by 1901: the long-term consequences (emigration as a way of life, the stem family) mattered more for the population than the Famine deaths themselves. A good example of distinguishing short- and long-term consequences.

Batch experiment 1: Does better technology make people richer?

Faster technology only slightly raises food per head, because population growth absorbs most of it: at 0.1% a year (typical before 1700) people are barely better off. Points sit a little below the line because famine years kill extra people.
Theory (steady state): 0: 0.954 · 0.05: 0.987 · 0.1: 1.02 · 0.2: 1.09 · 0.3: 1.17 · 0.4: 1.25 · 0.5: 1.33
Result from a test run: 0: 0.958 ± 0.00243 · 0.05: 0.994 ± 0.00525 · 0.1: 1.02 ± 0.0016 · 0.2: 1.1 ± 0.00444 · 0.3: 1.17 ± 0.00268 · 0.4: 1.26 ± 0.00247 · 0.5: 1.33 ± 0.00354 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: After the Black Death

Recovery takes about 70 years in the model, with wide variation from the luck of the harvests. England's population did not regain its 1300 level until the 1500s or later: repeated plague outbreaks kept it down, which this single-plague model leaves out.
Theory (average harvests): 69
Result from a test run: mean 68.7, SD 4, SE 0.283 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 3: Late marriage and famine

Without a preventive check, the population lives close to the famine line and bad harvests often push it over. A stronger preventive check lifts the equilibrium, so famines become rare: Malthus's own hope (he called it 'moral restraint').
Result from a test run: 0: 2.5 ± 0.207 · 0.2: 0.48 ± 0.097 · 0.4: 0.04 ± 0.04 · 0.6: 0 ± 0 · 0.8: 0 ± 0 · 1: 0 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Relief and the death toll

Relief matters a great deal in the model: doubling it more than halves the deaths. In reality, relief was limited by money, administration, transport and ideology (laissez-faire and providentialism), which is exactly where historians disagree.
Theory (no random variation): 0: 1810 · 0.5: 1490 · 1: 1150 · 1.5: 752 · 2: 436
Result from a test run: 0: 1690 ± 35.3 · 0.5: 1370 ± 33.2 · 1: 1010 ± 17.5 · 1.5: 662 ± 8.15 · 2: 386 ± 8.14 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: How certain is 'about a million'?

Small uncertainties in the inputs give a wide range of outputs. Historians face the same problem: there was no death registration in Ireland until 1864, so excess deaths are estimated from the 1841 and 1851 censuses and assumptions about births and emigration.
Theory (historians' estimate): 1000
Result from a test run: mean 1100, SD 157, SE 11.1 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 6: Exports or no exports?

In the model stopping exports saves about a quarter of the lives. But the size of the effect rests on a rough guess at how much food was exported; historians (e.g. Ó Gráda, Kinealy) argue about this figure, and the exported food was owned by farmers who needed the money for rent.
Result from a test run: 0: 1030 ± 11.8 · 1: 774 ± 9.16 (mean ± SE, 30 runs each)
