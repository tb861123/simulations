Pitch, Intervals and Tuning · Teacher notespitch-intervals.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The octave

What to look for: The top note has exactly twice the frequency (261.6 → 523.3 Hz). Every octave doubles the frequency, which is why notes an octave apart share a letter name.

Investigation 2: Same interval, different difference

What to look for: The difference grows (65 Hz → 262 Hz) but the ratio stays at about 1.5. Our ears hear equal ratios as equal intervals.

Investigation 3: Semitones

What to look for: 2^(1/12) ≈ 1.0595 every time: 12 equal steps multiply to 2, the octave. That is equal temperament.

Investigation 4: Consonant and dissonant

What to look for: Fifth (smoothest), major third, tritone, minor 2nd (roughest). Simple ratios (3:2, 5:4) make many harmonics coincide; a semitone puts harmonics close but not together, so they beat.

Investigation 5: Beats

What to look for: A 'wah-wah' wobble in loudness: about 1.5 beats a second at C4 (beat frequency = |f₁ − f₂|). Tuners and string players tune by making the beats slow down and vanish.

Investigation 6: Spelling intervals

What to look for: A 3rd always moves up three letter names (D-E-F, E♭-F-G). So a major 3rd above D is F♯, and above E♭ it is G. Interval names count letters; the quality (major, minor …) counts semitones.

Investigation 7: Equal against just A level

What to look for: The just 5:4 third is smoother; the equal-tempered third is 13.7 cents sharp and beats noticeably. Equal temperament gives up pure thirds so that music can modulate to every key.

Investigation 8: Tuning a fifth by its beats A level

What to look for: The 3rd harmonic of A3 (660 Hz) and the 2nd harmonic of E4 (659.3 Hz) are 0.75 Hz apart: about 3 beats every 4 seconds. Piano tuners count these beats.

Batch experiment 1: Frequency against semitones

Exponential: the same step in pitch multiplies the frequency by the same factor, so high notes are far apart in Hz. A log scale (or the keyboard) makes it a straight line.
