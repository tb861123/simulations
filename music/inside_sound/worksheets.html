Inside a Sound · Teacher notesinside-sound.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Compressions and rarefactions

What to look for: Each particle only vibrates back and forth around its own place; the pattern of compressions (bunched, high pressure) and rarefactions (spread out, low pressure) is what travels. That is a longitudinal wave.

Investigation 2: Higher pitch

What to look for: The pitch goes up an octave; the compressions come twice as often and are half as far apart (λ halves, from 1.31 m to 0.65 m), because v = fλ and the speed stays the same.

Investigation 3: Louder

What to look for: The particles move further and the pressure swings are bigger, so it sounds louder. The frequency (pitch) and wavelength stay the same.

Investigation 4: Waveforms and timbre

What to look for: The square wave contains odd harmonics (3f, 5f, 7f… with amplitudes 1/3, 1/5, 1/7…), so it sounds brighter and buzzier. Same pitch, different timbre.

Investigation 5: Reading an oscilloscope

What to look for: Period = number of squares × time base (ms per square); frequency = 1 ÷ period. Amplitude = squares from the centre × volts per square.

Investigation 6: Sound in water and steel

What to look for: Sound travels faster in water (1480 m/s) and steel (5960 m/s) because the particles are closer together and more strongly linked. The frequency stays the same, so the wavelength is longer (λ = v ÷ f).

Investigation 7: The limits of hearing

What to look for: Humans hear roughly 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz, and the upper limit falls with age. Below 20 Hz is infrasound; above 20 kHz ultrasound (used for scans and cleaning). Your speakers may not reproduce very low notes.

Investigation 8: Building a square wave A level

What to look for: One harmonic gives a sine wave; adding 3f and 5f squares the corners. Any periodic wave can be built from sine waves (Fourier). The wiggles near the jumps never quite go away (the Gibbs effect).

Investigation 9: Decibels A level

What to look for: Level = 20 log₁₀(p ÷ 20 µPa), so doubling the pressure amplitude adds 20 log₁₀ 2 = 6 dB. Doubling the intensity (power) adds 3 dB.

Batch experiment 1: Measuring wavelength: v = fλ

λ falls as 1/f: doubling the frequency halves the wavelength, because the speed of sound in the air is the same for every note.
Theory (λ = v ÷ f): 100: 3.43 · 200: 1.72 · 300: 1.14 · 400: 0.858 · 600: 0.572 · 800: 0.429
Result from a test run: 100: 3.43 ± 0.00128 · 200: 1.72 ± 0.0012 · 300: 1.14 ± 0.00101 · 400: 0.855 ± 0.00114 · 600: 0.572 ± 0.00155 · 800: 0.43 ± 0.00163 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Speed of sound and temperature

Warmer air carries sound faster (the molecules move faster): about 0.6 m/s more per °C.
Theory (331 + 0.6T): -10: 325 · 0: 331 · 10: 337 · 20: 343 · 30: 349 · 40: 355
Result from a test run: -10: 325 ± 0.194 · 0: 331 ± 0.186 · 10: 337 ± 0.168 · 20: 343 ± 0.165 · 30: 349 ± 0.321 · 40: 355 ± 0.313 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Harmonics of a square wave

Only odd harmonics, getting weaker as 1/n: that recipe is what makes a square wave sound like a clarinet-ish buzz.
Theory (1/n for odd n): 1: 1 · 2: 0 · 3: 0.333 · 4: 0 · 5: 0.2 · 6: 0 · 7: 0.143 · 9: 0.111
Result from a test run: 1: 1 ± 0 · 2: 1.82e-16 ± 0 · 3: 0.333 ± 0 · 4: 1.97e-16 ± 0 · 5: 0.2 ± 0 · 6: 6.27e-17 ± 0 · 7: 0.143 ± 0 · 9: 0.111 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Reading the period from an oscilloscope

About ±16 Hz (4%) from one reading: measuring across several cycles and dividing is far more precise.
Theory (true): 400
Result from a test run: mean 401, SD 16.5, SE 0.955 from 300 runs

Batch experiment 5: Doubling the amplitude

A straight line: every ×10 in pressure adds 20 dB, and every doubling adds 6 dB. Our ears hear ratios, not differences.
Theory (20 log₁₀(p ÷ 20 µPa)): -2.7: 40 · -2: 54 · -1.3: 68 · -0.7: 80 · 0: 94 · 0.3: 100
Result from a test run: -2.7: 40 ± 0 · -2: 54 ± 0 · -1.3: 68 ± 0 · -0.7: 80 ± 0 · 0: 94 ± 0 · 0.3: 100 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)
