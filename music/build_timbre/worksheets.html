Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Pure tone to buzzy

What to look for: The pitch stays the same (it is set by the fundamental's repeat time); the sound gets brighter and buzzier, and the waveform gets more jagged.

Investigation 2: Build a square wave

What to look for: A square wave: hollow, clarinet-like, because there are no even harmonics. The more odd harmonics you add, the squarer the corners (with a small overshoot at each edge).

Investigation 3: Same spectrum, different envelope

What to look for: No. The start of a note (the attack transient) is a big part of how we recognise an instrument: a slow attack sounds bowed or blown, a sudden attack with decay sounds plucked or struck.

Investigation 4: The missing fundamental

What to look for: No: you still hear the same pitch. The remaining harmonics (2f, 3f, 4f …) still repeat every 1/f seconds, and your ear finds that repeat. This is why you can hear a bass line on tiny phone speakers.

Investigation 5: Brightness

What to look for: Brass has strong harmonics up to the 5th–6th, so its centroid is higher and it sounds brighter and more brassy. The flute is mostly fundamental: soft and pure.

Investigation 6: Phase does not matter (much)

What to look for: Hardly at all for a steady note: the ear mostly hears the amplitudes of the harmonics, not how they line up. The same spectrum can have very different waveforms.

Investigation 7: Fourier synthesis A level

What to look for: Its harmonics shrink as 1/n² (a square's as 1/n), so the high ones matter much less: a triangle has no sudden jumps, only corners. Smoother waves have spectra that fall off faster.

Investigation 8: Real instruments lose their brightness A level

What to look for: It starts bright and mellows as it fades, like a real guitar or piano: the high harmonics lose energy faster (air drag, internal friction). A synthesiser imitates this with a low-pass filter that closes during the note.

Batch experiment 1: Building a square wave

The error falls, but slowly (about 1/√N): the sharp edges need many harmonics. The little overshoot at each edge (the Gibbs effect) never quite goes away.

Batch experiment 4: Same spectrum, different waveform

Usually 4–6: identical spectra (and nearly identical sounds) but very different shapes. Waveform shape alone does not tell you the timbre.

Result from a test run: mean 4.86, SD 0.695, SE 0.0401 from 300 runs

Batch experiment 5: Pitch from autocorrelation

Errors of only a cent or two up to noise ÷ signal ≈ 0.5. When the noise is as strong as the note, the tracker often locks on to the wrong octave or harmonic (errors of 1200 cents or more). Tuner apps face exactly this problem.
