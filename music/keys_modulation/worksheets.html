Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Tones and semitones

What to look for: Major: T T S T T T S (semitones between 3–4 and 7–8). Natural minor: T S T T S T T (between 2–3 and 5–6). The minor third is what makes it sound sad or serious.

Investigation 2: The modes

What to look for: Dorian is minor with a raised 6th (folk, 'Scarborough Fair', jazz). Mixolydian is major with a flattened 7th (rock, Celtic music). Modes give music a different colour from major and minor.

Investigation 3: Blues and pentatonic

What to look for: The blues scale adds the flattened 5th (E♭ in A), the 'blue note', to the minor pentatonic. Pentatonic scales have no semitones, so any of their notes sound good together: great for improvising.

Investigation 4: Find the key

What to look for: Early on, several keys fit equally well. After about 8–12 notes the tonic, fifth and third stand out and the correlation with G major is clearly highest. Your ear does something similar.

Investigation 5: Modulation to the dominant A level

What to look for: G major has F♯ instead of F. Once F♯ appears and F disappears, the profile of G fits better. Related keys share six of their seven notes, so the change is smooth.

Investigation 6: A distant key A level

What to look for: Distant keys share few notes, so the change is abrupt and detected fast (and sounds startling). The circle of fifths measures closeness: one step = one note different.

Batch experiment 1: How many notes to find the key?

Short tunes are ambiguous (a few notes fit many keys); with more notes the estimate improves. Minor tunes become clear because of the raised leading note; major tunes are sometimes heard as a minor key that shares their notes (E minor or A minor for C major), because rhythm and cadences are ignored here.
Result from a test run: 4: 77.5 ± 6.69 · 8: 85 ± 5.72 · 12: 80 ± 6.41 · 16: 92.5 ± 4.22 · 24: 82.5 ± 6.08 · 32: 80 ± 6.41 (mean ± SE, 40 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Closeness on the circle of fifths

Neighbouring keys (G, F) fit well, the tritone away (F♯) worst: the circle of fifths is a map of how related keys sound.
Theory (profile against profile): 0: 1 · 1: 0.591 · 2: 0.0414 · 3: -0.104 · 4: -0.186 · 5: -0.501 · 6: -0.683
Result from a test run: 0: 0.854 ± 0.0191 · 1: 0.675 ± 0.0645 · 2: 0.23 ± 0.0345 · 3: 0.172 ± 0.0555 · 4: 0.0864 ± 0.0466 · 5: -0.276 ± 0.0434 · 6: -0.658 ± 0.0254 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: How long to hear a modulation?

A short window reacts quickly but is easily fooled; a long one is reliable but slow. Listeners also need a few bars, often a cadence in the new key, to be sure.
Result from a test run: 4: 2.35 ± 0.365 · 6: 4.8 ± 1.04 · 8: 5.75 ± 1.29 · 12: 6.05 ± 1.02 · 16: 6.4 ± 1.2 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)
