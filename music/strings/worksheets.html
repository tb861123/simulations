Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Shorter string, higher note

What to look for: Twice the frequency: an octave higher. f₁ = (1/2L)√(T/μ), so f ∝ 1/L. That is how a guitarist's finger, or a fret, changes the note.

Investigation 2: Tighter string

What to look for: No: f ∝ √T, so doubling the tension raises the frequency by √2 (about 41%, a little more than a fifth). Turning a tuning peg changes the tension.

Investigation 3: Thick strings sound low

What to look for: The low E is about 16 times heavier per metre, so it is √16 = 4 times lower: two octaves (E2 = 82 Hz against E4 = 330 Hz).

Investigation 4: Where you pluck

What to look for: At the middle the even harmonics are missing (they have a node there): a soft, hollow sound. Near the bridge many harmonics are strong: bright and twangy. Guitarists use this (sul ponticello, sul tasto).

Investigation 5: Pluck, strike or bow

What to look for: Plucked and struck notes die away (the high harmonics fastest, so the sound gets mellower); a bowed note keeps going because the bow keeps feeding energy in. Striking gives stronger high harmonics than plucking.

Investigation 6: Frets

What to look for: Fret 12 halves the vibrating length: an octave up. Each fret shortens the string by a factor of 2^(−1/12) ≈ 0.944: one semitone.

Investigation 7: Stationary wave formula A level

What to look for: v = √(T/μ) = √(72 ÷ 0.0063) = 107 m/s; λ = 2L = 1.296 m; f = v/λ = 82.4 Hz: E2.

Investigation 8: Stiff strings A level

What to look for: No: fₙ = nf₁√(1 + Bn²), so the high partials are sharp. Piano tuners 'stretch' the octaves to match. An ideal flexible string has B = 0.
