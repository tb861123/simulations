Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · How close is one fieldtrip's mean?

What this shows. Each run is a whole fieldtrip: 10 random sites on the pebble beach, 10 pebbles measured at each. The trips' means form a bell-shaped spread around the true mean of the whole beach (yellow), with a standard deviation close to the standard error predicted by SD ÷ √n (blue curve; units are mm). Half the trips are more than about two-thirds of a standard error out: one fieldtrip gives an estimate, not the truth.

2 · Does a bigger sample fix a biased method?

What this shows. Opportunistic sampling near the car parks puts every site at the western end, where pebbles are small. The trips' means are about 12 mm too low however many sites are taken (grey dashed line: the bias of the method). More sites only make the wrong answer more consistent. Random sampling (yellow, 0) has no bias: the cure for bias is a better sampling method, not a bigger sample.

3 · How many sites to find a real pattern?

What this shows. Each run sends 40 classes to the beach with the same plan and counts how many find a significant Spearman's rank correlation at 0.05. The pattern is real but weak (whole-beach rs about 0.6). With 6 sites only about 1 trip in 9 finds it; with 20 or more almost all do. The yellow line is the power predicted with Fisher's z; it fits well from about 10 sites. With very few sites the critical value in the table (0.886 for 6 sites) is stricter than the formula assumes, so fewer trips succeed. Runs scatter because 40 trips is a small class.

4 · False alarms when there is no pattern (A level)

What this shows. Here pebble size does not change along the beach at all, so the null hypothesis is true. About 5% of fieldtrips still find a 'significant' correlation: Type I errors, as the 0.05 level promises, whatever the number of sites (slightly fewer with very few sites, because the table's critical values are conservative). The local differences in the beach are random, so they can line up with distance by chance.

5 · Stratified or random: which is more precise?

What this shows. The typical error of the mean (root mean square over 40 trips) falls as 1 ÷ √n: the log–log gradient is close to −0.5, so four times as many sites halve the error. Stratified sampling (yellow line, and the dots) is about twice as precise as random sampling (grey dashed) here, because each third of the beach is sampled equally and the zones are very different. Stratifying is worth most when the strata differ.

6 · Chi-squared when there is no link (A level)

What this shows. Pebble shape is the same everywhere (no link with the zone), 15 sites, 10 pebbles each. The chi-squared values scatter around their degrees of freedom (4), with a long tail to the right; about 5% of trips pass the critical value 9.49 by chance. The normal curve is only a rough guide: the real distribution is the chi-squared distribution, which is skewed.
