Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Since 1750

What to look for: From about 280 ppm to about 420 ppm: a 50% rise, almost all since 1950. The model follows the measurements closely because it is driven by the recorded emissions.

Investigation 2: Where does the carbon go?

What to look for: Only about 45% stays in the air (the airborne fraction). The ocean takes about 25–30% and plants and soils the rest: without these sinks CO₂ would already be well above 500 ppm.

Investigation 3: Stop today

What to look for: It falls, but slowly: the ocean and land keep taking up carbon, so CO₂ drops by a few ppm a year at first, then more slowly. It is still far above 280 ppm in 2300: some of our CO₂ stays in the air for thousands of years.

Investigation 4: High emissions

What to look for: Low: about 410 ppm; high: about 800 ppm. More CO₂ dissolving in the sea makes carbonic acid, so the pH falls (from 8.18 in 1750 to under 7.8): ocean acidification, which harms corals and shellfish.

Investigation 5: Cutting down forests

What to look for: Carbon in trees is released to the air (burning and decay), and the cleared land no longer photosynthesises as much, so the land sink weakens too.

Investigation 6: Plant trees

What to look for: It helps: forests take carbon out of the air as they grow. But the effect is limited by land area, and it is much smaller than cutting fossil-fuel emissions; trees can also burn or be cut down again.

Investigation 7: The ocean fills up A level

What to look for: Dissolved CO₂ reacts with carbonate ions; as they are used up, the water can take less extra CO₂ for each extra ppm in the air (the Revelle factor rises from about 9.5). Mixing into the deep ocean takes centuries, so the surface ocean saturates.

Investigation 8: No ocean A level

What to look for: Roughly 30–40 ppm higher: the ocean has absorbed about a quarter of all our emissions.

Batch experiment 1: CO₂ in 2100 under each scenario

The choice of emissions matters far more than the uncertainty in the natural sinks.
Result from a test run: 1: 370 ± 3.31 · 2: 395 ± 4.54 · 3: 416 ± 4.98 · 4: 535 ± 10.7 · 5: 900 ± 26.2 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Stop today: how fast does CO₂ fall?

Faster mixing carries carbon into the deep ocean sooner, so CO₂ falls further. Even so it stays well above 280 ppm.
Result from a test run: 0.5: 385 ± 0.898 · 0.75: 375 ± 1.11 · 1: 367 ± 0.866 · 1.5: 358 ± 0.397 · 2: 353 ± 0.337 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Plants and the land sink

Stronger fertilisation → plants take up more carbon → a smaller share stays in the air. The observed airborne fraction (about 0.44) suggests β around 0.2.
Result from a test run: 0: 0.59 ± 0 · 0.1: 0.526 ± 0 · 0.2: 0.473 ± 0 · 0.3: 0.428 ± 0 · 0.4: 0.391 ± 0 · 0.5: 0.359 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Warming against total emissions

Very nearly a straight line: each 1000 GtC emitted adds roughly 1.5 °C (the TCRE). That is why a temperature target sets a carbon budget.
Result from a test run: 706: 1.22 ± 0 · 1000: 1.63 ± 0 · 1300: 2.03 ± 0 · 1600: 2.4 ± 0 · 1900: 2.75 ± 0 · 2200: 3.07 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Ocean acidification

pH 8.18 in 1750, about 8.05 today; down to about 7.8 under high emissions. Each 0.1 fall in pH means about 26% more hydrogen ions.
Result from a test run: 1: 8.09 ± 0.00402 · 2: 8.07 ± 0.00509 · 3: 8.04 ± 0.0054 · 4: 7.96 ± 0.00512 · 5: 7.77 ± 0.0181 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)
