Carbon Cycle and CO₂ · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Where will CO₂ be in 2100 on the middle road?

Each run uses different, equally believable values for ocean mixing, CO₂ fertilisation and sensitivity. The spread is the uncertainty from the Earth's response alone — the emissions are identical. The IPCC's SSP2-4.5 path reaches about 603 ppm; our simple model lands close to it, but our emissions are simplified.

2 · How much warmer by 2100 if emissions stay high?

The spread is mostly from climate sensitivity. The IPCC's 4.4 °C is measured from 1850–1900 and includes methane and other gases, so our CO₂-only figure is not expected to match exactly — but CO₂ is the biggest part.

3 · Does the model match Mauna Loa?

The fan shows the spread of model runs from 1750; white dots are measured annual means. An RMS error of a few ppm means the box model captures the real rise well (with the default settings a single run is about 1 ppm out; runs with extreme random settings are worse). Runs scatter because each one picks different ocean and plant responses; real CO₂ also wobbles by about 1 ppm from year to year (El Niño), which the model does not have.

4 · How long does a pulse of CO₂ stay in the air?

We add 100 GtC to the air in 2015 and compare with a run held at the 2015 level (the Joos et al. 2013 method). The red curve is the multi-model mean from that paper: about 60% left after 20 years, 40% after 100 years and 25% after 1000 years. The ocean slowly mixes it down; about a fifth stays for thousands of years because the ocean's chemistry is buffered.

5 · Which future? CO₂ in 2100 for each scenario

The choice of scenario changes 2100 CO₂ far more than the uncertainty in the Earth's response does. Red diamonds are the IPCC scenario values (where they exist).

6 · Does stronger CO₂ fertilisation help much?

Bigger β means plants grow faster with more CO₂ and soak up more. This is one of the biggest uncertainties in the real carbon cycle; it matters, but less than the emissions themselves.
