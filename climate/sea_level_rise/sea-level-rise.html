Warming pathway (°C)

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Driver: global warming ΔT (°C above 1850–1900). 1900–2024 follows the measured, smoothed record; after that a scenario path (like the IPCC's SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5 or SSP5-8.5, extended to 2300) or your slider.
  • Thermal expansion: water expands when it warms (about 0.0002 of its volume per °C near the surface, less in cold deep water). The upper ocean warms towards ΔT over about 25 years, the deep ocean towards the upper ocean over about 400 years. Rise = 0.065 m per °C of upper-ocean warming + 0.22 m per °C of deep-ocean warming. Because the deep ocean is slow, expansion keeps going for centuries after warming stops.
  • Mountain glaciers: hold about 0.32 m of sea level. Melt rate = 0.42 mm/yr × (ΔT + 1.2) × (1 + 0.5ΔT) × (fraction left)1.5. The "+1.2" is because glaciers were already shrinking in 1900 after the Little Ice Age. They run out, so this part slows down.
  • Greenland (7.4 m): melt rate = 0.45 mm/yr × (ΔT − 0.2 + 3f) × (1 + 0.2(ΔT − 0.2)) × (1 − f), where f is the fraction already lost. The "3f" is the height–melt feedback: as the ice sheet gets thinner its surface sits in warmer air, so it melts faster. This is why there is a threshold beyond which Greenland would eventually all melt — over a thousand years or more.
  • Antarctica: warm ocean water melts the ice shelves from below, letting glaciers flow faster: 0.9 mm/yr per °C of ocean warming (which lags ΔT by about 30 years). Warmer air also brings more snowfall, which takes 0.05 mm/yr per °C off.
  • Ice-cliff collapse (option): if ΔT passes the threshold, tall ice cliffs could become unstable (marine ice-cliff instability). The extra loss ramps up over 50 years to 25 mm/yr, up to 15 m. Scientists disagree strongly about whether and when this could happen; the IPCC calls it low-likelihood but high-impact.
  • Land water: dams held water back from 1950 to 1990 (−0.3 mm/yr); pumping groundwater adds +0.35 mm/yr from 2000 to 2100.
  • Floating ice (sea ice and ice shelves) adds nothing when it melts, as the glass demo shows. (In salty sea water it adds a tiny bit — about 4 cm if all the world's floating ice melted.)
  • The maps are made up but realistic: 5 km × 3.25 km. Each square is 25 m at Medium detail (50 m Low, 12.5 m High, 6.25 m Ultra); the land is the same at every setting. Heights are in metres above today's (2020) mean sea level. Water spreads from the sea into every square lower than the water level that it can reach, unless a wall with a higher top is in the way. High tide is shown: +2.2 m (estuary), +0.8 m (delta), +0.5 m (island). Homes: 5 per 25 m × 25 m of built-up land, 2.3 people per home.
  • Storms: the highest water level each year (tide + surge) follows a Gumbel distribution (estuary: mode 3.0 m, scale 0.25 m; delta: 1.6 m, 0.30 m; island: 1.0 m, 0.10 m). The surge is added on top of the new mean sea level.
  • Left out: sea level is not the same everywhere (gravity changes near melting ice, land sinking or rising, ocean currents), erosion, wave run-up, rivers flooding at the same time, how long a flood lasts, and how people adapt. Ice-sheet behaviour is the most uncertain part.
  • Where the model and reality disagree: the model gives about 0.16 m from 1900 to 2018. Tide gauges show about 0.20 m. The IPCC's own sum of the parts also comes out a little low for the early 1900s.
