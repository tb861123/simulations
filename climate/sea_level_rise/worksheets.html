Sea Level Rise · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Low emissions (SSP1-2.6): does 2100 land in the IPCC range?

Each run picks a different amount of future warming and different ice and ocean sensitivities. The shaded band is the IPCC AR6 'likely' range (a 66% chance of being inside it), the red line its median. Our runs scatter for the same reason the IPCC's do: we do not know exactly how sensitive the climate and the ice sheets are.

2 · High emissions (SSP5-8.5): does 2100 land in the IPCC range?

With more warming the spread is wider, mainly because of the ice sheets. The IPCC range leaves out the possible Antarctic ice-cliff collapse; tick that option on the left to see the long upper tail it adds.

3 · How much higher by 2100 for each degree of warming?

More warming means a higher sea, but by 2100 the difference is 'only' tens of centimetres, because the ocean and the ice sheets respond slowly. Red diamonds are the IPCC medians for SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 at their 2100 warming.

4 · …and by 2300?

By 2300 the choice matters far more: metres rather than centimetres. The red bars are the IPCC AR6 ranges for 2300 (SSP1-2.6: 0.3–3.1 m, SSP5-8.5: 1.7–6.8 m; their SSP5-8.5 warming by 2300 is much higher than the slider's value, which is held after 2100).

5 · With 0.5 m more sea, how often is today's 1-in-100-year flood reached?

Each run is 100 years of storms with random heights (a Gumbel distribution, used by coastal engineers). Count how many years beat today's 100-year flood level. With no rise you would expect about 1 per century. Runs scatter because storms are random; the average matches the theory value from the distribution.

6 · How does the return period shrink as the sea rises?

Each run simulates 2,000 years of storms. A small rise turns a rare flood into a common one: the curve falls steeply on the log scale. The steepness depends on how much storms vary on this coast (the Gumbel scale): places with small storm variation, like coral islands, are hit hardest by a small rise.
