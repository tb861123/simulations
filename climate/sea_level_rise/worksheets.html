Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Why is the sea rising?

What to look for: Thermal expansion and melting glaciers have added the most (about 80% together); Greenland and Antarctica are growing. About 20 cm since 1900, now about 4 mm a year and speeding up.

Investigation 2: Floating or land ice?

What to look for: Only the land ice: floating ice already displaces its own weight of water. That is why melting Arctic sea ice adds almost nothing, but Greenland and Antarctica (ice on land) add a lot.

Investigation 3: Which future?

What to look for: About half a metre (low) against over three-quarters of a metre (high) by 2100, but by 2300 the gap is metres: the ice sheets respond slowly, so today's emissions decide the coast of centuries ahead.

Investigation 4: Committed rise

What to look for: No: the deep ocean keeps warming and the ice sheets keep losing ice for centuries, so the sea keeps rising (about 1 m by 2300 even here). That is committed sea level rise.

Investigation 5: Storms on a higher sea

What to look for: The same storm floods much more land when the sea starts higher. A rise of half a metre makes today's 100-year flood happen every few years.

Investigation 6: Build a sea wall

What to look for: It protects the land behind it until storm water rises above the wall; then everything behind floods at once. Walls must be raised as the sea rises (or the town must adapt or retreat).

Investigation 7: Return periods A level

What to look for: With a Gumbel distribution of storm heights (β = 0.25 m), each 0.25 m of rise multiplies the frequency by e: 0.5 m of rise → about 7 times more often, roughly once every 14 years.

Batch experiment 1: Sea level rise by 2100 in each pathway

Every pathway rises tens of centimetres (much of it already committed); the gap between pathways and the uncertainty both grow with time.
Theory (central values): 1: 40.1 · 2: 45 · 3: 49.3 · 4: 60.3 · 5: 77
Result from a test run: 1: 40.8 ± 0.619 · 2: 44.1 ± 1.2 · 3: 52.7 ± 1.79 · 4: 65.9 ± 2.48 · 5: 81.2 ± 2.86 (mean ± SE, 30 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: How sure are we? (middle pathway)

A skewed spread with a long high tail, mostly from Antarctica: low chances of a much bigger rise matter for planning.
Theory (central): 60.3
Result from a test run: mean 63.3, SD 12.2, SE 0.546 from 500 runs

Batch experiment 3: How much more often is the 100-year flood?

Each 25 cm of rise multiplies flood frequency by about e (2.7): half a metre makes it about 7 times more often.
Theory (Gumbel: ≈ e^(rise/β)): 0: 1 · 0.1: 1.49 · 0.2: 2.21 · 0.3: 3.28 · 0.4: 4.86 · 0.5: 7.16
Result from a test run: 0: 0.992 ± 0.0411 · 0.1: 1.46 ± 0.0628 · 0.2: 2.22 ± 0.0718 · 0.3: 3.23 ± 0.0875 · 0.4: 4.63 ± 0.089 · 0.5: 7.05 ± 0.118 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Committed rise after emissions stop

It keeps rising for centuries: the deep ocean and the ice sheets are still catching up with the warming we have already caused.
Theory (central): 2050: 18.4 · 2100: 40.1 · 2150: 61.3 · 2200: 82.2 · 2300: 123
Result from a test run: 2050: 18.5 ± 0.381 · 2100: 40.1 ± 1.25 · 2150: 65.3 ± 3.12 · 2200: 85.2 ± 2.91 · 2300: 123 ± 5.98 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)
