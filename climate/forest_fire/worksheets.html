Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · How often do firebreaks hold?

How often do firebreaks hold? The fire starts 300 m upwind of a grid of 20 m fire tracks. A surface fire can't cross bare ground at all in this model, so the tracks always hold — until the wind is strong enough for the flames to climb into the treetops. A crown fire throws embers 100 m or more, far wider than any track, and the break fails almost every time. That is why the change is so sudden (between about 4 and 5 m/s here). Try sweeping the track width instead: from 10 to 40 m it makes little difference, because embers fly much further than that. Real fire services find the same: breaks stop surface fires, but crown fires 'spot' over them. Each repeat uses a new random map (same kind of wood), so the woods and glades fall in different places. With 6 runs per step each percentage is uncertain by up to about ±20 points.

2 · Percolation: how dense must a forest be to burn through?

Percolation: how dense must a forest be to burn through? With no undergrowth, fire can only pass from tree to tree, like water through a sieve. Percolation theory says that on an endless square grid with trees placed at random, fire spreads forever once more than 59.3% of squares have trees (if it can only jump to the 4 touching squares) or 40.7% (8 squares, as in this model). The model needs more than 40.7% because (1) a tree only gets a few minutes to set its neighbour alight, so some jumps fail, (2) its trees are clumped into woods and glades rather than placed at random, and (3) the map is finite. Each repeat uses a new random map, as percolation theory assumes. Look at 'Actual tree cover' too: the density setting is not exactly the fraction of squares with trees. The change from 'fizzles' to 'burns through' is still sharp: a tipping point.

3 · Wind and the speed of spread

Wind and the speed of spread The theory line is the model's own rate-of-spread formula, R = base rate × dryness factor × (1 + 0.6V1.5), averaged over the grass on this map. The measured head speed comes out 2–4 times faster (see the 'measured ÷ theory' column). That isn't a mistake: the formula gives the AVERAGE time for flames to cross one square, but each square catches at a random moment, and the head of the fire is carried forward by whichever chain of squares happens to catch quickest — the fastest of thousands of possible paths, which is much faster than an average one (mathematicians call this 'first-passage percolation'). With no wind the 'head' is the furthest point in any direction, which makes the effect bigger. Above about 10 m/s embers start spot fires ahead of the front and the head jumps forward even faster. The shape — speed rising steeply with wind — matches real grass fires.

4 · Dryness: how wet must fuel be to stop a fire?

Dryness: how wet must fuel be to stop a fire? Water in the fuel has to be boiled off before it can burn, and damp fuel damps the flames. The theory line is the model's dryness factor (Rothermel's formula) times the wind and fuel terms. Speed falls steadily with moisture and reaches zero at the grass's 'moisture of extinction', 25%. The measured head is 2–4 times faster than the average formula, for the same reason as in the wind experiment (the fastest path wins), Near 24% the model's fire dies out even though the formula is still just above zero: each square flames for only about 2 minutes, too short to light the next square at such a slow rate. Real fires also have a 'go / no-go' moisture rather than slowing smoothly to nothing.

5 · Same forest, different luck

Same forest, different luck Exactly the same map and weather, 50 times. The only difference is chance: which embers land where, and which squares catch first. Some fires are stopped by the river; in others an ember lands on the far side and they burn much more (look for two humps in the histogram). There is no formula that predicts the area exactly — the histogram is the prediction: a fire manager would quote the range and the most likely outcome, as weather forecasters do.

6 · Does a river stop it?

Does a river stop it? A 50 m wide river with a gentle breeze (4 m/s) blowing across it. Flames can't cross water, so every crossing is an ember (a spot fire). The answer is a probability, not yes or no; its uncertainty with 40 runs is about ±√(p(1−p)/40), up to ±8 percentage points. Run it again with a different batch seed and the percentage changes by about that much. Then try a stronger wind.
