Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The tipping point

What to look for: There is a critical density close to 59%. Below it the forest is a set of separate clumps and every fire stays small. Above it the clumps join into one connected network and a single spark can cross the whole map. Scientists call this a percolation threshold: a tipping point.

Investigation 2: Wind

What to look for: Still air gives a roughly circular fire. Wind stretches it into a long ellipse with a fast 'head' downwind and slow 'flanks'. Embers carried by the wind start spot fires ahead of the front: this is how fires jump roads and rivers.

Investigation 3: Uphill

What to look for: Fire runs uphill: the flames lean over the fuel above them and pre-heat it. A fire can travel several times faster up a steep slope than on the flat, and it slows right down going downhill. Ridge lines are natural places to stop a fire.

Investigation 4: Drought

What to look for: Yes: another threshold. Damp fuel rarely passes fire on, so each burning cell lights on average fewer than one neighbour and the fire dies. Above the threshold each lights more than one and the fire grows. A warmer climate means more days above the threshold.

Investigation 5: Save the village

What to look for: A break right next to the fire is quickly outflanked; a break close to the village must be wide enough to stop embers. Good breaks are anchored to bare ground or water and angled so the fire is steered past. In strong wind, embers jump narrow breaks: the real reason villages burn.

Investigation 6: The firefighter's paradox

What to look for: Putting out every small fire lets fuel build up into one continuous, dense forest, far above the critical density. When a fire finally escapes on a hot windy day it is enormous. Many fire services now use controlled burning to keep fuel patchy.

Batch experiment 1: The percolation threshold

Each forest is a different random arrangement of trees. Close to the critical density (59.3% for an infinite forest) the outcome depends on whether a few chance bridges of trees happen to exist, so the spread of results is enormous there and small elsewhere. That is the mark of a tipping point.
Result from a test run: 40: 1.28 ± 0.045 · 48: 2.53 ± 0.281 · 52: 3.89 ± 0.561 · 55: 7.06 ± 1.16 · 57: 15.2 ± 2.91 · 59: 25.8 ± 4.14 · 61: 59.4 ± 5.6 · 63: 90.6 ± 0.601 · 66: 94.7 ± 0.471 · 70: 98 ± 0.1 · 80: 99.8 ± 0.0276 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Dryness

Near the threshold some fires die in the first few steps by bad luck and others escape, so the results split into two groups: almost nothing or almost everything.
Result from a test run: 10: 0.233 ± 0.0783 · 15: 0.658 ± 0.434 · 20: 1.76 ± 0.832 · 25: 9.94 ± 4.1 · 30: 26.4 ± 11.3 · 35: 86.4 ± 2.45 · 40: 94.1 ± 0.296 · 50: 98.5 ± 0.0545 · 70: 99.9 ± 0.0131 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Wind and survival of a fire

Wind makes spread downwind much more likely and upwind much less likely, and embers start new fires ahead. A fire that would die in still air can run as a narrow tongue in wind.
Result from a test run: 0: 2.82 ± 1.22 · 0.4: 11.2 ± 3.39 · 0.8: 19.8 ± 4.71 · 1.2: 28 ± 4.17 · 1.6: 36.4 ± 1.15 · 2: 38.4 ± 0.839 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: The firefighter's paradox

With no suppression, frequent small fires keep the forest patchy and near the critical density, so the final fire is limited. With total suppression the forest grows dense and connected, and the one fire that escapes takes nearly everything.
Result from a test run: 0: 0.772 ± 0.445 · 25: 0.259 ± 0.083 · 50: 0.832 ± 0.352 · 75: 0.115 ± 0.0388 · 90: 2.24 ± 1.7 · 100: 80.4 ± 0.0349 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)
