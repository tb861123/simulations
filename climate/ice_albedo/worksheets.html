Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Sun down, then back up: does the climate retrace its path?

What this shows. Starting from a bright Sun, the planet is ice-free. Going down (blue), ice caps appear suddenly just below 100%, then the ice edge creeps towards the equator until the Sun reaches the tipping point (about 93%) — then the ice runs away and the planet jumps to a snowball. Coming back up (red), the planet stays frozen far past where it froze: white ice reflects so much sunlight that it needs about 130% of today's Sun to melt. Then it jumps all the way to ice-free. (The model melts out a little before the theory says, because its ice-to-water switch is smoothed over ±1 °C.) Two different climates for the same Sun is called hysteresis. The points sit on the solid (stable) parts of the theory curve; the dashed middle part is unstable, so the model never stays there.

2 · Near the edge: how long until random weather tips it?

What this shows. The planet sits in a shallow valley just above the tipping point. Each year's weather is random, and eventually a run of cold years pushes the ball over the hill. If each year carried the same small chance of tipping, the waiting times would follow an exponential distribution (gold curve), like radioactive decay: you cannot predict when any one run will tip. The model tips a little less often than that in the first few decades: near a tipping point the climate responds very slowly (“critical slowing down”), so the ice needs decades of mostly cold weather to creep over the hill, and the weather has to “forget” its start before tipping becomes truly random. Runs that had not tipped by the end are counted but not plotted.

3 · Does stormier weather make tipping more likely?

What this shows. The fraction of runs that tip within 300 years rises steeply with the size of the weather noise: from almost never to almost always. Theory for escaping a valley (Kramers' rule) says the average waiting time grows like e^(k/σ²), so ln(waiting time) against 1/σ² should be a straight line — see the fit. Each point is a proportion of 30 runs, so it scatters by about ±9%.

4 · How much CO₂ to escape a snowball?

What this shows. Starting frozen, the planet stays a snowball until CO₂ is enormous — the theory says the equator only gets above −10 °C when CO₂ passes the threshold marked — more CO₂ than the whole of today's atmosphere! (The model melts out a little before the theory line because its ice-to-water switch is smoothed over ±1 °C, and the simple 5.35 ln(C) rule is only a rough guide at such huge amounts.) Then it jumps straight to ice-free. Geologists think this really happened: during a snowball, volcanoes kept adding CO₂ while frozen rocks could not remove it, until after millions of years it built up enough to melt the ice — followed by a scorching hothouse.

5 · Faint young Sun: how much CO₂ to stay unfrozen?

What this shows. With a Sun only 80% as bright (about 3.8 billion years ago), today's CO₂ would freeze the planet. Yet rocks show liquid water 4 billion years ago: the 'faint young Sun paradox'. Starting from a warm planet, the sweep shows how much CO₂ (or other greenhouse gas) is needed to keep the ice caps from running away — a lot. The solid curve is the stable theory and the dotted line marks where the ice caps stop running away; below it the run ends at 0° (frozen). This model needs several percent of the air to be CO₂; real early Earth probably also had methane, less land and darker oceans to help.

6 · CO₂ up, then down: Arctic ice hysteresis

What this shows. Raising CO₂ shrinks the ice caps smoothly until they are small — then the last ice vanishes suddenly (the small ice cap instability). Lowering CO₂ again, the ice does not come back at the same level: the ice-free planet absorbs more sunlight and has to be cooled further first. Sea ice in the real Arctic is thought to be less 'tippy' than this model suggests, because ice that melts in summer regrows quickly each winter.
