How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Bands of latitude: 90 bands, each 2° wide, from pole to pole. Each band has one temperature, averaged around the globe and over the year. Bands near the poles are weighted less because they cover less of the Earth's area.
  • Sunlight per m² of globe Q = 1361 × (Sun %)/4 W/m², shared between latitudes by s(φ) = 1 − 0.482 × ½(3 sin²φ − 1), which fits the yearly average (with the Earth's tilt).
  • Albedo: 0.30 for open ocean and land; the chosen ice albedo (0.62) where the temperature is below −10 °C (a year-round average of −10 °C is about where sea ice lasts all year). The switch is smoothed over about ±1 °C.
  • Infrared out = A + B T, with A = 207.5 W/m² and B = 2.09 W/m² per °C: a straight-line fit to satellite measurements, which already includes the greenhouse effect and water vapour feedback. CO₂ changes A by −5.35 ln(C/420 ppm) W/m².
  • Heat transport by winds and ocean currents is treated as diffusion: heat flows from each band to its cooler neighbours in proportion to the temperature difference, with strength D (0.45 W/m² per °C).
  • Heat capacity: ocean bands hold a 50 m deep mixed layer of water (6.6 W·year per m² per °C); land 2 m equivalent. "Earth-like" puts 40% land in the north and 20% in the south.
  • Time stepping: implicit steps of 0.25 years (0.5 years in batch runs) so the model stays stable.
  • Weather noise: each year a random extra energy flow (normal distribution, SD set by the slider, the same everywhere) is added. It stands for cloudy years, volcanic eruptions and ocean cycles.
  • The equilibrium (theory) curve is found without any time-stepping: fix the ice edge at latitude η, solve the steady-state equations (they are linear once the albedo is fixed), and work out what sunshine (or CO₂) would put −10 °C exactly at that edge. Where the ice edge moves towards the pole as the Sun brightens the state is stable (solid); where it moves the other way it is unstable (dashed).
  • The ball-in-a-valley picture: with the Sun and CO₂ fixed, the ice edge creeps towards the pole when the temperature there is above −10 °C and towards the equator when below. The height of the landscape is minus the running total of that "push", so valleys are stable climates and hilltops are tipping points.
  • Calibration: today's settings give an ice edge near 64° and a global mean of about 11.5 °C, a few degrees cooler than the real Earth (14–15 °C), and the model tips into a snowball if the Sun dims by about 7%.
  • Left out: seasons and day/night, separate land and sea ice, ice sheets that take thousands of years to grow, clouds changing, the carbon cycle (in reality CO₂ builds up in a snowball because rocks stop weathering — here you have to raise it yourself), and the shape of the continents. Because the infrared fit is a straight line it is only reliable within a few tens of degrees of today's climate; in a hot ice-free world or a deep snowball the numbers are rough.
