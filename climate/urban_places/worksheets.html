Changing Urban Places · Teacher notesurban-places.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Land use and land value

What to look for: Land is most accessible at the centre, so shops and offices outbid housing there (bid-rent theory). Values fall steeply with distance; homes get bigger and greener towards the suburbs and the rural–urban fringe.

Investigation 2: Where is deprivation?

What to look for: The old industrial inner east: low incomes, empty and derelict buildings, little green space. Deprivation is concentrated, not spread evenly.

Investigation 3: Regeneration

What to look for: Investment raises land values and lowers measured deprivation, but much of the improvement comes from richer people moving in. Many original residents are priced out (displaced): gentrification.

Investigation 4: Mild preferences, strong segregation

What to look for: Schelling's surprise: the index of dissimilarity climbs well above the random level. Small individual preferences add up to strongly segregated places, without anyone wanting segregation.

Investigation 5: Affordable housing A level

What to look for: Keeping homes affordable (social housing, rent controls, section 106 agreements) lets original residents stay. Deprivation falls less on paper, because the poorer people are still there, but the community is not broken up.

Investigation 6: Location quotient A level

What to look for: LQ = share of group B in the area ÷ share in the city. LQ > 1: over-represented; < 1: under-represented; 1: the same as the city. As the city separates, LQs move away from 1.

Investigation 7: Cooling the city A level

What to look for: Paved, built-up blocks store heat and release it at night: the urban heat island, several °C. More vegetation (evapotranspiration, shade) cools the centre: a sustainable urban strategy.

Batch experiment 1: Schelling: preference and segregation

Even 30–40% preferences produce strong segregation: the whole is more extreme than its parts. With no preference the index stays near its random value (about 0.15 here, from small districts).
Result from a test run: 0: 0.198 ± 0.0104 · 0.2: 0.291 ± 0.00905 · 0.3: 0.416 ± 0.0261 · 0.4: 0.509 ± 0.0244 · 0.5: 0.579 ± 0.0245 · 0.6: 0.65 ± 0.0143 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Bid-rent: land value against distance

A steep fall close to the centre, flattening out: accessibility is worth most where it is greatest.
Theory (bid-rent curve): 0: 960 · 1: 714 · 2: 535 · 4: 310 · 6: 192 · 8: 130 · 10: 96.7
Result from a test run: 0: 979 ± 46.7 · 1: 703 ± 37.9 · 2: 525 ± 23.9 · 4: 322 ± 13.5 · 6: 195 ± 9.81 · 8: 129 ± 6.55 · 10: 98.4 ± 3.84 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Affordable housing and displacement

Displacement falls steadily as more homes stay affordable. Regeneration can improve a place without replacing its people, but it costs public money or developers' profit.
Result from a test run: 0: 91.8 ± 3.42 · 0.2: 65 ± 2.35 · 0.4: 53 ± 2.8 · 0.6: 33.8 ± 0.629 · 0.8: 16.8 ± 2.32 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Green cover and the heat island

Each extra tenth of green cover cools the centre by about half a degree in this model.
Theory (model): 0: 4.27 · 0.1: 3.94 · 0.2: 3.46 · 0.3: 2.99 · 0.4: 2.51 · 0.5: 2.04
Result from a test run: 0: 4.18 ± 0.0666 · 0.1: 4.12 ± 0.0344 · 0.2: 3.37 ± 0.0937 · 0.3: 3.11 ± 0.227 · 0.4: 2.53 ± 0.222 · 0.5: 1.93 ± 0.0765 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: The LQ of a random area

Chance alone gives LQs between about 0.85 and 1.15 here: a measured LQ needs to be well outside that before it shows real concentration.
Theory (LQ = 1): 1
Result from a test run: mean 0.993, SD 0.0906, SE 0.00641 from 200 runs
