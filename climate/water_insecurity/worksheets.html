Teacher notes

Investigation 1: A year of soil water

What to look for: In a Mediterranean climate the rain comes in the cool winter, when evapotranspiration is low: the soil recharges and then there is a surplus that fills the reservoir. In the hot, dry summer the soil dries (utilisation) and plants go short (deficit).

Investigation 2: Day Zero

What to look for: One dry year is survivable; two or three in a row empty the reservoir. Restrictions (level 1 at 30% full, harsh rationing at 15%) cut demand and stretch the water. Cape Town came within weeks of turning off the taps in 2018.

Investigation 3: More people, more stress

What to look for: The water available stays the same (on average), but it is shared between more people, so water per person falls. Growth in demand, not only drought, causes water insecurity.

Investigation 4: Fix the leaks

What to look for: Leaks lose about a fifth of the water; meters cut use by about 15%. Desalination adds a reliable supply but needs a lot of energy and money. Demand management is often cheaper and more sustainable.

Investigation 5: A warmer world A level

What to look for: Higher temperatures raise evapotranspiration, so the soil dries more, less water runs off and irrigation demand rises: hydrological drought gets worse even with the same rain.

Investigation 6: Upstream, downstream A level

What to look for: Downstream, inflow falls and shortages grow even with the same rain: a transboundary conflict (the Nile and the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam, the Tigris–Euphrates). Agreements share water and data; integrated drainage basin management plans the whole basin.

Investigation 7: Drip irrigation A level

What to look for: Farms use most of the water. Drip irrigation cuts their demand by about 40%; a bigger dam stores more in wet years but cannot create water in a drought, and it loses more to evaporation.

Batch experiment 1: Day Zero risk and population

Risk rises steeply once demand approaches what the basin can supply in a run of dry years.
Result from a test run: 2: 0 ± 0 · 3: 5 ± 5 · 4: 5 ± 5 · 5: 75 ± 9.93 · 6: 95 ± 5 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Day Zero risk and warming

More evaporation means less runoff and more irrigation demand: warming alone makes water insecurity worse.
Result from a test run: 0: 30 ± 10.5 · 1: 60 ± 11.2 · 2: 90 ± 6.88 · 3: 85 ± 8.19 · 4: 95 ± 5 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Runoff against rainfall

Not proportional: the soil and plants take their share first, so dry years give very little runoff. A 30% fall in rain can halve the water reaching the reservoir.
Result from a test run: 300: 0 ± 0 · 450: 16.8 ± 15.7 · 600: 48.5 ± 18 · 750: 104 ± 20.4 · 900: 263 ± 64.3 · 1100: 445 ± 65.4 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Water stress over 30 years

The trend falls as population grows; the scatter is the weather. The city's own catchment gives far less than 500 m³ a person (absolute scarcity), which is why such cities depend on large dams, groundwater and transfers.
Result from a test run: 0: 151 ± 24.7 · 5: 134 ± 45.3 · 10: 122 ± 19.6 · 15: 150 ± 38.5 · 20: 84.7 ± 17.8 · 25: 107 ± 29 · 29: 126 ± 13.3 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Which measure saves most?

Each measure helps, and combining demand management with a new supply helps most. In wet futures there is no shortage to avoid, so many runs give zero: look at the means.
Result from a test run: 1: 136 ± 34.8 · 2: 163 ± 50.4 · 3: 97.2 ± 28.8 · 4: 167 ± 46.3 · 5: 185 ± 78.2 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)
