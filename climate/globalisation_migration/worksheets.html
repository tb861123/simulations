Globalisation and Migration · Teacher notesglobalisation-migration.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Who trades with whom?

What to look for: Big economies trade most with each other (USA–China, Germany–China, USA–Mexico), especially if they are close. That is the gravity model: trade ∝ size × size ÷ distance.

Investigation 2: A shrinking world

What to look for: Container ships, jet travel, the internet and falling tariffs make distance matter less (time–space compression). In the model, trade between these sixteen countries roughly doubles as a share of their GDP, and migration grows too.

Investigation 3: Push and pull

What to look for: Pull: higher wages in the USA and UAE. Push: lower wages at home. Distance and past links (diasporas) steer flows: Mexico to the USA, the Philippines to the UAE and the USA.

Investigation 4: Money flows home

What to look for: Countries with many emigrants in rich places: the Philippines, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Mexico, Ghana. Remittances can be a large share of GDP: an important flow for development (and a risk if host economies slump).

Investigation 5: Brexit: an intervening obstacle A level

What to look for: Flows from Poland and Romania to the UK fall sharply and partly divert to Germany. Borders and policies are intervening obstacles (Lee's model): they reshape flows without changing the push and pull.

Investigation 6: Switched off A level

What to look for: Places with small, distant economies are least connected (switched off). Tariffs cut trade most for the smaller, more trade-dependent economies. Globalisation is uneven.

Investigation 7: Distance decay A level

What to look for: Migrants ∝ 1 ÷ distance: double the distance, half the migrants. A log–log plot is a straight line of gradient −1. Twenty pairs with real-world scatter give exponents mostly between about 0.85 and 1.15.

Batch experiment 1: Ravenstein: migrants against distance

Distance decay: twice as far, half as many. The scatter is why geographers use log scales and many pairs.
Theory (∝ 1 ÷ distance): 500: 1050 · 1000: 523 · 2000: 262 · 4000: 131 · 8000: 65.4
Result from a test run: 500: 1150 ± 130 · 1000: 489 ± 67.4 · 2000: 296 ± 25.8 · 4000: 142 ± 13.8 · 8000: 61.9 ± 5.61 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Fitting the distance-decay exponent

Most are within ±0.15. More pairs, or less scatter, narrows the spread.
Theory (true exponent): 1
Result from a test run: mean 1, SD 0.0691, SE 0.00691 from 100 runs

Batch experiment 3: Tariffs and world trade

Each extra point of tariff cuts trade by about 2%: trade liberalisation (GATT and the WTO) was a big driver of globalisation.
Theory (model): 0: 17.1 · 5: 15.5 · 10: 13.9 · 15: 12.4 · 20: 11 · 30: 8.4
Result from a test run: 0: 17.5 ± 0.316 · 5: 15.7 ± 0.263 · 10: 14.1 ± 0.205 · 15: 12.1 ± 0.173 · 20: 11.1 ± 0.289 · 30: 8.69 ± 0.209 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: The shrinking world, 1960–2020

Trade grows much faster than output: cheaper transport and lower tariffs mean distance matters less.
Theory (model): 1960: 8.55 · 1970: 9.91 · 1980: 11 · 1990: 12.4 · 2000: 13.9 · 2010: 15.4 · 2020: 15.8
Result from a test run: 1960: 8.41 ± 0.221 · 1970: 9.71 ± 0.201 · 1980: 11 ± 0.243 · 1990: 12.7 ± 0.229 · 2000: 14.4 ± 0.271 · 2010: 15.8 ± 0.3 · 2020: 16 ± 0.336 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Remittances and the wage gap

Bigger wage gaps pull more migrants and each sends home more: remittances rise steeply with inequality between countries.
Theory (model): 2: 0.119 · 4: 0.657 · 6: 1.35 · 10: 3.03 · 15: 5.46 · 20: 8.15
Result from a test run: 2: 0.131 ± 0.00959 · 4: 0.701 ± 0.0475 · 6: 1.44 ± 0.0816 · 10: 2.8 ± 0.154 · 15: 5.8 ± 0.582 · 20: 9.56 ± 0.916 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)
