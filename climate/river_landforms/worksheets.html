Rivers and Landforms · Teacher notesriver-landforms.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The Bradshaw model

What to look for: Discharge, width, depth, cross-section area and (surprisingly) velocity increase; gradient, bed-load size and roughness decrease. The river is steeper upstream, but shallow, rough channels waste energy on friction.

Investigation 2: Fieldwork down the river

What to look for: Width rises downstream, but measurements scatter (±2% width, ±8% float timing, pebble sizes vary a lot). That is why fieldwork samples several sites and draws a line of best fit.

Investigation 3: Upper course to lower course

What to look for: Upper course: a steep V-shaped valley with interlocking spurs and a narrow, shallow, bouldery channel (vertical erosion). Lower course: a wide flat flood plain with levees and a deep, smooth channel (lateral erosion and deposition).

Investigation 4: The Hjulström curve

What to look for: Sand moves first (around 20–30 cm/s). Clay particles stick together (cohesion) so they need a faster flow to erode, but once moving they stay in suspension even in very slow water.

Investigation 5: A flood

What to look for: As the flood peaks, pebbles and cobbles start rolling (traction) and bouncing (saltation); as it falls they are dropped first, then the sand, while silt and clay stay suspended: deposition sorts the load by size.

Investigation 6: Meanders and oxbow lakes

What to look for: Fast water on the outside of a bend erodes a river cliff; slow water on the inside deposits a point bar. Bends grow until the neck is cut through (usually in a flood), leaving an oxbow lake that fills in.

Investigation 7: Waterfalls

What to look for: The soft rock below is eroded faster (hydraulic action and abrasion in the plunge pool), undercutting the hard cap rock. The overhang collapses, and the waterfall retreats, leaving a steep-sided gorge.

Investigation 8: Channel efficiency A level

What to look for: R grows (deeper channels lose less to the bed) and n falls (smaller bed load). Both raise v = R^(2/3)S^(1/2)/n by more than the falling gradient lowers it.

Investigation 9: Spearman's rank A level

What to look for: With 10 sites, the critical value is 0.648. Pebble size against distance usually gives r_s near −0.9: a significant negative correlation. With fewer pebbles per site the result is weaker.

Batch experiment 1: When do grains start to move?

A U-shaped curve with its minimum at sand (about 0.1–0.5 mm), about 12% below Hjulström's line because gusts lift grains early. Clay is as hard to move as gravel.

Batch experiment 3: Does velocity increase downstream?

Yes: velocity rises from about 0.5 m/s near the source to over 1 m/s downstream, levelling off near the mouth. Deeper, smoother channels win over the gentler gradient.

Batch experiment 4: Are meanders about 11 widths long?

A straight line through the origin with gradient about 11: wide rivers make big meanders. The same rule holds from streams to the Mississippi (and even meltwater channels on glaciers).

A log–log line of gradient about 0.5: retreat ∝ √Q. Niagara retreated about 1 m a year before water was diverted for power; High Force only a few millimetres.

Batch experiment 6: Spearman's rank: how many pebbles per site?

With one pebble per site r_s is often not significant (noise beats the trend over 20 km); with 10 or more it is strongly negative. Sample size matters as much as the statistic.
