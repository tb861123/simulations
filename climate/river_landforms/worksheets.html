Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · When do grains start to move?

What this shows. For each grain size, 20 grains sit on the bed while the velocity slowly rises; each dot is the velocity when half of them have moved. The dots trace a U shape like Hjulström's erosion curve (yellow): sand (about 0.1–1 mm) moves first, boulders need fast water because they are heavy, and clay and silt need fast water because they are cohesive. The dots sit about 15% below the curve, because turbulent gusts are faster than the average flow and lift grains early: that is why Hjulström drew a band, not a line. They scatter a little because each grain is exposed differently. (The log–log gradient printed below means little here: a U-shaped curve has no single gradient.)

2 · Do pebbles get smaller downstream?

What this shows. At each site, 10 pebbles are picked at random and the median long axis is recorded. Size falls downstream from boulders to sand, because attrition (and abrasion) wear stones smaller and rounder as they travel. On a log y-axis the trend is a straight line: Sternberg's law, D = D₀e^(−ax), where a is the attrition rate. The dots scatter by about ±25% because pebbles at any site vary a lot; measuring more pebbles (try 50 in the table) shrinks the scatter, and the mean of 20 groups lies close to the line. Real rivers also gain coarse stones from tributaries and valley sides, which breaks up the trend.

3 · Does velocity increase downstream?

What this shows. Each dot is one group's float measurement (three timings over 10 m, × 0.85). Velocity rises downstream from about 0.5 m/s near the source to about 1.3 m/s, levelling off in the gentlest lowest reach, even though the gradient falls a lot: upstream the shallow water is slowed by friction with the boulders, downstream the deep, smooth channel is more efficient. The yellow line is the true mean velocity from Manning's equation; the measurements scatter by about ±5% because of reaction time and eddies; three timings per site keep it small. The hard-rock band at 12 km makes a fast, steep reach (the waterfall).

4 · Are meanders about 11 widths long?

What this shows. Straight rivers of different widths are left to meander until their sinuosity reaches 1.35; the distance down the valley from one bend to the next-but-one is the wavelength. Wider rivers make bigger meanders: the wavelength is about 11 times the width, the relationship Leopold and Wolman (1960) found from rivers all over the world (yellow). In this model every length scales with the channel width, so the ratio comes out the same for every width; it scatters from about 9 to 13 because the starting wiggles are random.

5 · Does more water mean faster retreat?

What this shows. Each run follows a waterfall over gritstone and shale for 3000 years. More water means more powerful hydraulic action and abrasion in the plunge pool, so faster undercutting and more frequent collapses. The retreat rate grows as the square root of discharge (gradient 0.5 on the log–log graph): a small upland stream retreats a few mm a year, a Niagara-sized river most of a metre. Runs scatter because collapses come in big random steps and wet and dry years differ; over a longer time the average settles on the theory.

6 · Spearman's rank: how many pebbles per site? (A level)

What this shows. Ten sites 2 km apart, measuring 1 to 30 pebbles at each and taking the median. Over a short stretch the real downstream fall in size is small compared with the natural variety of pebbles, so with one pebble per site rs is often weak and only sometimes passes the critical value of −0.648 (grey dashed): the result may not be significant. With 10 or more pebbles per site rs is usually below −0.8. Sample size matters: a survey that is too small can miss a real trend.
