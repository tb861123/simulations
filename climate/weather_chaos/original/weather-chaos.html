    How the model works

    Why weather is hard to predict

    The equations of the atmosphere are deterministic: the same start always gives the same future. But in a chaotic system two starts that differ by a tiny amount drift apart exponentially, roughly doubling their difference every fixed time (the error doubling time). We can never measure the atmosphere perfectly, so after some days the forecast is no better than guessing. Edward Lorenz found this in 1961 when a rounded number (0.506 instead of 0.506127) changed a whole simulated winter. It is often called the butterfly effect.

    Lorenz 63: convection in three numbers

    dx/dt = σ(y − x), dy/dt = x(ρ − z) − y, dz/dt = xy − βz with σ = 10, β = 8/3. x is the strength of the overturning of a heated layer of air, y the temperature difference between rising and sinking air, z how far the temperature profile departs from a straight line, and ρ the heating. Below ρ ≈ 24.7 the motion settles down; above it, it is chaotic and never repeats. The largest Lyapunov exponent at ρ = 28 is λ ≈ 0.906 per time unit, so a small difference grows like eλt.

    Lorenz 96: a ring of weather stations

    dXk/dt = (Xk+1 − Xk−2) Xk−1 − Xk + F, for stations k = 1…K arranged in a ring. The first term is advection (winds moving weather round), −Xk is friction, F is heating. It has no real geography but looks like waves drifting round a latitude circle. With F = 8 and 40 stations it is chaotic with λ ≈ 1.7 per time unit; one time unit is about 5 days, which gives an error doubling time of about 2 days, close to the real atmosphere.

    Ensembles

    Forecasters run many forecasts from slightly different starts. If they stay together the forecast is confident; if they spread out it is not. The ensemble mean is a better forecast than a single run once the members have spread, because averaging cancels unpredictable details.

    Assumptions and simplifications

    • Both models are toys. They are not weather forecast models and use no real units or geography. The days shown for Lorenz 96 come from the 5-days-per-unit scaling above and for Lorenz 63 are only an illustration.
    • They are integrated with a fixed-step fourth-order Runge–Kutta method (step 0.01). Even the step size changes a long chaotic run in detail, which is itself the point.
    • "Forecast failed" means the two runs differ by more than the threshold you choose. There is nothing special about the number.
    • The Lyapunov exponent is an average growth rate. Over short times the growth fluctuates, so the measured horizons scatter around the theory line.
    • Once the difference is as big as the attractor it cannot grow further (saturation): the runs are then as unrelated as any two random states.
    • Real weather also has a gentler limit than this: the real atmosphere has many scales of motion, so the achievable forecast length is roughly two weeks however good the data.
