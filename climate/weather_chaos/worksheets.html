Weather Chaos and the Butterfly Effect · Teacher notesweather-chaos.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The butterfly effect

What to look for: They stay together for a while, then suddenly go different ways: a tiny difference has grown exponentially. After that the two 'forecasts' are as different as two random days.

Investigation 2: Better data, longer forecast?

What to look for: Only a few more time units: each 10× improvement adds the same small amount (ln 10 ÷ 0.906 ≈ 2.5 units). Perfect forecasts would need perfect data everywhere.

Investigation 3: An ensemble

What to look for: The blob stretches and smears over the whole attractor: individual forecasts become useless, but you can still say which states are likely (the attractor's shape: the climate). Weather services run about 50 forecasts like this every day.

Investigation 4: When does chaos start?

What to look for: Below about 24.7 the convection settles into a steady pattern and differences shrink: predictable. Above it, chaos. Weather is in the chaotic regime.

Investigation 5: The weather ring

What to look for: The error grows and spreads around the ring (with the 'wind'), until every station is wrong. With 1 time unit = 5 days, the error doubles in about 2 days, like the real atmosphere.

Investigation 6: Measuring the growth rate A level

What to look for: A straight line on a log scale means exponential growth: ln(separation) rises at about 0.906 per unit time (the Lyapunov exponent), so the error doubles every 0.77 time units.

Investigation 7: More forcing, faster chaos A level

What to look for: Stronger forcing (more heating) makes the 'weather' more turbulent: errors grow faster and forecasts fail sooner. At small F the ring is steady and predictable.

Batch experiment 1: Forecast horizon and starting error (Lorenz 63)

A straight line: each 10× better start adds only about 2.5 time units. Forecasts from some starting points last longer than others (the scatter).
Theory (ln(threshold ÷ δ) ÷ 0.906): -1: 4.32 · -2: 6.86 · -3: 9.4 · -4: 11.9 · -5: 14.5 · -6: 17 · -7: 19.6 · -8: 22.1
Result from a test run: -1: 3.67 ± 0.395 · -2: 5.63 ± 0.535 · -3: 8.11 ± 0.404 · -4: 11.5 ± 0.547 · -5: 14 ± 0.273 · -6: 16.6 ± 0.492 · -7: 19 ± 0.321 · -8: 21.3 ± 0.33 (mean ± SE, 15 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: How long does one forecast last?

A wide spread: some weather patterns are more predictable than others. Ensembles tell forecasters which kind of day they face.
Theory (average growth): 17
Result from a test run: mean 16.2, SD 1.63, SE 0.115 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 3: When does chaos switch on?

Below ρ ≈ 24.7 errors shrink (λ < 0): steady, predictable convection. Above it λ > 0: chaos. At ρ = 28, λ ≈ 0.9.
Theory (λ ≤ 0 below 24.74): 15: 0 · 20: 0 · 24: 0 · 26: – · 28: – · 32: – · 40: –
Result from a test run: 15: -0.375 ± 5.85e-5 · 20: -0.175 ± 0.0019 · 24: 0.665 ± 0.053 · 26: 0.821 ± 0.0301 · 28: 0.887 ± 0.0231 · 32: 0.987 ± 0.0192 · 40: 1.15 ± 0.00888 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Weather ring: forcing and growth rate

More forcing, more turbulence, faster error growth. At F = 8 errors double in about 0.4 time units: about 2 days if 1 unit is 5 days.
Result from a test run: 4: -0.182 ± 0.0882 · 6: 0.514 ± 0.117 · 8: 1.61 ± 0.391 · 10: 2.1 ± 0.112 · 12: 2.23 ± 0.078 · 16: 3.35 ± 0.373 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Weather ring: forecast length

About 2 extra days for each 10× better start: forecasts beyond about two weeks are out of reach.
Result from a test run: -1: 14.7 ± 0.925 · -2: 21.5 ± 1.67 · -3: 30.8 ± 1.7 · -4: 32.4 ± 1.59 · -5: 44.4 ± 2.6 · -6: 46.3 ± 1.53 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)
