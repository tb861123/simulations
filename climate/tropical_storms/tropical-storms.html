How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Global circulation is drawn, not calculated from the equations of the atmosphere: the cell edges sit at 0°, 30°, 60° and 90° for Earth and move with the ITCZ, which follows the Sun from about 2°S in January to 12°N in July (a zonal average; over land it moves further). At A level, the Hadley cell's edge = 30° × √(heating contrast) ÷ (spin) after Held and Hou (1980), and the rest of each hemisphere is split into equal cells. Real circulation is messier: monsoons, jet stream waves, land and sea.
  • Coriolis: a frictionless puck on a spinning sphere, dU/dt = fV + UV tanφ/a, dV/dt = −fU − U²tanφ/a with f = 2Ω sin φ (exact for that idealised case).
  • Energy budget: sunlight absorbed = 340 W/m² × (1 − 0.482 P₂(sin φ)) × (1 − albedo), albedo rising towards the poles; heat out = 236 − 70(sin²φ − ⅓) W/m². A simplified fit, annual means.
  • Climate graphs: rounded 1991–2020 averages from the sources shown; Cairo, Reykjavík and Utqiaġvik are approximate.
  • Storm strength: the wind V grows at 1.8% per hour × V × (1 − (V/Vpot)²) towards the potential intensity Vpot = 28.2 + 55.8e0.1813(SST − 30) m/s (DeMaria and Kaplan 1994), which falls to zero between 26 and 22 °C (illustrative). Vpot is cut by wind shear (to zero at 26 m/s), by dry air (each storm gets a random factor 0.25–1.05) and by cold water stirred up from below (2.5 °C × (V/70 m/s)² × 60 ÷ (warm-water depth + 20 m)). Growth also needs the Coriolis effect (none within 3° of the equator, full by 10°). A disturbance needs all four conditions to become a storm.
  • Over land: Kaplan and DeMaria (1995): the wind drops 10% at the coast, then V = 13.7 + (V₀ − 13.7)e−0.095t m/s (t in hours), faster over mountains (× 1.6).
  • Pressure and structure: central pressure from the wind (Atkinson and Holliday 1977, V = 6.7(1010 − p)0.644 knots); wind across the storm a modified Rankine vortex (rising inside the radius of maximum wind, falling as r−0.6 outside); pressure profile after Holland (1980). Eye size shrinks as storms strengthen (illustrative). The picture's clouds and arrows are drawn for clarity, not calculated.
  • A season: a stylised North Atlantic (coasts simplified). Sea temperature varies with latitude, season, a warm pool in the west, the Gulf Stream and the cold Canary Current; wind shear is low in the tropics at the peak of the season and higher with El Niño. About 60 disturbances a year (as from Africa) appear at random (a Poisson process peaking in September); each becomes a named storm with a chance set by the sea temperature, potential intensity, shear, latitude and depth of warm water. Storms are steered west by the trade winds and then north-east into the westerlies, with random wobbles; north of 36° over water below 24 °C they become post-tropical. Calibrated so today's average is about 14 named storms, 9 hurricanes and 3 major hurricanes (real: 14.4, 7.2, 3.2).
  • Warming: the simple setting keeps the 26.5 °C threshold; at A level 'rises with the tropics' raises the threshold by the warming and lets the potential intensity rise only about 2.5% per °C, closer to climate models. Neither includes changes in atmospheric stability, humidity or circulation, which models suggest cut storm numbers.
  • Storm surge: 1 cm per hPa below 1013 hPa (inverse barometer) plus wind set-up, stepped across a shelf of uniform depth: dη/dx = τ ÷ (ρg(h + η)), τ = ρair × 0.002 × (0.75V)². In the batch run the storm meets the coast at a random angle (SD 22°) and the tide is at a random point. Real surges also depend on the coastline's shape, waves, and the storm's size and speed.
  • The coast view: wind at the coast from the vortex as the storm passes at its forward speed; surge rising and falling around landfall with high tide at landfall; rain rate falling off 220 km from the centre, more over hills; landslides once 150 mm of rain has fallen on steep slopes; roofs go at 150–250 km/h depending on building quality. Illustrative: it shows the hazards, not a damage estimate. Real deaths and costs are given with sources.
  • Left out: storm size varying from storm to storm, eyewall replacement, the ocean's response in detail, extratropical storms (depressions) in detail, a drought model (drought IS in 1GA0 Topic 2, 2.7c and 2.8a: a short card on its causes and impacts sits under the climate graph, and the water budget and drought tab of Flooding and Hydrographs models a dry year), and the other Topic 2 content on climate change (see the linked simulations).
