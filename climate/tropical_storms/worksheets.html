Tropical Storms · Teacher notestropical-storms.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Why here? The global circulation

What to look for: The Sun heats the equator most. Air rises there (low pressure, cloud, heavy rain: the ITCZ), flows towards the poles high up, and sinks at about 30° (high pressure, clear skies, the great deserts). That loop is the Hadley cell. Air flowing back to the equator along the surface is turned by the Earth's spin and becomes the trade winds, blowing from the east: these carry tropical cyclones westwards. There are two more cells in each hemisphere, and the whole pattern shifts north and south with the seasons (move the Month slider).

Investigation 2: Reading a climate graph

What to look for: Singapore sits under the rising air of the ITCZ: hot all year (a range of only 2 °C) with over 2000 mm of rain. Cairo is under the sinking air of the subtropical high: almost no rain. London is in the westerlies, where depressions bring rain in every month and temperatures follow the seasons. Miami's wet summer and autumn are its hurricane season.

Investigation 3: The birth of a hurricane

What to look for: Over sea warmer than about 26.5 °C the air above is fed with heat and water vapour. The storm organises from a ragged cluster into spiral bands turning anticlockwise, and above about 33 m/s (74 mph) a clear, calm eye opens in the middle. It takes several days and thousands of kilometres of warm water. It is named when it becomes a tropical storm.

Investigation 4: What does a storm need?

What to look for: None. A tropical cyclone needs: sea warmer than about 26.5 °C (and warm to a depth of 50 m or so) to supply heat and moisture; low wind shear, so that the rising column of warm air is not tilted over and blown apart; and to be at least 5° from the equator, where the Earth's spin gives the inflowing air its twist (the Coriolis effect). That is why hurricanes form in a band between about 5° and 30°, in late summer.

Investigation 5: Inside the storm

What to look for: Air spirals in at the surface, picking up heat and moisture from the sea, and rises violently in the eyewall, a ring of towering cloud with the heaviest rain and the strongest winds. It flows out at the top. In the eye itself air sinks and warms, so it is calm and often clear. The pressure there is so low that the sea surface bulges upwards under it.

Investigation 6: Why does it curve?

What to look for: In the tropics storms are carried west by the trade winds, drifting slowly towards the pole. When they reach the edge of the subtropical high (the ridge, at about 30°N) they curve north, then north-east as the mid-latitude westerlies take over. Where the ridge sits decides whether a storm hits the Caribbean, the United States, or curves harmlessly out to sea. Forecasting the track is mostly forecasting the steering winds.

Investigation 7: Landfall

What to look for: Over land the storm loses its supply of warm, moist air, and the rough ground slows the wind. The winds roughly halve every half day or so. But the rain goes on: the worst flooding is often hundreds of kilometres inland, days after landfall. Crossing a narrow island such as Cuba weakens a storm only briefly; it can strengthen again over the warm Gulf of Mexico.

Investigation 8: The storm surge

What to look for: Most hurricane deaths are caused by water, not wind. Onshore winds pile the sea up against the coast, most of all where the sea bed shelves gently (as in the Gulf of Mexico or the Bay of Bengal), and the low pressure lifts the sea further. A category 4 or 5 storm on a shallow coast can raise the sea by 6 to 9 m: Hurricane Katrina's surge reached 8.5 m in 2005.

Investigation 9: Rich coast, poor coast

What to look for: The hazard is the same; the disaster is not. Wealthier countries can afford forecasting, warnings, evacuation routes, sea defences and strongly built homes, so far fewer people die, though the cost of the damage in money is usually higher. Typhoon Haiyan (Philippines, 2013) killed over 6000 people; Hurricane Katrina (USA, 2005) about 1800 and cost over $100 billion; Cyclone Idai (Mozambique, 2019) over 1000.

Investigation 10: Three real storms

What to look for: Haiyan was the strongest and the deadliest, but the weaker Katrina cost over fifty times as much, and Idai, weaker still, killed nearly as many as Katrina in a far smaller population. Deaths depend heavily on development: warnings, evacuation, strong buildings, sea defences, and help afterwards. Cost in money is highest in rich countries because there is more expensive property to destroy, though the loss is a far larger share of income in poor ones. Physical factors matter too: Leyte Gulf funnelled Haiyan's surge, and New Orleans lies below sea level.

Investigation 11: A whole season

What to look for: The season peaks in August to October, when the sea is warmest. In an average Atlantic season there are about 14 named storms, of which 7 become hurricanes and 3 become major hurricanes (category 3 or more). The numbers vary a lot from year to year by chance alone, as well as with conditions such as El Niño, which raises wind shear over the Atlantic and suppresses storms.

Investigation 12: A warmer world

What to look for: Warmer seas raise the speed limit: the strongest storms get stronger, and a larger share reach categories 4 and 5. Whether there will be MORE storms is much less certain, and many studies expect about the same number or fewer. Warmer air also holds more moisture, so storms bring heavier rain, and a higher sea level makes every surge worse.

Investigation 13: The power of the wind A level

What to look for: The force of the wind on a building goes as the square of its speed, and the power it delivers as the cube. A category 4 hurricane has about (60/35)³ ≈ 5 times the destructive power of a category 1, and real damage data rise faster still, because once roofs fail everything inside is exposed. Small increases in peak wind matter a great deal.

Batch experiment 6: Storms in a season

Nothing about the climate changes from run to run here: the spread is pure chance. So one very active or very quiet season, on its own, says little about climate change.
Theory (real average 1991–2020): 7.2
Result from a test run: mean 7.08, SD 2.47, SE 0.174 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 7: Major hurricanes in a warmer world

The model keeps the number of disturbances and the wind shear the same. In the real atmosphere both may change with warming, which is why projections of storm numbers are uncertain while projections of the strongest storms' intensity are more confident.
Result from a test run: -1: 0 ± 0 · 0: 2.8 ± 0.319 · 1: 3.6 ± 0.367 · 2: 4.23 ± 0.392 · 3: 4.63 ± 0.385 (mean ± SE, 30 runs each)
