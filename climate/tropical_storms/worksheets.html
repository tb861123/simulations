Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · How many storms in a season?

What this shows. Each run is a whole season, 1 June to 30 November: thunderstorm clusters appear at random and each has a chance of growing into a named storm that depends on the sea temperature, wind shear, latitude and depth of warm water where it is. The yellow line is the expected number (the disturbances times their average chance), about 14, close to the real Atlantic average of 14.4. Seasons scatter widely, from under 8 to over 20, with an SD close to √14 ≈ 3.8: this is a Poisson distribution, what you get when many rare events happen independently. So one busy or quiet season on its own says little about climate change; you need many years of records.

2 · How strong can a storm get?

What this shows. A storm over open ocean for 10 days at each sea temperature. Warmer water gives more evaporation and more latent heat, so the strongest wind possible (the potential intensity, yellow) rises steeply: about 180 km/h at 26 °C to 340 km/h at 31 °C. But almost no storm reaches it (grey: the limit lowered by the average wind shear). Dry air drawn in, gusts of shear and cold water stirred up from below all hold storms back, so the dots scatter well below the line. Real hurricanes are the same: few reach their potential intensity.

3 · How fast do storms weaken over land?

What this shows. A Category 4 storm crosses the coast. Cut off from warm water, and slowed by friction, its wind falls quickly at first and then levels off: the Kaplan–DeMaria model, fitted to real US landfalls, has the wind dropping by about 60% in the first day. The dots scatter because some land is rougher (forest, hills, cities) and slows the storm faster. The storm's rain does not stop with the wind: inland floods often kill more people than the wind.

4 · How high is the storm surge?

What this shows. A storm crossing a shelf 100 km wide and 15 m deep. Low pressure alone raises the sea only about 1 cm per hPa (grey: under 1.2 m even at 900 hPa). Most of the surge is wind set-up: deeper storms have stronger winds (force ∝ wind²), which pile water against the coast, up to about 5 m here. The dots scatter by up to ±1 m because the tide (2 m range) can be high or low, and storms meet the coast at different angles; on average they sit about 20% below the yellow line, because storms rarely meet the coast exactly head-on and because, as water piles up, the sea gets deeper and the wind's push has less effect: the simple formula ignores both. Try a wider, shallower shelf (like the Gulf of Mexico): the surge rises sharply.

5 · Slow storms, more rain?

What this shows. A rain area 200 km in radius raining 8 mm an hour passes over towns at random distances from the track. The rain at a town = rain rate × the time it spends under the rain, and that time = distance across ÷ speed. Halve the speed and the rain doubles: the log–log gradient is −1. A storm that stalls is the most dangerous for flooding: Hurricane Harvey (2017) dropped over 1 500 mm on Texas, and the slow Cyclone Idai caused the deadly floods in Mozambique and Zimbabwe. The dots scatter because towns near the edge of the rain area get far less than towns on the track.

6 · Warmer seas, more storms? (A level)

What this shows. Whole seasons with the ocean warmed by 0 to 4 °C. With a fixed threshold of 26.5 °C, more of the ocean is warm enough and storms grow stronger, so the number of named storms climbs by about 70% at 2 °C (yellow, matched by the runs), then levels off once almost every disturbance in the region can grow. But that is not what climate models predict: they suggest about 14% fewer storms at 2 °C (grey), with more of them very intense and wetter. The reason is that the whole tropical atmosphere warms too, so the sea must be warmer than before to start a storm. Change the threshold option to 'rises with the tropics' and run again: the rise almost disappears. A simple model can be badly wrong when it leaves out how the whole system changes.
