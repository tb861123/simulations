How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Plate boundaries are drawn, not calculated: shapes, depths and speeds are typical values (plate speeds approximate). Earthquakes are random, with sizes from Gutenberg–Richter (b = 1) and depths typical of each boundary: shallow at ridges and transform faults, a wide shallow zone at collisions, and along the top of the sinking slab (the Benioff zone, 30° under the Andes, 45° under Japan) at subduction zones. Magnetic reversals happen at random (about every 300 000 years on average). The transform fault shows elastic rebound: strain builds steadily and is released when it passes a random breaking point.
  • Seismic waves: one uniform crust with P waves at 6.0 km/s and S waves at 3.5 km/s, so distance = 8.4 km × (S − P time). The real Earth has layers and curved paths. Seismograms are drawn shapes with the right arrival times, not calculated waveforms.
  • Magnitude and intensity: energy = 101.5M + 4.8 J; intensity I = 3.67 + 1.17M − 3.19 log₁₀(distance to the focus in km) (Bakun and Wentworth 1997, fitted to California), +1 on soft ground, felt reports ±0.5. Other regions attenuate differently.
  • How often: the catalogue draws M4.5+ quakes at the USGS world rate (about 1 469 a year of M5+) with b = 1; the b-value is estimated with Aki's (1965) formula.
  • Tsunami: the linear shallow-water equations on a line through the ocean (a staggered grid; the quality setting changes the number of grid cells in the live view only). The sea floor's uplift (about 1.6 × 100.5(M − 8.5) m) and width are rough scalings. There is a wall at the coast, so the coastal height includes the reflected wave; real run-up, flooding inland and the spreading of the wave in two dimensions are left out.
  • Volcanoes: viscosity rises with silica (illustrative); eruptions are explosive when sticky magma holds gas, or when magma meets ice. Tephra volume = eruption rate × time × 2.5; VEI from the volume (effusive eruptions capped at 1). The column is a buoyant plume (Morton, Taylor and Turner 1956: entrainment 0.1, wind entrainment 0.25, uniform stability) with its strength set to match Mastin et al. (2009) at 10 000 m³/s. Ash fall and lava flow lengths are illustrative.
  • Risk: 40 districts at random within the city; intensity from Bakun–Wentworth, 30% on soft ground; a district's buildings collapse with chance Φ((I − (10.5 − 3V)) ÷ 0.8); deaths = people × collapses × 0.6 (indoors) × 0.075 ÷ capacity (at most 0.5). The case studies' vulnerability and capacity values are our illustrative choices, set so the model's deaths are of the right size (within a factor of about two). The Park model curve is an illustrative shape. Real disasters depend on far more: time of day, aftershocks, fires, landslides, tsunamis, governance and aid.
  • Left out: the global distribution of boundaries on a map, hot spots in detail, landslides and liquefaction modelled physically, earthquake prediction (not possible), trends in disasters since 1960 and the wider management framework.
