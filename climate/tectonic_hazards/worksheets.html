Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · How many big earthquakes a year?

What this shows. Each run is one year: about 1 469 earthquakes of M5 or more happen worldwide (USGS average), and with b = 1 each has a 1-in-100 chance of reaching M7. The mean comes out near 14.7 a year (yellow); the USGS average for M7+ is about 16. Years scatter from about 5 to over 25, with an SD close to √14.7 ≈ 3.8: a Poisson distribution, as for any rare, independent events. So a year with 20 big quakes does not mean the Earth is 'more active'.

2 · Finding the distance from S − P

What this shows. A seismometer times the P and S arrivals; distance = 8.4 km/s × (S − P time). The estimates follow the true distance (yellow line, gradient 1) but scatter: arrivals are read to about ±0.3 s (±2.5 km), and real rock varies, so the wave speeds differ by a few % from place to place (the error grows with distance: about ±7% at 600 km). That is why seismologists use many stations and models of the Earth's layers.

3 · Does shaking fall with distance?

What this shows. Felt reports from towns at different distances from an Mw 7.0 quake 15 km deep. Intensity falls steadily with the logarithm of distance (Bakun and Wentworth, grey on rock; yellow including the average ground effect). Reports at the same distance scatter by about one intensity level: towns on soft sediment shake more (Mexico City 1985 was 350 km from its epicentre), and building types and how people describe shaking vary. Close to the epicentre the intensity levels off, because even directly above it the focus is 15 km down.

4 · How fast is a tsunami?

What this shows. A tsunami is released into an ocean of even depth and timed between two tide gauges 400 km apart, using the shallow-water wave equations step by step. The speed matches c = √(g d) almost exactly: about 36 km/h in 10 m of water, 700 km/h over 4 000 m, and the log–log gradient is 0.5. The runs do not scatter at all, though each starts with a different-sized wave: the speed depends only on the depth. That is what lets warning centres predict arrival times across whole oceans within minutes.

5 · Bigger eruptions, higher columns

What this shows. A buoyant column of hot gas and ash rises through stable air, mixing in air as it goes, until its upward speed runs out. The model's height grows as the eruption rate to the power ¼ (the theory of buoyant plumes), close to Mastin's fit to real eruptions (yellow, power 0.241): a thousand times more magma only gives about 5–6 times the height. Wind bends the column over and mixes in more air, so runs scatter below the line, most for small eruptions (a weak plume is easily bent): that makes the measured log–log gradient steeper, about 0.29. Big eruptions punch through the wind and match the fit. The model ignores the stratosphere's extra stability.

6 · Does capacity reduce deaths? (A level)

What this shows. The Haiti earthquake (Mw 7.0, 25 km from a city of 2.5 million, very vulnerable buildings) with different capacities to cope: faster rescue, better hospitals, more preparation. Deaths fall roughly in proportion to 1 ÷ capacity (log–log gradient about −1), as the risk equation R = H × V ÷ C says, until at very low capacity almost everyone trapped dies (the curve flattens). Runs scatter because which districts sit on soft ground and how strongly each shakes varies. In reality capacity and vulnerability are linked: richer, better-governed places have both better buildings and better response.
