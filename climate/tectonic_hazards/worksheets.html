Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Where do earthquakes happen?

What to look for: Constructive and conservative boundaries have only shallow earthquakes (less than about 20 km). At a destructive boundary the earthquakes follow the sinking oceanic plate down into the mantle, some as deep as 700 km: a sloping band called the Benioff zone. Collision zones have shallow to intermediate quakes spread over a wide area.

Investigation 2: Why volcanoes form where they do

What to look for: Volcanoes grow where the sinking slab reaches about 100 km depth: there, water driven out of the slab lowers the melting point of the mantle above, and magma rises. A steeper slab reaches 100 km sooner, so the volcanoes are closer to the trench. At constructive boundaries magma rises where the plates pull apart.

Investigation 3: Elastic rebound

What to look for: The plates are locked at the fault, so the rock on each side bends (stores elastic strain) as the plates keep moving. When the strain is too great, the fault slips suddenly and the rock springs back: an earthquake. The fences are left offset. The 1906 San Francisco earthquake moved fences by up to 6 m.

Investigation 4: P waves and S waves

What to look for: P (primary) waves travel at about 6 km/s, S (secondary) waves at about 3.5 km/s, so the further away a station is, the more the S wave lags. Each second of S − P gap means about 8.4 km of distance. Surface waves arrive last and shake the most.

Investigation 5: Find the epicentre

What to look for: Distance = S − P time × 8.4 km/s. One station tells you how far, but not which direction: a circle. Two circles cross at two points; the third picks the right one. That is triangulation, used by seismologists since the early 1900s.

Investigation 6: A tsunami crosses the ocean

What to look for: In 4000 m of water the wave travels at √(9.81 × 4000) ≈ 200 m/s (720 km/h, as fast as a jet) but is less than a metre high, so ships do not notice it. On the shallow shelf it slows down, the back catches up with the front, and the wave grows several times taller.

Investigation 7: Close to the source

What to look for: Only about 20–30 minutes, because the earthquake was so close to the coast. Japan's warnings, sea walls and evacuation drills saved many lives, but some walls were overtopped. Distance from the source is a huge part of the risk.

Investigation 8: Haiti and Chile

What to look for: Chile's earthquake released about 500 times more energy, yet killed far fewer people. Haiti's buildings were weak (high vulnerability), its government and emergency services had little capacity, and the quake was shallow and close to the capital. Disaster risk = hazard × vulnerability ÷ capacity: the hazard is only part of it.

Investigation 9: Measure the Benioff zone A level

What to look for: The quakes deeper than 60 km lie along the top of the sinking slab. The gradient of depth against distance is tan(angle): the fitted angle matches the slab angle you set, within a few degrees. Old, cold, dense ocean plates sink more steeply (like the Mariana trench); young warm ones sink at a shallow angle (like Chile).

Investigation 10: Soft sediment A level

What to look for: Soft, wet sediment amplifies shaking (about one intensity level here) and can liquefy, as in Christchurch 2011 and Mexico City 1985 (the old lake bed). Land-use zoning that keeps important buildings off soft ground lowers vulnerability.

Batch experiment 1: How often do big earthquakes happen?

A straight line: each step of one magnitude has ten times fewer earthquakes. The world has about 13 000 quakes of magnitude 4–4.9 a year, about 15 of 7 or more and about 1 of 8 or more (USGS).

Batch experiment 6: Does stronger building save lives?

Deaths rise steeply with vulnerability: the same hazard becomes a catastrophe where buildings are weak. Building codes, and enforcing them, are the most effective way to cut earthquake deaths.
