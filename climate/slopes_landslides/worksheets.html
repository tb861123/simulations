Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · How steep can wet sand stand?

What this shows. Each run fills a box with sand (friction angle about 34°, a little different in each sample) and tips it until it slides. Dry sand stands up to its friction angle, the angle of repose (grey dashed line). As the water table rises, pore water pressure pushes the grains apart and the angle falls along the yellow theory line, tan β = (1 − mγ_w/γ) tan φ′: saturated sand slides at only about 18°. Runs scatter by about ±1.5° because sand samples differ (wet runs scatter a little less, because the friction is multiplied by a smaller number).

2 · Do roots stop landslides?

What this shows. A strip of hillside (34°, soil 1.5 m deep, 2400 mm of rain a year) through 200 years of random daily rain. Roots that reach the base of the soil add strength: about 1 kPa halves the number of landslides, and 3 kPa (woodland roots) stops almost all of them. The yellow line counts only single days with enough rain on their own; the model gives more slides because soil already wet from earlier days needs less extra rain (antecedent rainfall), as before Holbeck Hall in 1993. Runs scatter because each 200 years of weather is different.

3 · How long until an undercut cliff fails?

What this shows. Waves cut back the foot of a 60 m till cliff by a random amount each year (stormy years more). The slope model finds the critical undercut, about 27 m here, at which the factor of safety falls below 1. The time to failure is that distance ÷ the erosion rate: the gradient of the log–log graph is −1 (double the erosion, half the time). Runs scatter because storms come in clusters; fast erosion rounds up to whole years, so the means sit slightly above the line.

4 · Does chemical weathering double every 10 °C?

What this shows. Each run is five granite tablets left out for 10 years and weighed. Hydrolysis speeds up with temperature (the Arrhenius law, activation energy 55 kJ/mol): on the log scale the line is almost straight, rising about ×2.1–2.4 for every 10 °C, so a tropical granite rots about 8 times faster than one at 0 °C. Tablets scatter because each has different minerals and cracks; the standard error shrinks with more tablets. Try limestone: carbonation hardly speeds up, because cold water holds more CO₂.

5 · Where is freeze–thaw most active?

What this shows. Count the days in a year when the rock surface freezes and thaws. Places that are always frozen (−15 °C) or rarely freeze (+15 °C) have few cycles. The peak is where the temperature hovers around 0 °C for much of the year, here about −3 to +3 °C: UK mountains, and the edge of periglacial areas. The theory (yellow) adds up, for every day, the chance that its range straddles 0 °C; weather that lasts several days makes single years scatter more but leaves the mean on the line.

6 · Does soil creep faster on steeper slopes?

What this shows. Each run follows a soil grain for 10 years: every wetting or freezing heaves it up at right angles to the slope by a random amount (mean 2 mm); every drying or thawing it drops straight down. Each cycle moves it ε tan β downslope, so creep rises with tan β: about 7 mm a year at 10°, 23 mm a year at 30° here (real soil creep is about 1–10 mm a year at the surface). Runs scatter because each heave is different.
