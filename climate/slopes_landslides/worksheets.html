Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The tilt box

What to look for: About 34°, the sand's angle of friction (its angle of repose). With no cohesion, F = tan φ ÷ tan β, so the depth cancels out.

Investigation 2: Rain and landslides

What to look for: Water in the pores pushes the grains apart (pore water pressure), which reduces the friction holding them. The driving force hardly changes, so F falls below 1.

Investigation 3: Do trees hold slopes up?

What to look for: Roots add strength (cohesion) only if they cross the slip surface. Tree roots reach about 1.8 m, so they protect shallow slides but not deep ones.

Investigation 4: Where is freeze–thaw fastest?

What to look for: Freeze–thaw peaks near 0 °C, where the temperature crosses freezing most often (the Cairngorms). Chemical weathering is fastest where it is hot and wet (the Amazon): about twice as fast for every 10 °C.

Investigation 5: Undercutting a cliff

What to look for: The overhang hangs like a shelf; its bending stress grows with the overhang squared. When it beats the rock's strength it falls as a rockfall. The debris then protects the foot for a while until the waves clear it.

Investigation 6: Soil creep

What to look for: Each cycle the soil heaves up at right angles to the slope and drops straight down, so it moves h tan β downhill. The top moves most, so posts and walls lean downhill and terracettes form.

Investigation 7: The Rest and Be Thankful

What to look for: Slides follow intense, long-lasting rain that soaks the shallow soil. Heavier storms make them more frequent even if the yearly rainfall stays the same.

Investigation 8: Caine's threshold A level

What to look for: Caine (1980): I = 14.82 D^−0.39. Short storms need high intensity; long ones trigger slides at lower intensity because the total rain is what raises the water table.

Investigation 9: Factor of safety A level

What to look for: F = [c + (γ − mγw)z cos²β tan φ] ÷ [γz sin β cos β]. Here F is about 1.0: on the edge of failing.

Batch experiment 1: Tilt box: at what angle does dry sand slide?

The sand slides at its angle of friction (angle of repose), whatever its depth, because with no cohesion F = tan φ ÷ tan β.
Theory (angle of friction): 33.8
Result from a test run: mean 34.1, SD 1.17, SE 0.151 from 60 runs

Batch experiment 2: How does water change the safe angle?

A saturated slope is safe only up to about half the dry angle: pore water pressure takes away half the friction. This is why landslides follow heavy rain.
Theory (tan β = (1 − mγw/γ) tan φ): 0: 33.8 · 0.2: 31.1 · 0.4: 28.3 · 0.6: 25.3 · 0.8: 22.1 · 1: 18.8
Result from a test run: 0: 34.2 ± 0.437 · 0.2: 31.6 ± 0.341 · 0.4: 28.4 ± 0.287 · 0.6: 24.9 ± 0.28 · 0.8: 22.2 ± 0.465 · 1: 19.2 ± 0.365 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Do roots only help if they reach the slip surface?

Nothing changes until the roots cross the slip surface; then F jumps above 1. Trees protect against shallow slides, not deep-seated ones.
Theory (infinite slope): 0: 0.908 · 0.5: 0.908 · 1: 0.908 · 1.25: 0.908 · 1.75: 1.32 · 2: 1.32 · 2.5: 1.32 · 3: 1.32
Result from a test run: 0: 0.908 ± 0.00459 · 0.5: 0.905 ± 0.00636 · 1: 0.922 ± 0.0143 · 1.25: 0.926 ± 0.0116 · 1.75: 1.32 ± 0.0189 · 2: 1.31 ± 0.0149 · 2.5: 1.33 ± 0.0113 · 3: 1.32 ± 0.0169 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Does chemical weathering double every 10 °C?

Each 10 °C doubles the rate (5 → 15 → 25 °C: ×2, ×4), so hot, wet places like the Amazon weather deeply.

F falls with the notch depth squared: the cliff falls when the notch is about 6 m deep, then the cycle starts again.

Batch experiment 8: Landslides and rainfall

Few slides in drier years, many more as rainfall rises: the heavy wet days that push the water table up become more common.
