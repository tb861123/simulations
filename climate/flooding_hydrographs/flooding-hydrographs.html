How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • A bucket model, run every 15 minutes. The basin is one hillslope repeated: leaves (interception store, 0.5 mm on grass up to 2.7 mm under full woodland), soil (holds 100 mm, or 150 mm over permeable rock; field capacity 45%), rock (groundwater), plus roofs and roads. Rain fills them in that order; water moves between them by the flows named on the hillslope picture.
  • Infiltration: the soil can soak up f = fc + (f0 − fc)(1 − θ)1.5 mm/h, where θ is how full it is: 15 mm/h when dry on clay (40 on chalk), more under woodland (+50 × woodland share), only 3 mm/h (12 on chalk) when wet. Rain beyond that, or any rain on a saturated soil, becomes overland flow.
  • Throughflow drains the water above field capacity: q = (Smax − Sfc) ÷ k × w2.5, with k = 10 × (2 ÷ drainage density) ÷ √(slope ÷ 4) hours. Percolation into the rock: up to 3 mm/h (permeable) or 0.12 mm/h (impermeable). Groundwater flow (base flow) = G ÷ kg, kg = 1200 h (chalk) or 300 h.
  • Getting to the gauge: overland flow first runs 1 ÷ (2 × drainage density) km to a stream at 0.02 + 0.06√slope m/s (slower under trees). Channels carry water at 0.35 + 0.65√slope m/s, faster in urban basins (drains, culverts) and dredged channels. The main river is L = 1.5 A0.6 km long (Hack's law; × 0.75 round, × 1.35 elongated basins). The basin's shape sets how arrivals are spread over the channel travel time (a "time–area" curve: peaky for round basins, flat for long ones).
  • Discharge: Q (m³/s) = flow (mm/h) × area (km²) ÷ 3.6. Lag time = time of peak discharge − middle of the hour with the most rain. Runoff coefficient = storm flow (overland + throughflow) ÷ rainfall.
  • Bankfull: the town's channel carries the 2-year flood (median annual flood) of the basin with its usual land use: rivers tend to shape their channels to about that size. Above it the river floods its flood plain; the share of the flood plain's homes flooded rises with the overflow (illustrative). Each flooded home costs £30 000 (illustrative, of the order of insurance claims).
  • Flood management: a dam catches the quick flow of the top 35% of the basin, with room for 50 mm; a relief channel takes up to 0.8 × bankfull round the town; embankments raise the channel's capacity to 1.7 × bankfull (but do not slow the water); dredging and straightening give +25% capacity and +25% speed (water reaches places downstream sooner); natural flood management (leaky dams, ponds, wetlands) holds back the quick flow from half the basin, with 10 mm of room, releasing it over about 8 h, and slows channels by 15%; SuDS hold half the urban runoff in 25 mm of storage; zoning keeps 60% of flood-plain plots free of homes; warnings cut the damage per home by a third (illustrative).
  • Rational method (theory line in the batch run): Q = C i A ÷ 3.6, with C from land use (roofs 0.95, SuDS 0.5) and soil wetness, and i the average rain rate over the time of concentration. It is a rule of thumb for small basins and steady rain.
  • Flood frequency: each year's biggest storm is drawn from a Gumbel distribution with the basin's 2-year storm depth (Boscastle 45 mm in 5 h, the Eden 60 mm in 36 h …, illustrative); the 100-year storm is 2.2 × the 2-year storm. Recurrence interval = (n + 1) ÷ rank.
  • Water budget (A level): one day at a time, rain on rain days chosen at random (average monthly rain and rain days from approximate Met Office 1991–2020 averages); actual evapotranspiration = PE while the soil is more than 60% full, less when drier; a full soil passes on surplus (80% to groundwater on chalk, 25% on clay).
  • Left out: rain varying across the basin, snow and snowmelt, frozen ground, tides (tide-locking made the Somerset Levels flood worse), river channels overflowing upstream and storing water on flood plains, debris blocking bridges (it made Boscastle worse), sewers overflowing, and sediment. Real flood estimates use the Flood Estimation Handbook (UKCEH), not this model.
