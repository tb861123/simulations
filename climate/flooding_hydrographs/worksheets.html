Flooding and Storm Hydrographs · Teacher notesflooding-hydrographs.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Read a storm hydrograph

What to look for: Water takes time to reach the gauging station: overland flow arrives first, then throughflow, while rain from the far end of the basin is still travelling down the channels. The lag time here is about 7 hours. After the peak, the falling limb is gentler than the rising limb because throughflow and groundwater keep draining for days. The shaded part at the bottom is base flow from groundwater.

Investigation 2: Urbanisation

What to look for: Roofs and roads are impermeable: rain cannot infiltrate, so almost all of it runs off at once, and drains carry it to the river quickly. The peak is much higher and arrives sooner: a flashy hydrograph. That is why towns flood after storms that a rural basin would absorb, and why new estates must include SuDS (sustainable drainage).

Investigation 3: Deforestation

What to look for: Trees intercept rain (some evaporates from the leaves), their roots open up the soil so more infiltrates, and the deeper soil stores more. Remove them and more rain reaches the ground and runs off quickly. The peak rises and the lag shortens. Replanting upland woods is part of natural flood management.

Investigation 4: Saturated ground

What to look for: Dry soil soaks up most of the rain. Saturated soil cannot: once it is full, rain runs over the surface (saturation-excess overland flow). The same storm gives a much bigger flood. Antecedent conditions (what the weather was like before the storm) matter as much as the storm itself.

Investigation 5: Boscastle, 2004

What to look for: The peak is about 130–140 m³/s within 2–3 hours of the heaviest rain: a flash flood. Steep slopes, thin soils on slate, a small basin and an extreme rainfall rate meant almost no lag. In reality, debris jammed against the bridge made it worse.

Investigation 6: Hard engineering

What to look for: A reservoir stores water and lets it out slowly: the peak falls and is delayed. Embankments do not change the river's flow at all; they make the channel deeper so more water fits before it floods (here, 60% more). Both are expensive, and if defences are overtopped (as at Carlisle in 2015) the flooding can be worse because people feel safe.

Investigation 7: Soft engineering

What to look for: Each slows water down or stores it, so the peak is lower and later. They are cheaper and better for wildlife than concrete, but each does a little: they work best together, and across a whole basin. Pickering's 'Slowing the Flow' project is a UK example.

Investigation 8: Permeable chalk

What to look for: Chalk is permeable: water percolates into the rock and reaches the river slowly as groundwater (base flow). Storm peaks are small and the river flows steadily even in dry spells. But after a very wet winter the groundwater itself can rise and cause long-lasting groundwater floods.

Investigation 9: How often do floods come?

What to look for: A 1-in-10-year flood has a 10% chance every year. Some decades have none, others two or three. Over a century you get about 10 on average, but anywhere from about 4 to 16 is normal. The recurrence interval is a probability, not a timetable.

Investigation 10: Infiltration excess or saturation excess? A level

What to look for: Intense rain on dry ground can fall faster than the soil can absorb it: infiltration-excess (Hortonian) overland flow, typical of thunderstorms and compacted or baked soils. Long rain on wet ground fills the soil to the surface: saturation-excess overland flow, typical of British winters. Both make the hydrograph flashier.

Investigation 11: The Somerset Levels A level

What to look for: Very gentle slopes and a large, round basin give a slow, long flood: the river stays above bankfull for weeks rather than hours. The water has nowhere to go quickly (in reality high tides also locked the rivers out of the sea). Dredging helps a little by speeding the channel, but cannot remove a month's rain.

Batch experiment 1: Does building on the basin raise the peak?

The peak rises steeply as fields become roofs and roads: impermeable surfaces turn almost all rain into fast runoff, and drains speed it to the river.
Result from a test run: 0: 8.73 ± 0.795 · 10: 12.7 ± 0.916 · 20: 20.7 ± 1.14 · 30: 29.8 ± 1.75 · 40: 39.1 ± 1.35 · 50: 48 ± 1.63 · 60: 54.4 ± 2.02 · 70: 65.6 ± 2.12 · 80: 77.2 ± 2.5 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Does a bigger basin mean a longer lag?

Bigger basins have longer rivers, so water from the far end takes longer to arrive: the lag grows from a few hours to more than a day.
Result from a test run: 5: 2.25 ± 0 · 25: 3.08 ± 0.0833 · 100: 5.33 ± 0.167 · 250: 8.58 ± 0.0833 · 500: 12.7 ± 0.167 · 1000: 19.3 ± 0.144 · 2000: 29.2 ± 0.0833 · 3000: 36.8 ± 0.167 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Do trees reduce the flood?

More trees, lower peaks: interception, better infiltration and deeper soils hold more of the storm back.
Result from a test run: 0: 14 ± 0.721 · 20: 12.5 ± 0.488 · 40: 12 ± 0.466 · 60: 10.8 ± 0.38 · 80: 10.8 ± 0.806 · 100: 11.3 ± 0.629 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Does wet ground make floods worse?

The same storm gives a far bigger flood on wet ground: antecedent conditions.
Result from a test run: 0: 9.24 ± 0.499 · 0.2: 9.74 ± 0.466 · 0.4: 10.7 ± 0.484 · 0.6: 13.9 ± 0.969 · 0.8: 20.7 ± 1.57 · 1: 25.8 ± 2.53 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: How long do you wait for a 1-in-20-year flood?

Most waits are shorter than 20 years, but a few are very long: the average is 20. A flood last year does not make one less likely this year.
Theory (theory): 20
Result from a test run: mean 20.9, SD 20.1, SE 0.898 from 500 runs

Batch experiment 6: How many 10-year floods in a century?

About 10 on average, but 5 or 15 in a century is not unusual. Recurrence intervals are averages over a very long time.
Theory (theory): 10
Result from a test run: mean 9.86, SD 3.12, SE 0.18 from 300 runs
