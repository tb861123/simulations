Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Does building on the basin raise the peak?

What this shows. 40 mm of steady rain in 12 hours on a 60 km² basin. As roofs and roads replace fields, the peak discharge rises steadily: impermeable surfaces send nearly all their rain to the river, fields only some of it, and drains get it there faster. The rational method (yellow, Q = CiA ÷ 3.6 with C from the land use) gets the rural basin and the trend about right because the rain lasts longer than the time of concentration (about 3 hours). It falls behind as the basin becomes more urban: it uses the AVERAGE rain rate, but roofs and roads pass on every gust at once, and the heaviest hour of a gusty storm is 1.3–1.5 × the average. The dots scatter because each of the five storms at each step is gusty in a different way.

2 · Does a bigger basin mean a longer lag?

What this shows. The same short storm falls on basins from 5 to 3000 km². Big basins have long rivers (length grows as area^0.6, Hack's law), so water takes longer to reach the gauge: the lag grows from under an hour to about 14 hours. On log axes the points lie close to a straight line with a gradient of about 0.5. The yellow line is an engineers' rule of thumb, lag ≈ 0.6 × time of concentration (the travel time from the far end); the model's lags are a little shorter for big basins because in an average-shaped basin most of the area is nearer the gauge than the far end. The lag is measured to the nearest quarter hour, so small basins show steps, and gusts change which hour has the most rain.

3 · How long do you wait for a 1-in-20-year flood?

What this shows. Each run starts a new record and counts the years until the first flood bigger than the 20-year flood. The average wait is about 20 years (the theory), but the spread is huge: many come within 5 years, some take 60 or more. The histogram is a geometric distribution, highest at 1 year: a '20-year flood' has a 5% chance every year, whatever happened last year. The SD is almost as big as the mean (√(1 − p) ÷ p = 19.5 years).

4 · How many 10-year floods in a century?

What this shows. In 100 years we expect 100 ÷ 10 = 10 floods bigger than the 10-year flood, and the average of 500 centuries is close to 10. But single centuries range from about 4 to 18: a binomial spread with SD √(100 × 0.1 × 0.9) = 3. Now tick 'Soil wetness varies each year' in the table: the count changes, because a big flood needs a big storm AND wet ground, so flood and rainfall return periods are no longer the same (joint probability).

5 · How long a record do you need?

What this shows. Each dot fits a Gumbel line to a record of annual floods and reads off the 100-year flood. With only 10–20 years of record the estimates scatter widely (an SD of 40% or more of the answer), because a short record may or may not happen to include a big flood. Longer records narrow the spread roughly as 1 ÷ √n, towards the value a 3000-year record gives (grey dashed). But that is still well below the true 100-year flood (yellow): bigger storms fall on wetter soil and run off more efficiently, so floods have a longer tail than the Gumbel curve assumes. A longer record cannot fix the wrong curve. Many UK gauges have only 30–60 years of record.

6 · Does the water budget balance? (A level)

What this shows. A year of London weather with less and more rain than average. The grey dashed line is the water balance, P − AE − abstraction − ΔS: it matches every dot exactly, because water is never created or destroyed. The yellow line is the surplus P − PE. In wet years runoff follows it, but in dry years runoff stays above it, because when the soil dries plants cannot evaporate as much as PE (a soil moisture deficit), so actual evapotranspiration falls. The dots scatter a little because the rain days differ from run to run.
