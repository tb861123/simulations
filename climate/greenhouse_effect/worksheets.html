Greenhouse Effect · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · How much warming per doubling of CO₂?

What this shows. Each run settles at a new balance temperature. Plotted against ln(CO₂) the points lie almost on a straight line, because each doubling of CO₂ traps the same extra 3.7 W/m². The gradient × ln 2 is the warming per doubling: close to 3 °C with water vapour feedback, the IPCC's best estimate (likely range 2.5–4 °C), compared with only about 1.1 °C with no feedback. The curve bends slightly upwards because the feedback gets a little stronger as the climate warms. Points scatter because random year-to-year variability is still there when the last 40 years are averaged.

2 · No atmosphere: is T = (S(1 − a)/4σ)^¼?

What this shows. With no air the ground must radiate exactly what it absorbs: σT⁴ = S(1 − a)/4. So T plotted against (S(1 − a)/4σ)^¼ is a straight line through the origin with gradient 1. For Earth (a = 0.3) that gives −18 °C: the greenhouse effect is why we have +14 °C instead. The tiny scatter comes from random variability.

3 · How sure are we? A climate sensitivity ensemble

What this shows. Nobody knows the feedback strength exactly, so each run draws it at random (mean 2.05, SD 0.3 W/m² per °C) — like a 'perturbed physics ensemble' of climate models. A symmetric spread in the feedback gives a lopsided spread in sensitivity, with a long tail to high values, because ΔT = 3.7/(λ₀ − f) blows up as f gets near λ₀. Compare the middle of the spread with the IPCC likely range (2.5–4 °C, shaded).

4 · Without feedback, is it only ~1.1 °C?

What this shows. With water vapour held fixed, doubling CO₂ adds 3.7 W/m² and the planet only has to warm until it radiates 3.7 W/m² more. That needs ΔT = 3.7/λ₀ ≈ 3.7/3.3 ≈ 1.1 °C (the 'no-feedback' or Planck-only sensitivity). Feedbacks roughly triple it. Runs differ because of the random year-to-year variability. The model's average comes out about 2% above the simple formula: the formula treats σT⁴ as a straight line near today's temperature, which is only an approximation. With 100 runs the standard error is so small that even this 2% shows up as many standard errors — a reminder that a 'theory' is often itself an approximation.

5 · Does a deeper ocean slow the warming?

What this shows. After CO₂ jumps from 280 to 560 ppm, more water takes longer to heat: the time to get 63% of the way is the 'response time' C/λ, which grows in proportion to depth (about 8 years per 70 m here). The final temperature does not depend on depth. The real deep ocean is ~4000 m, which is why some warming takes centuries to arrive.

6 · Stronger feedback, more warming?

What this shows. The warming for doubled CO₂ is ΔT = 3.7/(λ₀ − f). It rises slowly at first, then shoots up as the feedback f approaches the Planck response λ₀ ≈ 3.3 W/m² per °C. The model rises a bit faster than the simple formula at high f because its feedback strengthens as the planet warms. That is why small uncertainties in feedbacks matter so much.
