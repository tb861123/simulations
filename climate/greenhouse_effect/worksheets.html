The Greenhouse Effect · Teacher notesgreenhouse-effect.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: No air at all

What to look for: With no atmosphere the Earth would average about −18 °C: sunlight in = infrared out at that temperature. Pre-industrial Earth settles near 14 °C: the greenhouse effect adds about 32 °C. Without it, oceans would freeze. The greenhouse effect is natural and essential; the problem is making it stronger.

Investigation 2: Watch the packets

What to look for: Sunlight (short wavelength) passes through the air almost unaffected. The warm ground gives off infrared (long wavelength), which CO₂, water vapour and methane absorb. They re-emit it in random directions: some back down, warming the ground further. More gas, more infrared sent back down.

Investigation 3: More CO₂

What to look for: More CO₂ traps more infrared, so energy out drops below energy in. The Earth gains energy and warms until its extra infrared restores the balance. The oceans take a lot of heating, so this takes decades. Doubled CO₂ gives about 3 °C of warming here (the IPCC's best estimate is 3 °C).

Investigation 4: History and futures

What to look for: The model, driven only by rising CO₂ and pollution haze, follows the observed warming of about 1.2 °C to the 2020s. The futures differ hugely: fast cuts keep warming near 1.5–2 °C; high emissions give about 4 °C or more by 2100. The difference is our choices, not the physics.

Investigation 5: A volcano

What to look for: Volcanic haze (sulfate droplets) reflects sunlight: the albedo rises a little, and the Earth cools by about 0.3–0.5 °C for a year or two. Pinatubo (1991) did this. The haze falls out within a couple of years, so the effect does not last, unlike CO₂, which stays in the air for centuries.

Investigation 6: Venus and Mars

What to look for: Venus averages about 460 °C, hotter than Mercury, even though it absorbs less sunlight than the Earth. Its thick CO₂ atmosphere is a runaway greenhouse. Mars, with almost no greenhouse effect, is about −60 °C.

Investigation 7: Water vapour feedback A level

What to look for: Without the feedback about 1.1 °C; with it about 3 °C. Warmer air holds more water vapour (about 7% more per °C), which traps more infrared and warms further: a positive feedback. It is not a forcing because the amount of water vapour is set by temperature: extra water vapour rains out in days.

Investigation 8: Ocean depth and the lag A level

What to look for: Response time = heat capacity ÷ feedback parameter: proportional to depth. 70 m gives about 8 years. The real deep ocean adds a slow tail of centuries, so even if CO₂ stopped rising, some warming would still be 'in the pipeline'.

Batch experiment 1: How much warming per doubling of CO₂?

Each doubling (140 → 280 → 560 → 1120) adds about the same warming, about 3 °C: CO₂'s effect is logarithmic.
Theory (energy balance): 140: 10.4 · 200: 12.3 · 280: 13.9 · 400: 15.6 · 560: 17 · 800: 18.5 · 1120: 19.9
Result from a test run: 140: 10.3 ± 0.00954 · 200: 12.2 ± 0.0581 · 280: 14 ± 0.0256 · 400: 15.6 ± 0.0271 · 560: 16.9 ± 0.0483 · 800: 18.5 ± 0.0231 · 1120: 20 ± 0.0492 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Without feedback, about 1.1 °C?

About 1.1 °C per doubling: the warming CO₂ causes by itself. The feedback roughly triples it.
Theory (energy balance, no feedback): 140: 12.8 · 200: 13.4 · 280: 13.9 · 400: 14.5 · 560: 15 · 800: 15.6 · 1120: 16.1
Result from a test run: 140: 12.7 ± 0.00149 · 200: 13.4 ± 0.0292 · 280: 13.9 ± 0.0109 · 400: 14.5 ± 0.0139 · 560: 15 ± 0.0172 · 800: 15.6 ± 0.00578 · 1120: 16.1 ± 0.0249 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Stronger feedback, more warming?

The warming shoots up as the feedback approaches 3.3 W/m² per °C, the Planck response (how much extra infrared a warmer Earth gives off). Uncertainty in feedbacks is why the climate sensitivity is uncertain.
Theory (3.7 ÷ (3.32 − f)): 0: 1.11 · 0.5: 1.31 · 1: 1.59 · 1.5: 2.03 · 2: 2.8 · 2.5: 4.51
Result from a test run: 0: 1.11 ± 0.0178 · 0.5: 1.33 ± 0.0204 · 1: 1.59 ± 0.0339 · 1.5: 1.98 ± 0.00869 · 2: 2.72 ± 0.084 · 2.5: 4.06 ± 0.0548 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Climate sensitivity ensemble

A lopsided spread: a long tail of high values. A symmetric uncertainty in feedback gives an asymmetric uncertainty in warming, because the warming depends on 1 ÷ (3.32 − f). The IPCC's likely range is 2.5–4 °C.
Theory (IPCC best estimate): 3
Result from a test run: mean 3.34, SD 0.951, SE 0.0549 from 300 runs

Batch experiment 5: Albedo

A small change in albedo matters a lot: 0.01 is about 3.4 W/m², nearly a CO₂ doubling. That is why melting ice (dark ocean replacing white ice) is such an important feedback.
Theory (energy balance): 0.2: 38 · 0.25: 28.5 · 0.3: 15.8 · 0.35: 0.569 · 0.4: -12.1 · 0.45: -22.1
Result from a test run: 0.2: 38 ± 0.0239 · 0.25: 28.5 ± 0.0735 · 0.3: 15.7 ± 0.0601 · 0.35: 0.658 ± 0.0463 · 0.4: -12.1 ± 0.0492 · 0.45: -22.1 ± 0.0444 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: Does a deeper ocean slow the warming?

A straight line through the origin: twice the depth, twice the heat to store, twice as long. About 0.11 years per metre.
Theory (heat capacity ÷ feedback parameter): 10: 1.07 · 25: 2.68 · 50: 5.37 · 75: 8.05 · 100: 10.7 · 150: 16.1 · 200: 21.5
Result from a test run: 10: 1.14 ± 0 · 25: 2.82 ± 0 · 50: 5.66 ± 0 · 75: 8.48 ± 0 · 100: 11.3 ± 0 · 150: 17 ± 0 · 200: 22.6 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)

Batch experiment 7: Warming by 2100: three futures

Each path's runs agree to within a few tenths of a degree: natural variability is small compared with the difference between the paths. What happens depends on how much CO₂ we emit.
Result from a test run: 1: 1.79 ± 0.056 · 2: 3.04 ± 0.0464 · 3: 4.21 ± 0.0324 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)
