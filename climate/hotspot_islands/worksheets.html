Hotspot Island Chains · Teacher noteshotspot-islands.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Measure the plate speed

What to look for: Speed = distance ÷ age. For example 510 km ÷ 6.0 Myr = 85 km/Myr. Since 1 cm/yr = 10 km per Myr, that is 8.5 cm/yr: the speed you set. The fitted value uses every volcano at once (a line through the origin on the age–distance graph).

Investigation 2: Slow and fast plates

What to look for: A slow plate keeps each volcano over the plume for longer (the time over the plume is about 2σ ÷ v), so more magma reaches it and it grows taller. A new volcano still starts every 1.6 Myr, but the plate has moved less in that time, so the volcanoes are closer together: spacing = v × 1.6 Myr.

Investigation 3: Erosion or subsidence?

What to look for: Erosion (rain, rivers and waves) only works above sea level, and waves cut the top flat at sea level. Subsidence (the plate bending under the volcano and cooling as it moves away) carries the whole volcano down. A guyot needs both: planed flat by erosion, then sunk by subsidence.

Investigation 4: Follow a volcano's life

What to look for: Once extinct and worn down to sea level, the volcano keeps sinking, but coral grows upwards fast enough to stay at the surface: an atoll. The reef dies when it is carried past the reef limit into water too cool for coral (the Darwin point), or if the volcano sinks faster than the reef can grow. Then it becomes a guyot.

Investigation 5: From plume to lava

What to look for: Hot plume rock rises from deep in the mantle; as it rises the pressure falls, so a few per cent of it melts (decompression melting, at about 80–150 km). The magma is less dense, so it rises through cracks in the plate (the dashed conduits) and erupts, building a shield volcano above the plume.

Investigation 6: Compare with Hawaiʻi A level

What to look for: A line through the origin and the real volcanoes gives about 11 cm/yr (published estimates for the last few million years are about 8–10 cm/yr). They scatter because each Hawaiian island is built from several overlapping volcanoes, the dating has errors, the plume may not be perfectly still, and volcanoes do not form at perfectly regular times. Switch on the irregular plume to see the model scatter too.

Investigation 7: How fast do they sink? A level

What to look for: Young volcanoes sink at about 2–3 mm/yr because the plate bends (flexes) under their huge weight; this slows over about a million years. After that, slower sinking continues as the plate cools and contracts and moves off the hotspot swell (∝ √age). Real Hawaiʻi Island is sinking at about 2–3 mm/yr today.

Batch experiment 1: Reading the plate speed from the chain

The fitted speeds scatter round the true speed by a few per cent: each age has a dating error, but fitting many volcanoes at once averages much of it out. This is how the Pacific plate's speed over Hawaiʻi (about 9 cm/yr) was measured.
Theory (true speed): 3: 3 · 4.5: 4.5 · 6: 6 · 7.5: 7.5 · 9: 9 · 10.5: 10.5 · 12: 12
Result from a test run: 3: 2.98 ± 0.0455 · 4.5: 4.57 ± 0.0367 · 6: 6.04 ± 0.0635 · 7.5: 7.38 ± 0.106 · 9: 9.05 ± 0.0877 · 10.5: 10.5 ± 0.138 · 12: 12 ± 0.227 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Spacing of the volcanoes

Faster plates spread the volcanoes further apart. The scatter comes from the irregular timing of new volcanoes.
Theory (v × 1.6 Myr): 2: 32 · 4: 64 · 6: 96 · 8: 128 · 10: 160 · 12: 192
Result from a test run: 2: 32.5 ± 0.376 · 4: 64.3 ± 1.57 · 6: 96.2 ± 1.4 · 8: 133 ± 3.8 · 10: 161 ± 3.4 · 12: 202 ± 4.19 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Faster plate, smaller volcanoes

A slow plate keeps each volcano over the plume for longer (about 2σ ÷ v), so it grows taller, up to the 13 km limit above the sea floor in this model. Fast plates carry volcanoes off before they grow much.
Result from a test run: 2: 4890 ± 22 · 3: 4770 ± 17.9 · 4: 4720 ± 45.4 · 6: 4360 ± 46.4 · 8: 4090 ± 48.4 · 10: 3790 ± 76.1 · 12: 3350 ± 61.1 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: How long does an island last?

Faster sinking drowns the islands sooner. In the model, islands last a few million years; real Hawaiian islands last longer (Kauaʻi is still 1600 m high at 5 Myr), because the model is sped up to fit the life cycle into one run.
Result from a test run: 0.25: 8.92 ± 0.0239 · 0.5: 7.21 ± 0.00993 · 0.75: 5.98 ± 0.0422 · 1: 4.88 ± 0.025 · 1.25: 4 ± 0.0486 · 1.5: 3.28 ± 0.0241 · 2: 2.06 ± 0.012 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Erosion before drowning

Above about half the normal rate, roughly 2 km is worn away whatever the rate: erosion only works above sea level, so faster erosion just gets the island down to sea level sooner. Only very slow erosion leaves more of the work to sinking. Either way the top ends up at sea level, where waves plane it flat: the flat top of a guyot.
Result from a test run: 0.25: 1690 ± 18.1 · 0.5: 2040 ± 28.4 · 1: 2060 ± 43.3 · 1.5: 2030 ± 9.84 · 2: 2010 ± 38.2 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: Dating errors and the fitted speed

The fitted speeds form a narrow peak round 8.5 cm/yr. A real chain is only one run: its fitted speed could be a little off, which is why geologists combine several chains and methods.
Theory (true speed): 8.5
Result from a test run: mean 8.51, SD 0.236, SE 0.0192 from 150 runs
