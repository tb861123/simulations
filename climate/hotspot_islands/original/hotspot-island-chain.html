      Age vs distance from the hotspot

      Theory: if the plate moves at a steady speed v, age = distance ÷ v. The fitted speed is a least-squares line through the origin for volcanoes older than 0.3 Myr.

      Summit height vs age

      The model's islands wear down and sink faster than real Hawaiian islands (grey crosses), so the full life cycle fits in one loop. Real islands take roughly 10–30 Myr to reach the atoll stage (for example, Midway is about 28 Myr old).

      How the model works (and what it leaves out)

      The key idea. The plume stays still in the deep mantle while the plate slides over it. Each volcano is built while it sits above the plume, then the plate carries it away, so ages increase with distance from the hotspot.

      Rules used (a = age in Myr, x = distance from the plume centre in km):

      • Plate: every volcano moves at speed v. A new volcano is born above the plume centre every 1.6 Myr.
      • Growth: dH/dt = G·s·exp(−(x/σ)²)·(1 − H/Hmax), with G = 45 km/Myr, σ = 70 km and Hmax = 13 km. Growth stops once the volcano is carried off the plume.
      • Subsidence: S(a) = k·[F(1 − e^(−a/τ)) + C√a], with F = 2 km and τ = 1 Myr (the plate bends under the volcano's weight) and C = 0.8 km/√Myr (the plate cools and moves off the hotspot swell).
      • Erosion: dH/dt = −k_E·e·z for the part above sea level (k_E = 0.5 per Myr). Waves cut the top flat at sea level.
      • Coral reef: grows up to 0.5 mm/yr to stay at sea level once the volcano is extinct. It dies if it falls more than 100 m behind or is carried past the reef limit (680 km, illustrative), where the water is too cool.
      • Life cycle: active shield volcano → eroding island → atoll → guyot (flat-topped seamount).
      • Method: fixed steps of 0.01 Myr. The run is deterministic, so every loop is identical.

      Advanced. The plume is about 200–300 °C hotter than the surrounding mantle. As it rises, the pressure falls and a few per cent of the rock melts (decompression melting, at roughly 80–150 km depth). Slow plates give larger volcanoes because each one spends longer over the supply: the residence time is about 2σ ÷ v.

      Calculated vs assumed. Positions, ages and the fitted speed follow directly from the plate speed. Growth, erosion, subsidence and reef rates are illustrative and sped up. Real Hawaiian ages and distances (Clague & Dalrymple, 1987) are shown for comparison.

      What it leaves out:

      • Real mantle flow: the plume, particles and melting zone are drawn, not calculated.
      • Each Hawaiian island is really built from 2–5 overlapping volcanoes. Here, one volcano = one island, with fixed spacing in time.
      • Real new volcanoes start slightly upstream of the plume, for example Kamaʻehuakanaloa (formerly Lōʻihi).
      • Plume fixity and depth are debated. Palaeomagnetic evidence suggests the plume drifted while the Emperor chain formed. The 47 Ma Hawaiian–Emperor bend is not modelled.
      • Hotspot swell, giant landslides, rejuvenated volcanism, individual eruptions, sea-level change and climate.
      • The base of the plate is drawn flat. Heights above the scale break are exaggerated.
