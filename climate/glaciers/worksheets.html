Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Accumulation and ablation

What to look for: Above the ELA more snow falls than melts (accumulation); below it more melts (ablation). Ice flows from the accumulation zone to replace what melts below. In balance, the snout stays still while the ice moves.

Investigation 2: Warmer climate

What to look for: The ELA rises about 230 m (154 m per °C). The glacier shrinks back over decades to centuries until the ablation zone is small enough: several kilometres here.

Investigation 3: Moraines

What to look for: A terminal moraine marks the furthest advance. As the glacier retreats in stages, recessional moraines are left where the snout paused.

Investigation 4: Which way does a corrie face?

What to look for: North to north-east: shaded from the midday sun (less melting) and on the lee side of the prevailing south-westerly winds, so drifting snow builds up there.

Investigation 5: V to U

What to look for: A U shape: the ice drags hardest on the bed and lower sides, widening and deepening the valley. Spurs are cut off (truncated spurs) and tributary valleys are left hanging.

Investigation 6: Freeze–thaw

What to look for: Near 0 °C, where the temperature crosses freezing most often: water in cracks freezes, expands by 9%, and wedges the rock apart. It produces scree slopes below corrie back walls.

Investigation 7: Warm-based ice A level

What to look for: With meltwater at the bed, basal sliding adds to internal deformation: the glacier flows faster and becomes thinner and longer. Warm-based glaciers erode much more (abrasion and plucking need sliding).

Batch experiment 1: Does the snowline rise about 150 m per °C?

A straight line of gradient about 154 m per °C (the air cools 6.5 °C per km of height).
Theory (154 m per °C): -2: 992 · -1: 1150 · 0: 1300 · 1: 1450 · 2: 1610 · 3: 1760
Result from a test run: -2: 1000 ± 3.65 · -1: 1140 ± 5.79 · 0: 1300 ± 4.37 · 1: 1450 ± 7.15 · 2: 1610 ± 7.86 · 3: 1760 ± 4.24 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: How long is a glacier in a warmer climate?

About 2.5–3 km shorter per °C: a small warming makes a big change. The simple rule is within about 10%; it is less good for the longest glaciers, which reach the end of the valley in this model.
Theory (L = 2(b₀ − ELA + H̄)/s): -1: 19.7 · -0.5: 18.1 · 0: 16.6 · 1: 13.5 · 2: 10.4 · 3: 7.37
Result from a test run: -1: 17.8 ± 0 · -0.5: 16.8 ± 0 · 0: 15.6 ± 0 · 1: 13.2 ± 0 · 2: 10.6 ± 0 · 3: 7.8 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Which way should a corrie face?

Batch experiment 4: Does ice turn a V into a U?

It starts near 1 (a V) and reaches about 2 (a parabola, the U shape) after roughly 40 thousand years as the ice widens the floor and steepens the sides. Left longer, b keeps rising: a flat-floored trough.
Theory (parabola (U)): 0: 2 · 10: 2 · 20: 2 · 30: 2 · 40: 2 · 50: 2 · 60: 2
Result from a test run: 0: 1.11 ± 0 · 10: 1.27 ± 0 · 20: 1.27 ± 0 · 30: 1.62 ± 0 · 40: 1.99 ± 0 · 50: 2.37 ± 0 · 60: 2.93 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: When is freeze–thaw most active? (A level)

Most active within a few degrees of 0 °C (where both the daily and yearly cycles cross freezing). Very cold places stay frozen; warm places rarely freeze. Weather adds extra crossings.
