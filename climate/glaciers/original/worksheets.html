Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Does the snowline rise about 150 m per °C?

What this shows. Each run is a team measuring the equilibrium line with 10 stakes for 5 years and fitting a straight line to balance against height. The ELA rises in a straight line with temperature: the best-fit gradient is about 154 m per °C, because air cools by about 6.5 °C per km of height, so 1 °C of warming moves the 0 °C level (and the melting) up by 1 ÷ 0.0065 ≈ 154 m. Runs scatter by about ±30–40 m: each year's weather moves the ELA, and stake readings have errors. More stakes or more years shrink the scatter. Real glaciers show 100–150 m per °C, because snowfall changes too.

2 · How long is a glacier in a warmer climate?

What this shows. Each glacier is grown for 1200 years until it stops changing. Warmer climates give shorter glaciers: about 3 km shorter for each 1 °C on this slope (best-fit gradient −3.1 km per °C) (the ELA rises 154 m per °C and the bed falls 100 m per km, and the length changes twice as fast as the snout height). The yellow line uses the idea that a glacier's mean surface height settles at the ELA: L = 2(b₀ − ELA + H̄) ÷ s, with a mean ice thickness H̄ of 130 m. It is within about 7% for glaciers longer than 5 km; the smallest glacier (+3 °C) is thinner than 130 m on average, so it comes out about 25% shorter than the line. Real glaciers are not uniformly thick either. The grey dashed line ignores the ice thickness and is about 2.6 km too short: the ice itself raises the surface above the bed. Lengths are measured to the nearest 200 m grid step, so repeats often agree exactly.

3 · How quickly does a glacier respond? (A level)

What this shows. Each glacier starts in balance in a climate from −1 to +1.5 °C, then warms suddenly by 1 °C. The value is how long the snout takes to make 63% of its eventual retreat. Jóhannesson's rule, τ = thickness ÷ (−balance at the snout), predicts decades (yellow). The model's glaciers take about 20–50% longer, because the rule assumes the whole glacier thins at once, while really the thinning spreads up the glacier. Both say the same thing: valley glaciers take decades to catch up with the climate, so today's retreat is partly a delayed response to past warming.

4 · Which way should a corrie face?

What this shows. A corrie glacier's balance for each direction it could face, averaged over 30 years of random weather and wind. Slopes facing north to east keep their snow: they are shaded from the midday sun, and south-westerly winds drop blown snow on them. The theory (yellow) peaks at about 20–25° (north-north-east) with these settings. South- and west-facing hollows lose more than they gain, so no glacier forms and no corrie is cut: most UK corries face north to east. Runs scatter because winters differ.

5 · Does ice turn a V into a U?

What this shows. A rough V-shaped river valley (b ≈ 1, grey dashed) is filled with ice 700 m deep. The ice flows fastest far from the walls, so the drag on the bed is greatest on the lower valley sides, not at the very bottom: erosion widens the floor and steepens the sides. Within about 100 000 years of ice cover the shape exponent reaches about 2, a U-shaped parabola (yellow), and then it stays near 2.1–2.3 as the trough deepens without changing shape (after Harbor 1992). Real troughs were cut over many glacial cycles; runs differ slightly because each starting valley is a little different.

6 · When is freeze–thaw most active? (A level)

What this shows. Count the days in a year when the rock surface crosses 0 °C. Very cold places (−12 °C) freeze and stay frozen; warm places rarely freeze. Cycles peak where temperatures hover around 0 °C for much of the year: here about −3 °C to +4 °C, like UK mountains in winter or the edge of the periglacial zone. The theory (yellow) adds up, for every day, the chance that the day's temperature range straddles 0 °C; the counts agree within a few cycles. Runs scatter because each year's weather is different.
