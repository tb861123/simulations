Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Fetch and wind

What to look for: Higher and longer: wave height grows with the square root of fetch until the sea is 'fully developed'. Coasts facing a long fetch (like Cornwall facing the Atlantic) get the biggest waves.

Investigation 2: Constructive or destructive?

What to look for: Destructive waves are high and steep (steepness above about 0.025), break frequently and have a strong backwash that drags material away. Constructive waves are long and low with a strong swash that builds beaches.

Investigation 3: Headlands and bays

What to look for: Bays are cut into the soft rock, leaving hard-rock headlands. Waves refract (bend) and concentrate their energy on headlands, while bays become sheltered and grow beaches.

Investigation 4: Cave, arch, stack, stump

What to look for: Hydraulic action and abrasion widen a crack into a cave; caves on both sides meet to make an arch; the arch roof collapses leaving a stack; the stack is eroded at its base to a stump (like Old Harry and his Wife, Dorset).

Investigation 5: Longshore drift

What to look for: Swash carries it up the beach at the angle of the waves; backwash pulls it straight down the slope. It zig-zags along. 45° gives the biggest sideways step (½ × swash length × sin 2θ).

Investigation 6: Holderness and groynes

What to look for: The groynes trap drift and protect Mappleton, but starve the beaches downdrift, so the cliffs south of the groynes retreat faster: terminal groyne syndrome.

Investigation 7: The Bruun rule A level

What to look for: R = S L/(B + h): about 54 m per metre of rise here, so 27 m. The whole beach profile moves up and back. Real coasts vary a lot (sediment supply, defences).

Investigation 8: Wave power A level

What to look for: P = ρg²H²T/(64π) ≈ 155 kW per metre. Doubling the height quadruples the power, which is why storms do most of the erosion.

Batch experiment 4: How fast does Holderness retreat?

About 1.8 m a year, like the measured average: soft till, a thin beach and strong drift that carries the sand away south.
