Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Do waves grow with fetch?

What this shows. Each run records 200 waves (their heights scatter, following a Rayleigh distribution) in a gusty 15 m/s wind, and takes the mean of the highest third: the significant wave height. Hs rises with the square root of the fetch, so the log–log gradient comes out close to 0.5 (theory 0.5, yellow line): four times the fetch, twice the wave height. Above about 530 km at this wind speed the sea is 'fully developed' and waves stop growing. Results scatter by about ±12% because the wind gusts and because a record of 200 waves is only a sample: record more waves in the table and the scatter shrinks. The same 10 random records are used at every fetch, so all the means sit about 3% below the line together: that is chance in those 10 records, not a fault in the theory.

2 · Which wave angle drives longshore drift fastest?

What this shows. A pebble is moved by 500 waves: swash up the beach at the wave angle, backwash straight down. Head-on waves (0°) move it nowhere along the coast; waves almost parallel to the beach have short swashes and move it little. The distance peaks at 45°, following sin 2θ, the same shape as the CERC formula engineers use for drift. Scatter comes from wave directions spreading ±10° and swash lengths varying; at 0° the pebble wanders randomly either way.

3 · Does a spit grow at a steady rate?

What this shows. A spit grows as fast as sediment arrives at its tip divided by its cross-section. With the same waves each year on average, the length grows in a straight line with time (yellow: about 35 m a year here, so roughly 4 km in 120 years, the scale of Spurn Head). Runs scatter because some years are stormier and move much more sediment; the scatter grows with time but the mean stays on the line. Real spits grow in fits and starts, are breached in storm surges (Spurn, 2013), and slow as they reach deeper water.

4 · How fast does Holderness retreat?

What this shows. Each run is 50 years of random storms on an undefended Holderness. The mean retreat between 20 and 40 km comes out about 1.8–1.9 m a year, matching the equilibrium theory (cliff sediment supply = beach losses offshore) within 1% and the measured average of about 1.8 m a year (East Riding of Yorkshire Council). Runs differ (SD about 0.07 m a year) because a run of stormy years strips the beaches and speeds erosion; any one place can lose 10 m or more in a single year, which is why one year's measurement says little about the long-term rate.

5 · Terminal groyne syndrome: how much extra erosion?

What this shows. Groynes are built starting at 28.5 km (Mappleton) and the coast is run for 30 years, with and without them, on the same storms. The value is the extra cliff retreat in the 3 km just south (downdrift). It rises in proportion to the length of the groyne field, because every cubic metre of sand trapped in the groynes is missing from the beaches downdrift, so the cliffs there erode faster until they have made it up. After 30 years the extra retreat is only about 60% of the sediment-budget estimate (yellow), because some of the shortfall is still 'owed' and spreads further south: the budget is a long-run limit, not a 30-year forecast.

6 · The Bruun rule: retreat as the sea rises (A level)

What this shows. A sandy beach is moved up and back as the sea rises, keeping its shape. Each year the closure depth (how deep storm waves move sand) depends on that year's biggest waves, so runs vary. The retreat is in proportion to the rise: about 53 m per metre of sea-level rise (the best-fit gradient), close to the Bruun rule R = S L ÷ (B + h) with the average storm (yellow, 54 m per metre). Runs sit about 2% below the rule: it is worked out with the average closure depth, but each year's depth varies, and averaging a curved relationship does not give the same answer as using the average. Real beaches also depend on sediment supply, which the Bruun rule ignores.
