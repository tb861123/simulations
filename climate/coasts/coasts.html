How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Wave growth (JONSWAP): significant wave height Hs = 0.0016 (U²/g) √(gF/U²) and peak period T = 0.286 (U/g)(gF/U²)1/3, until the sea is fully developed (Hs = 0.243 U²/g, T = 8.13 U/g). The wind is assumed to blow long enough to fill the fetch. Swell: height falls by e−d/2500 km and the period rises 12% per 1000 km travelled (illustrative).
  • Wave energy E = ρgH²/8 (J per m² of sea); wave power P = ρg²H²T ÷ 64π (W per metre of crest), deep water, using Hs and the peak period. Breaker height Hb = 0.39 g0.2(TH²)0.4 (Komar and Gaughan 1972); run-up from Stockdon et al. (2006).
  • Constructive or destructive: decided by steepness H ÷ L. Above about 0.025 waves are destructive (backwash stronger than swash, beach combed down to an offshore bar); below, constructive (swash stronger, a berm builds). On shingle water soaks in, weakening the backwash. The backwash ÷ swash ratio and the beach-change rate are illustrative. Tides are left out.
  • Cliffs: each point on the cliff line moves inland at rate = rock erodibility × wave energy × exposure. Exposure falls in bays (e−depth/320 m: shelter and a protective beach) and rises on headlands that stick out (wave refraction). Erodibilities at moderate waves (m/yr): granite 0.002, Portland limestone 0.03, chalk 0.25, Wealden clays and sands 1.0, boulder clay 1.8, typical of published ranges. Caves, arches, stacks and stumps are drawn as stages that each headland passes through every 30–55 m of retreat.
  • Cliff profile: a notch is cut between the tides; the overhang falls when the notch passes 2–5 m (rockfall), or a soft clay cliff slumps every 1–3 m (rotational slumping). Fallen debris protects the foot until the waves remove it. The wave-cut platform is what is left behind.
  • Longshore drift: CERC formula Q = 0.0909 Hb2.5 sin 2θ (m³/s). Pebbles: swash up the beach at the wave angle, with a length ∝ Hb cos θ; backwash straight down the slope. Drift per wave = ½ S sin 2θ, greatest at 45°.
  • Spits, bars, tombolos: the end grows at Q × share ÷ ((depth + 4 m) × 150 m) per year, curving slowly towards the land; spells of waves from another direction add recurved hooks. A river keeps a channel 150 m + 2.5 m per m³/s open. Saltmarsh grows behind. Storm years vary at random.
  • Holderness: 120 stretches of 500 m. Drift south = 120 000 m³/yr × storminess × (beach ÷ 120 m³/m, up to 1). Cliff retreat = 4 m/yr × storminess × e−beach width/40 m × (1 + sea-level rise effect); 30% of the eroded clay is sand and gravel that stays on the beach; beaches lose 8.4% a year offshore. This gives about 1.8–1.9 m/yr with no defences, as measured. Groynes trap drift until the beach reaches their tips; a sea wall stops retreat but reflected waves scour the beach (2.5× losses); rock armour cuts retreat by 85%; nourishment adds 60 m³/m every 5 years.
  • Costs and values (illustrative, rounded from typical UK figures): sea wall £8000/m, rock armour £2500/m, groynes £4000 per m of coast, nourishment £15/m³, maintenance 1% a year; houses £250 000, farmland £20 000 per hectare, Easington gas terminal £500 million. Houses are placed at random distances behind the cliff.
  • Bruun rule (A level): R = S × L ÷ (B + h), closure depth h = 1.57 × the year's biggest wave (Hallermeier), L from Dean's profile h = 0.1 x2/3.
  • Sea level (A level): relative sea level = eustatic (an approximate global curve, −125 m 22 000 years ago) + isostatic rebound, which decays as e−t/τ after the ice melts (τ ≈ 3–4 thousand years). Rounded and simplified: real curves vary from place to place.
  • Left out: tides and storm surges in detail, wave refraction computed properly, sand dunes, salt marsh processes, sub-aerial weathering in detail, human uses of the coast beyond defences, and ecosystems.
