Probability Distributions · Teacher notesdistributions.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Build a binomial distribution

What to look for: The middle bin (5) is most likely: there are 10C5 = 252 different paths to it but only 1 path to bin 0. P(X = 5) = 252 × 0.5¹⁰ ≈ 0.246. The histogram settles onto the predicted outline as more balls fall.

Investigation 2: A biased board

What to look for: The peak moves to 2 = np, and the shape is positively skewed (a long tail to the right). It is only symmetrical when p = 0.5.

Investigation 3: Cumulative probability

What to look for: P(X ≤ 2) = 0.3828. X is a whole number, so 'less than 3' and 'at most 2' are the same event; with a continuous variable they would not need converting.

Investigation 4: Mean and variance

What to look for: The mean settles near 5 and the variance near 3.75. The variance is largest at p = 0.5 for a given n and shrinks to 0 as p → 0 or 1.

Investigation 5: Areas under the normal curve A level

What to look for: P(85 < X < 115) = 0.6827 (within 1 σ), P(70 < X < 130) = 0.9545 (within 2 σ). The points of inflection of the curve are at μ ± σ: 85 and 115.

Investigation 6: Inverse normal A level

What to look for: x = 1010 + 1.6449 × 6 = 1019.9 g. The inverse normal works backwards from an area to a value: P(X < x) = 0.95 gives z = 1.6449.

Investigation 7: From board to bell A level

What to look for: Very closely: N(20, 10). With p = 0.5 the approximation is good even for small n; the larger n is, the better it gets (central limit theorem).

Investigation 8: The continuity correction A level

What to look for: Exact 0.2915. N(12, 7.2): P(Y < 10.5) = 0.2883 (with correction) but P(Y < 10) = 0.2280 (without). The bar for X = 10 stretches from 9.5 to 10.5, so the area must go to 10.5.

Investigation 9: When does the approximation fail? A level

What to look for: np = 1, so the distribution is squashed against 0 and strongly skewed; the bell spills into negative values. Rule of thumb: n large and p close to 0.5 (np and n(1 − p) both above about 5).

Investigation 10: Random events: Poisson (beyond 9MA0) A level

What to look for: For a Poisson distribution, mean = variance = λ = 3. P(X = 0) = e⁻³ = 0.0498. It is the limit of B(n, λ/n) as n → ∞: many tiny chances of an arrival.

Batch experiment 1: Does the mean equal np?

Each sample mean scatters around 6 with standard deviation √(np(1−p)/50) = √(4.2/50) ≈ 0.29.
Theory (np): 6
Result from a test run: mean 5.99, SD 0.291, SE 0.0168 from 300 runs

Batch experiment 2: Variance and p

A parabola, largest at p = 0.5 (variance n/4) and symmetrical: p and 1 − p give the same spread, mirrored.
Theory (np(1 − p)): 0.05: 0.95 · 0.1: 1.8 · 0.2: 3.2 · 0.3: 4.2 · 0.4: 4.8 · 0.5: 5 · 0.6: 4.8 · 0.7: 4.2 · 0.8: 3.2 · 0.9: 1.8 · 0.95: 0.95
Result from a test run: 0.05: 0.917 ± 0.04 · 0.1: 1.8 ± 0.0287 · 0.2: 3.31 ± 0.132 · 0.3: 4.25 ± 0.141 · 0.4: 4.59 ± 0.271 · 0.5: 5.23 ± 0.3 · 0.6: 5.14 ± 0.177 · 0.7: 4.27 ± 0.0396 · 0.8: 3.19 ± 0.0704 · 0.9: 1.83 ± 0.0565 · 0.95: 0.95 ± 0.0323 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: The 68–95–99.7 rule (A level)

0.683 at 1σ, 0.954 at 2σ, 0.997 at 3σ, whatever μ and σ are. That is why standardising with Z = (X − μ)/σ lets one table serve every normal distribution.
Theory (2Φ(k) − 1): 0.5: 0.383 · 1: 0.683 · 1.5: 0.866 · 2: 0.954 · 2.5: 0.988 · 3: 0.997
Result from a test run: 0.5: 0.388 ± 0.0131 · 1: 0.678 ± 0.00802 · 1.5: 0.867 ± 0.00675 · 2: 0.959 ± 0.00341 · 2.5: 0.986 ± 0.0026 · 3: 0.997 ± 4.47e-4 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Normal approximation with a continuity correction (A level)

The points (the true binomial probability) jump about because ⌊np⌋ jumps; the approximation follows them more and more closely as n grows.
Theory (normal approx. Φ((⌊np⌋ + 0.5 − np)/σ)): 5: 0.5 · 10: 0.635 · 20: 0.596 · 40: 0.568 · 80: 0.549 · 160: 0.534
Result from a test run: 5: 0.532 ± 0.00612 · 10: 0.655 ± 0.00696 · 20: 0.612 ± 0.0028 · 40: 0.583 ± 0.00376 · 80: 0.551 ± 0.00114 · 160: 0.54 ± 0.00404 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Poisson: mean = variance? (beyond 9MA0)

Close to 1 at every rate. Real counts often have a ratio above 1 (overdispersion): buses that bunch, or illnesses that spread, are not independent events.
Theory (1 for a Poisson distribution): 0.5: 1 · 1: 1 · 2: 1 · 4: 1 · 8: 1 · 12: 1
Result from a test run: 0.5: 0.945 ± 0.0469 · 1: 1.01 ± 0.0267 · 2: 1.01 ± 0.0339 · 4: 0.973 ± 0.0376 · 8: 1 ± 0.0152 · 12: 0.953 ± 0.0302 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)
