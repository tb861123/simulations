Regression and Correlation · Teacher notesregression-correlation.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Find the best line

What to look for: The sum of the squared residuals (the total area of the squares). Any other line has a bigger total. The line always passes through the mean point (x̄, ȳ).

Investigation 2: Drag one point

What to look for: A point far from x̄ has high LEVERAGE: it acts like a long lever and swings the gradient. A point near x̄ mainly moves the line up and down a little.

Investigation 3: One outlier

What to look for: One outlier can change r a lot. Remove it only if there is a reason (a recording error); a genuine unusual value should be kept and discussed. That is 'cleaning data' (2.4).

Investigation 4: Predict safely

What to look for: 8 hours is inside the data (interpolation) and reliable. 25 hours is extrapolation, beyond the data and also impossible (there are only about 16 hours of daylight in summer).

Investigation 5: Correlation is not causation

What to look for: No. Both are caused by a third (confounding) variable: hot weather, when people buy ice cream AND go swimming. Correlation shows association, not cause.

Investigation 6: How much does r vary?

What to look for: With n = 10, r can easily be anywhere from about −0.4 to 0.8. Small samples give very unreliable correlation coefficients.

Investigation 7: Is the correlation significant? A level

What to look for: Critical value for n = 12, one tail 5%: 0.4973. A sample with r above it gives evidence of positive correlation. With a true ρ of 0.4 many samples of 12 fail to show it (a Type II error).

Investigation 8: Exponential growth: y = kbˣ A level

What to look for: log y = log k + x log b. Gradient ≈ 0.204 so b = 10^0.204 ≈ 1.6 (60% growth an hour); intercept ≈ 2.08 so k = 10^2.08 ≈ 120.

Investigation 9: Kepler's third law: y = axⁿ A level

What to look for: log y = log a + n log x. The gradient is 1.50, so T² ∝ r³ (Kepler's third law). The intercept is 0, so a = 1 in units of years and AU (the Earth's orbit).

Batch experiment 1: How variable is r?

Skewed: r cannot exceed 1, so the spread is longer below 0.5. The mean is very slightly below ρ. Standard deviation about (1 − ρ²)/√n ≈ 0.17.
Theory (population ρ): 0.5
Result from a test run: mean 0.504, SD 0.167, SE 0.00749 from 500 runs

Batch experiment 2: False alarms (A level)

About 5% at every n: the critical values are set so that this happens. Each point is only 200 samples (±1.5 percentage points).
Theory (significance level): 5: 5 · 8: 5 · 12: 5 · 20: 5 · 30: 5 · 50: 5
Result from a test run: 5: 3.5 ± 0.354 · 8: 5 ± 0.866 · 12: 4.8 ± 0.718 · 20: 4.9 ± 0.828 · 30: 4.7 ± 0.406 · 50: 6.1 ± 1.12 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Power of the correlation test (A level)

A moderate correlation needs about 40 points to be detected 80% of the time. The Fisher z formula is an approximation, slightly off for tiny n.
Theory (Fisher z approximation): 6: 18.1 · 10: 30 · 15: 43 · 20: 54.1 · 30: 71.1 · 45: 86.4 · 60: 94
Result from a test run: 6: 20.3 ± 1.45 · 10: 31.6 ± 2.35 · 15: 46.5 ± 1.35 · 20: 57.3 ± 0.561 · 30: 72.8 ± 1.63 · 45: 86.6 ± 0.485 · 60: 93.7 ± 1.12 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Leverage of one outlier

A single far-away point can create a 'strong' correlation from nothing. Always look at the scatter diagram, not just r.
Theory (approximate expected r): 0: 0 · 15: 0.0696 · 30: 0.228 · 45: 0.396 · 60: 0.535 · 90: 0.719 · 120: 0.818
Result from a test run: 0: -0.00512 ± 0.0763 · 15: -0.0806 ± 0.0456 · 30: 0.177 ± 0.05 · 45: 0.364 ± 0.0413 · 60: 0.533 ± 0.0181 · 90: 0.722 ± 0.0167 · 120: 0.791 ± 0.0265 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Estimating Kepler's n (A level)

Very precise: the planets span almost two powers of ten in distance, so the gradient is pinned down (high Sxx). Errors near the ends matter most.
Theory (Kepler's n): 1.5
Result from a test run: mean 1.5, SD 0.024, SE 0.0012 from 400 runs
