Central Limit Theorem · Teacher notescentral-limit-theorem.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Where does the bell come from?

What to look for: The means pile up in a bell shape centred on the population mean (the dashed line), even though no individual value comes from a bell-shaped population. Averaging cancels out the extremes.

Investigation 2: Bigger samples

What to look for: The spread of the means is σ/√n, the 'standard error'. To halve it you need four times the sample size; to get ten times more precision you need a hundred times the data. The left-hand chart collects your results.

Investigation 3: Try to break it

What to look for: Within this box you cannot: any population with a finite spread obeys the theorem. Odd shapes just need a larger n before the bell appears. (Populations with infinite variance, like the Cauchy distribution, do break it, but they do not fit in a box.)

Investigation 4: How big is 'big enough'?

What to look for: With rare, extreme values the sampling distribution stays lopsided (several bumps: samples with 0, 1, 2… outliers) long after n = 30. 'n ≥ 30' is a rule of thumb, not a law: skewed data such as incomes or insurance claims need much more.

Investigation 5: Not every statistic is normal

What to look for: The maximum piles up against the top of the range and stays skewed for ever: the theorem is about sums and means. The median and the standard deviation do become roughly normal for large n, but more slowly and with a different spread.

Investigation 6: What does '95% confident' mean?

What to look for: About 1 in 20 should miss: that is what 95% means. It is a statement about the method over many samples, not about any single interval. With tiny samples from a skewed population the method under-performs, because it leans on the theorem.

Batch experiment 1: The standard error

Each estimate comes from only 400 sample means, so it has sampling error of its own (about 3.5%). Even a standard error has a standard error.
Theory (σ / √n): 1: 1.46 · 2: 1.03 · 4: 0.728 · 8: 0.515 · 16: 0.364 · 32: 0.258 · 64: 0.182 · 100: 0.146
Result from a test run: 1: 1.48 ± 0.0508 · 2: 0.984 ± 0.0153 · 4: 0.698 ± 0.0111 · 8: 0.516 ± 0.0132 · 16: 0.362 ± 0.00517 · 32: 0.255 ± 0.00365 · 64: 0.178 ± 0.0031 · 100: 0.145 ± 4.35e-4 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: How fast does skew disappear?

Skewness is sensitive to the few most extreme sample means, so it is a noisy thing to measure.
Theory (population skewness / √n): 1: 1.77 · 2: 1.25 · 4: 0.885 · 8: 0.626 · 16: 0.442 · 32: 0.313 · 64: 0.221
Result from a test run: 1: 1.71 ± 0.023 · 2: 1.31 ± 0.0461 · 4: 0.866 ± 0.0265 · 8: 0.601 ± 0.0312 · 16: 0.45 ± 0.0125 · 32: 0.299 ± 0.00585 · 64: 0.205 ± 0.0139 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Do 95% intervals cover 95%?

Coverage is estimated from 1000 intervals, so it wobbles by about ±0.7 percentage points. The shortfall at small n is real: small samples often contain no outlier at all, so they underestimate both the mean and the spread.
Theory (the promised 95%): 3: 95 · 5: 95 · 10: 95 · 20: 95 · 40: 95 · 80: 95 · 160: 95
Result from a test run: 3: 70.1 ± 0.395 · 5: 65.4 ± 0.384 · 10: 51.9 ± 0.576 · 20: 53.7 ± 0.582 · 40: 74.8 ± 0.786 · 80: 90.8 ± 0.449 · 160: 91.7 ± 0.223 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Mean or median?

For normal data the mean uses the information more efficiently: the median needs about 57% more data for the same precision. With outliers it is the other way round, which is why median house prices are quoted.
Theory (√(π/2) ≈ 1.25 for large n): 5: 1.25 · 11: 1.25 · 21: 1.25 · 41: 1.25 · 81: 1.25
Result from a test run: 5: 1.2 ± 0.00943 · 11: 1.24 ± 0.00754 · 21: 1.24 ± 0.00935 · 41: 1.25 ± 0.00768 · 81: 1.24 ± 0.00945 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)
