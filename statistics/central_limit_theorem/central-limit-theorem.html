Galton Board & the Central Limit Theorem

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Galton board: each ball meets R rows of pegs and at each one goes right with probability p, independently of everything else. The bin number K is the number of right-bounces, so K ~ Binomial(R, p): P(K = k) = C(R,k) pk(1−p)R−k, mean Rp, SD √(Rp(1−p)), skewness (1−2p)/√(Rp(1−p)).
  • Normal curve on the board: the normal curve with the same mean and SD. Each bin is compared with the normal area from k − ½ to k + ½ (a "continuity correction").
  • The animation: balls hop from peg to peg along little arcs, like a thrown ball. Real balls spin, bounce more than once and hit each other; that makes real boards a little less tidy than the binomial. Here every bounce is a clean 50:50 (or p : 1−p).
  • χ² test: χ² = Σ (observed − expected)² / expected. Bins at the edges are merged until every group expects at least 5 balls. If the board really is binomial, χ² follows the χ² distribution with (groups − 1) degrees of freedom; its mean equals the degrees of freedom and it is above the 5% critical value in 5% of runs.
  • "Gap from the normal curve": half the sum of |observed proportion − normal-curve proportion| over all bins, as a percentage (the share of balls in the "wrong" place). It comes from two things: random scatter (shrinks like 1/√balls) and the true difference between binomial and normal (shrinks as rows increase, more slowly for a tilted board).
  • Sample means machine: each sample is n independent values from the parent. The CLT predicts the means have mean μ, SD σ/√n and skewness γ/√n, and look more and more normal as n grows.
  • Parents: fair die; loaded die (P = 0.35, 0.05, 0.05, 0.05, 0.10, 0.40 for 1 to 6); exponential waiting time with mean 2 minutes; two humps (60% normal around 2.5, 40% around 7.5); U-shape (arcsine on 0–10); log-normal incomes (median £27k, σ of ln income 0.6); your own bars (each bar uniform across its width); Cauchy: a lighthouse 1 km off a straight coast spins and flashes at a random moment, and we record where the beam hits the coast. That has no mean and infinite variance.
  • Cauchy: the mean of n Cauchy values has exactly the same Cauchy distribution as one value, so the spread never shrinks. We use the interquartile range (IQR = 2 km for every n) instead of the SD, which is meaningless here.
  • Random numbers: a seeded generator (mulberry32), so the same seed gives exactly the same run. Normal values come from the Box–Muller method.
  • Not modelled: real dice wear and real boards are not perfectly level; real samples are often not independent (for example, people in the same street have similar incomes); real populations are finite.
