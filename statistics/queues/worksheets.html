Supermarket Queues · Teacher notesqueues.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Busy but coping?

What to look for: Arrivals are random: sometimes three people arrive in a minute, sometimes nobody for three minutes. Service times vary too. The tills cannot store up their idle time for later, so a burst of customers has to queue. The mean wait is about 2 minutes here, matching the Erlang C formula.

Investigation 2: Close to the limit

What to look for: Waiting time is roughly proportional to 1 ÷ (1 − ρ), so it shoots up as ρ approaches 1: at ρ = 0.93 the mean wait is about 6 minutes, with long spells far worse. One extra till drops ρ to 0.7 and the wait to under a minute. Small changes in capacity matter enormously near the limit.

Investigation 3: One snake or separate queues?

What to look for: With separate queues you can get stuck behind someone slow while another queue races ahead: a third or more of customers are overtaken by someone who arrived later. One snake queue is first come, first served: nobody is overtaken, the longest waits are much shorter and the mean is a little lower too, because no till sits idle while people wait elsewhere.

Investigation 4: Does variability matter?

What to look for: The utilisation is the same each time, but the wait grows with the variability: Wq = ρ ÷ (1 − ρ) × (1 + CV²) ÷ 2 × service time. CV 0 gives 4 minutes, CV 1 gives 8, CV 2 gives 20. Express tills work partly by separating quick customers from slow ones, cutting the variability.

Investigation 5: Lunchtime rush

What to look for: The queue keeps growing for as long as people arrive faster than the tills can serve them, so it is longest AFTER the peak in arrivals, and takes a long time to clear. Staff need to open extra tills before the rush, not when the queue is already long.

Investigation 6: Giving up

What to look for: Without giving up, an overloaded queue grows without limit. When customers walk away (balking), the queue stays bounded and the people who stay wait less, but the shop loses sales: count the customers lost. Real shops face exactly this trade-off.

Investigation 7: Jockeying

What to look for: Jockeying stops tills sitting idle while people wait, so the mean wait gets close to the snake queue's. But it is still unfair: the person who jumps gets ahead of people who arrived before them.

Batch experiment 1: Why do queues explode as ρ → 1?

Flat at first, then a steep climb: the wait goes as roughly 1 ÷ (1 − ρ). Close to ρ = 1 single runs scatter wildly, because a long queue, once formed, takes a long time to clear.

Batch experiment 2: How much does one day vary?

Most days are close to the average, but a few bad days are much worse (a long right tail): one unlucky burst early on can spoil the whole day. Separate queues sit a little above the snake-queue theory.
Theory (Erlang C (one snake)): 2.16
Result from a test run: mean 2.39, SD 1.03, SE 0.0597 from 300 runs

Separate queues are always a little slower, because a till can sit idle while people wait in another queue.

Batch experiment 4: How often are you overtaken?

The busier the shop, the more often the queue you chose turns out slower than the one next to it.
Result from a test run: 0.4: 1.56 ± 0.252 · 0.6: 4.78 ± 0.225 · 0.8: 10.7 ± 0.73 · 1: 18.4 ± 0.675 · 1.2: 28.1 ± 0.636 · 1.3: 34.5 ± 0.599 · 1.4: 45.4 ± 2.07 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Does variability matter?

Same average workload, very different waits: the wait grows with (1 + CV²). At high CV a few very long services dominate, so single runs scatter widely.
Theory (Pollaczek–Khinchine): 0: 4 · 0.25: 4.25 · 0.5: 5 · 0.75: 6.25 · 1: 8 · 1.25: 10.3 · 1.5: 13 · 2: 20
Result from a test run: 0: 3.43 ± 0.13 · 0.25: 4.49 ± 0.18 · 0.5: 4.61 ± 0.308 · 0.75: 7.34 ± 1.31 · 1: 6.79 ± 0.456 · 1.25: 10.9 ± 1.38 · 1.5: 14.2 ± 2.23 · 2: 18.7 ± 2.35 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: How many tills so that 95% wait under 5 minutes?

Six tills could serve everyone on average (ρ = 1) but the queue would grow without end. Seven reach about 88%, eight about 99%: service targets need well over the bare minimum of tills.
Theory (theory): 7: 88.4 · 8: 98.7 · 9: 99.9 · 10: 100
Result from a test run: 7: 88.5 ± 2.33 · 8: 99.3 ± 0.0917 · 9: 99.8 ± 0.122 · 10: 100 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 7: Little's law

Little's law holds for any queue in a steady state, whatever the distributions: it only needs people to arrive and leave.
