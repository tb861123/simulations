1 · Why do queues explode as ρ → 1?

Why do queues explode as ρ → 1?
3 tills, 2-minute services, one snake queue: the tills can serve at most 1.5 customers a minute. As λ creeps towards 1.5 (ρ → 1) the Erlang C mean wait shoots up like 1/(1 − ρ): going from ρ = 0.8 to 0.96 multiplies the wait by about six. Days scatter more and more near overload, and the last points fall below theory: the shop opens empty and an 8-hour day is not long enough for such a busy queue to reach its long-run average.

2 · How much does one day vary?

Within about 2 standard errors of the theory counts as agreement.

How much does one day vary?
500 separate days with ρ = 0.8. The average of the daily mean waits matches Erlang C closely, but single days range from about half to double that. A manager who judges a shop on one day is mostly measuring luck. Switch the outcome to Longest wait of the day: there is no simple formula for that, and it varies even more.

3 · Is one snake queue faster and fairer than separate queues?

Paired comparison: each day the same customers arrive at the same moments with the same shopping, once with separate queues and once with a snake, so the difference is due to the layout alone.

Is one snake queue faster and fairer than separate queues?
Each day is run twice with exactly the same customers: once with a queue per till (customers pick the shortest, no switching), once with one snake. The snake wins on almost every day: its mean wait is lower, and the spread of waits (SD, 95th percentile, longest wait) is much smaller, because nobody gets stuck behind a slow customer while another till sits idle. Unfairness drops to zero.

4 · Does variability matter? (Pollaczek–Khinchine)

Does variability matter? (Pollaczek–Khinchine)
One till, ρ = 0.8, the same average service time, only the spread of service times changes. Pollaczek–Khinchine: Wq = ρ/(1 − ρ) × (1 + CV²)/2 × 2 min, so CV = 2 waits 5 times longer than fixed service times (CV = 0). The days scatter a lot at high CV: a few very long services cause most of the waiting, so most days come in a little under the formula and a few unlucky days far over it. With only 40 days the high-CV averages usually sit slightly below theory; the shape (wait growing with 1 + CV²) is the point.

5 · How many tills so 95% wait under 5 minutes?

How many tills so 95% wait under 5 minutes?
120 customers an hour, 3 minutes each: that is 6 tills' worth of work (6 erlangs), but with 7 tills (ρ = 0.86) far too many people wait over 5 minutes. Erlang C says P(wait > 5) = C e−(cμ − λ)×5. The red line is the 95% target: read off the smallest number of tills that reaches it. Each extra till helps less than the one before.
