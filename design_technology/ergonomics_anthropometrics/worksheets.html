Ergonomics and Anthropometrics · Teacher notesergonomics-anthropometrics.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: How tall should a door be?

What to look for: Clearance: design for the tallest. The 95th percentile of all adults is about 1,845 mm (almost all of the tallest 5% are men), + 75 mm for shoes and headroom ≈ 1,920 mm. Standard UK doors are 1,981 mm (6'6"), which fits about 98.5% of men and nearly every woman.

Investigation 2: Can everyone reach?

What to look for: Reach: design for the smallest. The 5th percentile of adults' grip reach is about 660 mm (5th percentile woman: 647 mm), so the control must be no further than that; then almost everyone can reach.

Investigation 3: One chair for everyone?

What to look for: A fixed seat fits only those whose popliteal height (+25 mm shoe) is close to it: about 30%. Adjusting from about 390 to 505 mm covers the 5th percentile woman to the 95th percentile man.

Investigation 4: Percentiles on the curve

What to look for: 5th = mean − 1.645 SD = 1,625 − 102 = 1,523 mm; 50th = mean = 1,625 mm; 95th = 1,727 mm. The same z-values work for any normally distributed dimension.

Investigation 5: Mixed populations A level

What to look for: No: the mixed 95th percentile (about 1,845 mm) is much closer to the men's (1,870 mm) than to the women's (1,727 mm), because nearly all of the tallest 5% of adults are men. Designing for 'the 95th percentile' needs saying of whom.

Investigation 6: Children are users too A level

What to look for: Everything shifts: children's elbows are ~110 mm lower than women's, and their reach is shorter. Choose data for the actual users; a design for adults can exclude children (and vice versa).

Investigation 7: A small survey A level

What to look for: Estimates of extreme percentiles from small samples are unreliable (scattered by about ±13 mm, and biased): only one or two people are below the 5th percentile in a sample of 30. Large published datasets are better for design.

Batch experiment 1: Door height and the percentile designed for

Designing for the 95th percentile man accommodates about 97.5% of all adults (all but 5% of men and a few women). Beyond the 99th percentile each step costs height for very few extra people.
Theory (mixed-population normal CDF): 50: 74.1 · 75: 87.4 · 90: 95 · 95: 97.5 · 99: 99.5 · 99.9: 99.9
Result from a test run: 50: 75.6 ± 0.482 · 75: 87.1 ± 0.717 · 90: 95.6 ± 0.538 · 95: 97.4 ± 0.486 · 99: 99.2 ± 0.183 · 99.9: 100 ± 0.04 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Adjustable chair: range against fit

Each extra centimetre of adjustment helps less than the last: the tails of the distribution are thin. About 75 mm of adjustment fits around 90%; 100 mm fits about 97%.
Theory (normal CDF of the range): 0: 33.8 · 20: 54.5 · 40: 71.9 · 60: 84.7 · 80: 92.8 · 100: 97.1 · 120: 99 · 160: 99.9
Result from a test run: 0: 34.1 ± 0.88 · 20: 55.2 ± 0.412 · 40: 72.2 ± 1.24 · 60: 83.8 ± 0.204 · 80: 93.2 ± 0.279 · 100: 97 ± 0.426 · 120: 98.9 ± 0.258 · 160: 99.9 ± 0.049 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Surveying 30 people

The estimates scatter by about ±13 mm and sit a little high on average: a design based on a small survey could exclude more people than intended. Use large published datasets, or calculate the percentile from the mean and SD.
Theory (true 5th percentile): 660
Result from a test run: mean 667, SD 12.8, SE 0.738 from 300 runs

Batch experiment 4: From the mean and SD instead (A level)

Using all 30 numbers (through the mean and SD) gives a tighter estimate than counting from the bottom, as long as the data really are normal. For a mixed population (two humps) the shortcut is biased.
Theory (true 5th percentile): 647
Result from a test run: mean 647, SD 9.39, SE 0.542 from 300 runs
