Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Pull a steel bar

What to look for: The straight line is elastic (it would spring back). At the yield point (about 250 MPa) it starts to stretch plastically. The peak is the UTS (400 MPa); then it necks and breaks at about 25% strain: very ductile.

Investigation 2: Brittle against ductile

What to look for: Acrylic snaps at about 4% strain with no warning: brittle. HDPE yields and draws out to hundreds of %: ductile and tough (the area under its curve is far larger), though much weaker.

Investigation 3: Stiff is not strong

What to look for: Glass is 25 times stiffer (E = 70 GPa against 2.8) but breaks at a similar stress, suddenly. Stiffness, strength and toughness are different properties.

Investigation 4: Every sample is different

What to look for: Metals vary by a few per cent. Timber varies by ±20% or more (knots, grain, moisture): designers use a bigger factor of safety for wood.

Investigation 5: Choose for the brief

What to look for: Lightest by far: carbon fibre (about 64 g). Steel, aluminium and titanium all come out about the same mass (about 230 g), because stiffness, not strength, sets the size and their E/ρ is almost equal; steel is cheapest. Real aluminium frames win by using fatter, thinner-walled tubes, which are stiffer in bending.

Investigation 6: Work hardening and annealing A level

What to look for: Work hardening (e.g. cold rolling) raises yield strength a lot but cuts ductility: harder to form. Annealing (heat, slow cool) restores ductility so the metal can be bent or pressed again. Stiffness E doesn't change.

Investigation 7: Factor of safety A level

What to look for: Area for strength ∝ FoS, so mass and cost rise in proportion where strength governs. Where stiffness governs, the FoS makes no difference until strength takes over.

Investigation 8: Specific stiffness A level

What to look for: Materials on the same line give equally light parts for a stiff tie. Steel, aluminium and titanium all have E/ρ ≈ 26 MN m/kg: aluminium is not 'stiffer for its weight'. Only carbon fibre lies well above.

Batch experiment 1: Twenty steel samples

A narrow bell curve: metals are consistent, so a modest factor of safety is enough.
Theory (data-book UTS): 400
Result from a test run: mean 400, SD 15.7, SE 1.11 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 2: Twenty glass samples

Lopsided, with a long tail of weak samples: a few hidden cracks make some pieces fail at half the typical strength. Brittle materials need large factors of safety, or tempering/lamination.
Theory (typical (median)): 45
Result from a test run: mean 44.1, SD 9.44, SE 0.667 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 3: Factor of safety and failure (A level)

With FoS = 1, half the parts fail; by 1.5 almost none do. Uncertainty in loads and materials is the reason for a factor of safety, so the right FoS depends on how uncertain they are.
Theory (Φ(−(FoS − 1)/√(σL² + σS²))): 1: 50 · 1.1: 28.3 · 1.2: 13.1 · 1.3: 5.01 · 1.5: 0.462 · 1.75: 0.0128 · 2: 2.56e-4
Result from a test run: 1: 50.2 ± 0.911 · 1.1: 28.4 ± 0.848 · 1.2: 12.7 ± 0.574 · 1.3: 4.93 ± 0.199 · 1.5: 0.55 ± 0.131 · 1.75: 0.0333 ± 0.0333 · 2: 0 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Stiffness: measuring E from the graph

Within a few per cent: a best-fit line through several readings averages out the noise.
Theory (true E): 69
Result from a test run: mean 69, SD 1.44, SE 0.102 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 5: The lightest bike tube (A level)

Steel, aluminium and titanium come out almost the same: here stiffness sets the size, and their E/ρ is nearly equal. Carbon fibre wins. Scatter only matters where strength sets the size.
Theory (data-book design): 1: 224 · 2: 235 · 3: 233 · 4: 64 · 5: 456 · 6: 350
Result from a test run: 1: 224 ± 1.27e-14 · 2: 235 ± 1.27e-14 · 3: 233 ± 0 · 4: 64 ± 0 · 5: 456 ± 0 · 6: 350 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)
