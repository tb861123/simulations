Manufacturing Processes and Quality · Teacher notesmanufacturing-processes.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: One phone stand

What to look for: 3D printing: about £6 with no tooling. Injection moulding needs a £12,000 steel mould, so one part costs over £12,000.

Investigation 2: A million stands

What to look for: Injection moulding: the £12,000 tool adds only 1.2p per part, and each part costs about 80p. 3D printing would still be £6 each and take centuries on one printer.

Investigation 3: Where's the crossover?

What to look for: Break-even quantity = difference in tooling ÷ difference in cost per part = 12,000 ÷ (6.10 − 0.80) ≈ 2,260 stands. Below that, print; above, mould.

Investigation 4: Scales of production

What to look for: Up to about 10: 3D printing. About 10–250: laser-cut acrylic (small batch). About 250–6,000: vacuum forming (batch). Above about 6,000: injection moulding (mass). Each has a range where it is cheapest; CNC is never cheapest here, but it is the most accurate.

Investigation 5: Within tolerance?

What to look for: Vacuum forming varies by ±0.2 mm (SD), so most parts fail ±0.10. CNC (σ = 0.02 mm) or injection moulding (0.04 mm) easily meet it: choose the process for the accuracy needed, not just the cost.

Investigation 6: Catch the drift A level

What to look for: The mean creeps upwards. Stop when points pass the warning or action lines (or several in a row on one side), before parts leave the tolerance: quality control catches the problem, quality assurance prevents it.

Investigation 7: Capability A level

What to look for: Rejects become noticeable as Cpk falls below about 1 (tolerance = 3σ = ±0.12 mm). Industry usually wants Cpk ≥ 1.33.

Batch experiment 1: Cost per unit and quantity

On log scales the cost falls in a straight line (tooling dominates) until it levels off at the cost per part (80p): beyond about 100,000 parts more volume hardly helps.
Theory (tooling ÷ n + cost per part): 1: 3.08 · 2: 2.08 · 3: 1.11 · 4: 0.301 · 5: -0.0362 · 6: -0.0904
Result from a test run: 1: 3.08 ± 6.34e-6 · 2: 2.08 ± 5.12e-5 · 3: 1.11 ± 4.22e-4 · 4: 0.297 ± 0.00296 · 5: -0.0369 ± 0.0115 · 6: -0.0812 ± 0.0115 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: 3D print or mould?

The ratio crosses 1 at about 2,260 parts (10^3.35): the break-even quantity = 12,000 ÷ (6.10 − 0.80).
Theory (exact ratio): 2: 19.8 · 2.5: 6.35 · 3: 2.1 · 3.25: 1.24 · 3.5: 0.753 · 3.75: 0.481 · 4: 0.328 · 5: 0.151
Result from a test run: 2: 20.2 ± 0.568 · 2.5: 6.32 ± 0.15 · 3: 2.18 ± 0.0669 · 3.25: 1.27 ± 0.0365 · 3.5: 0.772 ± 0.0281 · 3.75: 0.479 ± 0.00708 · 4: 0.325 ± 0.00789 · 5: 0.15 ± 0.00762 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Tolerance and rejects

±1σ rejects 32%, ±2σ 4.6%, ±3σ 0.3%. A tolerance tighter than the process can hold means expensive scrap; designers specify only the accuracy the product needs.
Theory (2Φ(−tolerance ÷ σ)): 0.02: 61.7 · 0.04: 31.7 · 0.06: 13.4 · 0.08: 4.55 · 0.1: 1.24 · 0.12: 0.27
Result from a test run: 0.02: 61.5 ± 0.964 · 0.04: 32 ± 0.921 · 0.06: 13.4 ± 0.432 · 0.08: 4.34 ± 0.204 · 0.1: 1.34 ± 0.147 · 0.12: 0.22 ± 0.02 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Measuring capability (A level)

Thirty parts give Cpk to about ±15%, and on average a little low (chance offsets in the sample mean count as drift): small samples can make a process look better or worse than it is. That is why capability studies use many parts.
Theory (true Cpk): 0.833
Result from a test run: mean 0.799, SD 0.112, SE 0.00649 from 300 runs
