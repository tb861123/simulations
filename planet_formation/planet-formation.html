How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • The star is fixed at the centre and never moves or grows once planetesimals exist; its luminosity is L = M4 (solar units) for the whole run, even before fusion starts (a real young star is brighter at first, then dims while contracting).
  • Time: the clock shows an estimate of real time. While gas is present each model year counts as 375 real years, chosen so the gas lifetime matches real discs (about 3 million years). After the gas has gone, collisions in real systems become far rarer, so each model year counts as 20,000 real years, putting the giant-impact phase at tens of millions of years as in our Solar System. A single conversion factor can't be right for every process, so treat the times as rough.
  • Hydrogen fusion starts ("ignition", joining the main sequence) at 40 million years × (star mass)−2.5, the approximate time a young star takes to contract until its core is hot enough. Earlier glow comes from gravitational energy.
  • Cloud prologue: 2000 parcels of gas and dust in a slowly spinning ball 10,000 AU across, densest in the middle (density ∝ 1/r², a standard model of a cloud core), all turning together. Each feels gravity from all the mass closer to the centre (as if the cloud were spherical). Gas pressure, magnetic fields and outflows are left out. Inside 800 AU, material hitting the disc loses its up-down and outward motion (shocks) and slowly loses spin (friction in the disc) so it drifts onto the star. Anything within 40 AU joins the protostar. The cloud is taken to be 1/0.7 of the final star's mass, and at the end the model starts the planetesimal disc with the slider settings; the collapse does not set the disc's mass.
  • The model jumps over planetesimal formation (dust sticking into km-sized bodies), which is still an active research topic.
  • The system is called "settled" once the star has ignited and nothing bigger than 0.05 M⊕ has collided, been ejected or fallen into the star for 30 million (estimated) years. Big caveat: 30 million real years is tens of millions of orbits, but the model only runs a few thousand, so slow instabilities that take millions of orbits (thought to have caused late giant impacts such as the one that made our Moon) can't happen on their own. The late-stage collision boost (default ×4 the Hill-radius collision size) stands in for that missing time; it works well for the widely spaced outer planets but less well close to the star, where Hill radii are small, so inner systems often stay more crowded than real ones.
  • Frost line = 2.7 × √L AU, fixed for the whole run (real discs cool, so it moves inward over time).
  • The gas disc is a set of 160 thin rings. It starts spread over the same distances as the solids with the same fall-off, fades away exponentially everywhere at the same rate (real discs clear from the inside out), and slowly flows sideways to smooth out gaps.
  • Gas only gathers onto cores above the critical mass. The rate is the slower of: the core's ability to squeeze its envelope (faster as it grows: time ∝ (critical mass ÷ mass)³, compressed to 1000 years at the critical mass), and a few orbits to sweep its feeding zone (3.5 Hill radii either side). Growth stops when the zone is emptied (a gap) or the gas is gone.
  • Gas drag gently pulls every body towards a circular, flat orbit, more strongly for small bodies and where there is more gas. The gas moves at exactly the circular-orbit speed, so it causes no inward drift.
  • No planet migration: planets don't push on the gas disc and get moved by it, so no "hot Jupiters" from migration.
  • Gas envelopes are given the density of Saturn (0.7 g/cm³) for working out radius.
  • Outside the frost line each planetesimal starts with 4× the solid mass (3 parts ice to 1 part rock); the change is a sharp step.
  • All planetesimals at the same distance start with the same mass.
  • Gravity between planetesimals is only included if at least one of the pair is above the mass threshold; gravity is slightly "softened" at very short range.
  • Collision radius = a multiple of the Hill radius, far bigger than the real body, so collisions are much more frequent than in nature — this is the main way time is compressed.
  • Impact speed is measured when the inflated spheres touch and compared with the escape speed from the real (physical) radii.
  • Merging, bouncing and shattering are decided by fixed thresholds (1 × and 1.5 × escape speed); real outcomes also depend on angle and material strength.
  • Bounces lose half their approach speed (coefficient of restitution 0.5).
  • Shattering makes 3–5 fragments with random shares of the mass, flying apart at the escape speed; the rest of the impact energy is assumed to become heat. If the pieces would be smaller than 0.005 M⊕, the pair is instead "ground to dust" and removed (as if blown away by starlight) — the only way mass leaves except falling into the star or ejection. Above 1500 bodies, would-be shatters bounce instead.
  • Physical radius assumes Earth-like density for rock and water-ice density for ice.
  • Bodies closer than 0.1 AU fall into the star; unbound bodies beyond 4× the disc radius are ejected.
  • Each body follows its exact Kepler ellipse around the star between steps; pulls from other bodies and the gas are added in fixed steps of 1/20 of an orbit at the inner edge (the "Wisdom–Holman" method used by professional planetary codes). Collisions are checked assuming bodies move in straight lines relative to each other during a step, so a few very fast encounters near the star may be missed.
  • Asteroids & comets are 400 "test particles": too small to pull on anything, they feel the star, the gas and every body above 1 M⊕, and vanish if they hit one. They start spread like the planetesimals but out to 1.6× the disc radius (real discs have a thin outer fringe). They are moved every 5 steps to save time. A belt is a range of distances where at least a quarter of the starting asteroids are still there once the gas has gone; these leftovers are what we see today as the asteroid belt and Kuiper belt.
  • Once fewer than 120 bodies remain, every body pulls on every other one, whatever the mass threshold.
  • Sizes on screen ∝ (mass)1/3 and are hugely exaggerated.
  • Spin: planetesimals start with a random spin (4–24 h, random axis). In a merger the spins add up together with the "sideways" angular momentum of the impact, worked out as if the bodies met at their real radii. Spin can't exceed break-up speed (the excess is assumed lost with debris). Fragments get fresh random spins; bounces don't change spin; tides are ignored, so days never lengthen.
  • Gas giants are spun up by the gas they gather, which is given a set fraction (30%) of the break-up angular momentum at the surface, around the orbit's axis.
  • Moment of inertia: 0.4 m R² for a solid body, falling to 0.25 m R² for a gas giant (Jupiter's is about 0.25).
  • The inspector's orbit (eccentricity, perihelion, aphelion, year) is the ellipse the body would follow if only the star pulled on it, so it changes slightly as other bodies tug it. Temperature assumes a black body with no atmosphere.
