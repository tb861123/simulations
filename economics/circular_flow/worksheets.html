The Circular Flow and the Multiplier · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Does the multiplier equal 1 ÷ MPW?

What this shows. MPW = 0.1 + 0.2 + 0.1 = 0.4, so the formula predicts a multiplier of 2.5, and the mean of 300 economies lands on it. Nobody in the model knows the formula: it comes from rounds of re-spending. Runs scatter because each economy's households are drawn at random: the real MPC is the income-weighted average of their MPCs, and when the richest few happen to spend more (or less) of each £1, the multiplier is higher (or lower). The blue curve is the spread predicted from that alone; the random ups and downs in spending add a little more.

2 · Bigger leakages, smaller multiplier

What this shows. Every extra 1p of each £1 spent on imports leaks out of the UK's circular flow, so each round of re-spending is smaller. The multiplier follows 1 ÷ MPW = 1 ÷ (0.3 + MPM): about 2.9 for a fairly closed economy, falling to 1.25 for a very open one. This is why the multiplier is small in open economies like the UK (imports are about a third of GDP). The scatter at each step is the random draw of households.

3 · Rounds of spending: how fast does it build?

What this shows. With MPC 0.8 the long-run multiplier is 5, but it is not reached at once. After one round income is up by the injection (1×); after two by 1 + 0.8 = 1.8×; after n rounds by (1 − 0.8ⁿ) ÷ 0.2, a geometric series. After 10 rounds (about 2½ years) it has reached only about 89% of the final effect. Real multipliers take time to work, so fiscal policy has time lags. Single runs scatter only a little (the households differ).

4 · Who gets the money? (A level)

What this shows. When richer households save and import more of each extra £1, and they receive most of the income, the economy's real MPC is the income-weighted average, lower than the simple average. The measured multiplier falls below the 1 ÷ MPW formula (gold) and follows 1 ÷ (1 − income-weighted MPC) (grey). So the simple formula can overstate the multiplier, and who receives new spending matters: more goes round again if it reaches households with a high MPC.

5 · The accelerator: booms and busts (A level)

What this shows. With no accelerator income rises smoothly to 1 ÷ MPW = 2.5 × the injection. As investment reacts more to rising output, income overshoots: rising output raises investment, which raises output more, until growth slows and investment falls back. The peak follows the multiplier–accelerator model (Samuelson); at v = 1 the swings no longer die away (and above 1 they grow). Real investment is far more volatile than consumption for this reason. Runs scatter because each economy's households differ, so its real MPC differs a little; the accelerator magnifies those differences, so the scatter grows with v.

6 · Does income settle where J = W?

What this shows. Each economy starts with national income of only £400bn, far below equilibrium. Injections (£400bn) are then bigger than withdrawals, so income rises round after round until withdrawals (0.4 × income) equal injections: Y = 400 ÷ 0.4 = £1,000bn. It does not matter where it starts. The economies scatter around £1,000bn because each one's real MPC depends on its randomly drawn households (blue curve: the predicted spread).
