How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Rounds. Money moves in rounds of about a quarter of a year. In each round firms pay out all they earned last round as incomes (wages, rent, interest and profit): that is national income, Y.
  • Households split each £1 of income: some is spent on UK-made goods and services (here this is what "MPC" means: in the formulas MPC = 1 − MPW), the rest is withdrawn: saved (MPS), paid in tax (MPT) or spent on imports (MPM). The shares are the same for every extra £1 (straight-line consumption), so marginal and average propensities are equal.
  • Injections (investment, government spending, exports) are set by you and do not depend on income, except investment when the accelerator is on: I = I₀ + v × (the rise in income last round), never below zero.
  • Equilibrium. Firms' revenue = C + I + G + X, which becomes next round's income. Income stops changing when injections = withdrawals: Y = (I + G + X) ÷ MPW. The flows balance in total, not one by one (saving need not equal investment, nor tax equal government spending).
  • The multiplier is not built in: it comes out of the rounds. Extra spending of ΔJ becomes someone's income; they spend MPC × ΔJ on UK goods, which becomes someone else's income, and so on: ΔJ (1 + MPC + MPC² + …) = ΔJ ÷ (1 − MPC).
  • Households differ (A level). Each household's MPC is the average plus a random difference, and incomes are unequal (log-normal). The economy's real MPC is the income-weighted average, so the measured multiplier scatters around 1 ÷ MPW, and is lower if richer households (who get more of the income) save more of each £1. Random ups and downs in spending add a little more scatter.
  • AD/AS (A level). AD = A − b (P − 100): a higher price level reduces the real quantity demanded (real wealth, interest-rate and trade effects). A change in spending shifts AD by the multiplier × the change. Classical SRAS: P = W + costs + β (Y − Y*); wages W rise when output is above potential and fall when it is below, so the economy returns to LRAS (Y*) in the long run. Keynesian AS: flat with lots of spare capacity, then steeper, vertical at full capacity; wages do not fall, so there is no automatic return to full employment.
  • Real UK numbers: the default flows are chosen so national income is about £714bn a quarter, close to UK GDP in 2024 (about £2.85 trillion a year, ONS), and the shares of C, G, I, X and M are close to the ONS figures. The propensities (MPS 0.1, MPT 0.3, MPM 0.3) are illustrative: "tax" is net of benefits. Real estimates of UK multipliers are lower than 1 ÷ MPW (the OBR uses roughly 0.3 to 1 for different tax and spending changes), because of the time lags, crowding out, the Bank of England's response and confidence, which this model leaves out.
  • Left out: interest rates and money, prices in views 1 and 2 (they are fixed: everything is in real terms), changes in the tax system with income (progressive taxes), wealth, borrowing to spend more than income, firms keeping profits, stocks of unsold goods, and the government's or the country's debt.
