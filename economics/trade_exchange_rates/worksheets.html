Trade and Exchange Rates · Teacher notestrade-exchange-rates.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Better at everything?

What to look for: The UK gives up only 2 clothing per machine; B gives up 6. So the UK has the comparative advantage in machines, B in clothing. Both consume beyond their PPFs: the gold dots move outside the lines.

Investigation 2: Terms of trade

What to look for: Always between the two opportunity costs. A small country trades at the big country's price and takes nearly all the gains; when B is big the UK gains more.

Investigation 3: A tariff on steel

What to look for: UK firms (more producer surplus) and the government (revenue) gain; buyers lose more than both together. The two red triangles are the deadweight loss: too much made at home at high cost, and too little bought.

Investigation 4: Quota

What to look for: A quota can give the same price as a tariff, but the extra on each imported tonne goes to whoever holds the import licences (quota rent), often foreign firms, not the government.

Investigation 5: Interest rates and the pound

What to look for: Savers want UK assets, so demand for pounds rises: the pound appreciates (about $1.36). A £20,000 car costs Americans more: Strong Pound, Imports Cheap, Exports Dear.

Investigation 6: Higher inflation

What to look for: UK goods become less competitive, so foreigners demand fewer pounds and UK buyers supply more to buy imports: the pound depreciates.

Investigation 7: The real pound

What to look for: The pound fell from about $2 to $1.55 in the financial crisis, and again after the 2016 referendum: imports became dearer, adding to UK inflation in 2017.

Investigation 8: Marshall–Lerner and the J-curve A level

What to look for: PEDx + PEDm = 0.9 < 1, so in the long run it gets slightly worse; with PEDx = 1 it improves. Whatever the PEDs, it gets worse at first (contracts are fixed, volumes adjust slowly): the J-curve.

Investigation 9: Defending a fixed rate A level

What to look for: The Bank must buy the unwanted pounds with its reserves. When they run out the peg cannot hold: the pound falls to the market rate, as in September 1992 when the UK left the ERM.

Investigation 10: Customs union A level

What to look for: Imports now come from the partner at £450 instead of the world at £490: buyers gain (trade creation), but the UK pays £50 a tonne more than the cheapest world producer (trade diversion) and loses the tariff revenue.

Batch experiment 1: Where do the terms of trade settle?

Between the two opportunity costs (2 and 6). A small partner trades at the big country's own price, so the big country gains nothing; the terms of trade move towards the partner's cost as it grows.
Theory (6nB ÷ 200, between 2 and 6): 20: 2 · 50: 2 · 89: 2.67 · 130: 3.9 · 160: 4.8 · 189: 5.67 · 250: 6 · 400: 6
Result from a test run: 20: 2 ± 0 · 50: 2 ± 0 · 89: 2.67 ± 0 · 130: 3.9 ± 0 · 160: 4.8 ± 0 · 189: 5.67 ± 0 · 250: 6 ± 0 · 400: 6 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Who gains most from trade?

Gains depend on the terms of trade: the UK gains nothing when B is small (B takes 73%), and 73% when B is large. Both always gain at least a little, never lose.
Theory (√(ToT ÷ 2) − 1): 20: 0 · 50: 0 · 89: 15.5 · 130: 39.6 · 160: 54.9 · 189: 68.4 · 250: 73.2 · 400: 73.2
Result from a test run: 20: 0 ± 0 · 50: -2.22e-14 ± 0 · 89: 15.5 ± 0 · 130: 39.6 ± 0 · 160: 54.9 ± 0 · 189: 68.4 ± 0 · 250: 73.2 ± 0 · 400: 73.2 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Higher tariff, fewer imports?

Imports fall in a straight line and stop at a prohibitive tariff of £300 (the UK price reaches the no-trade price, £700). Real traders scatter around the line.
Theory (Qd − Qs at Pw + t): 0: 24 · 30: 21.6 · 60: 19.2 · 90: 16.8 · 120: 14.4 · 150: 12 · 180: 9.6 · 210: 7.2 · 240: 4.8 · 300: 0
Result from a test run: 0: 23.9 ± 0.212 · 30: 21.2 ± 0.277 · 60: 19.4 ± 0.36 · 90: 16.9 ± 0.274 · 120: 14.3 ± 0.433 · 150: 12.1 ± 0.39 · 180: 9.49 ± 0.39 · 210: 7.02 ± 0.442 · 240: 5.59 ± 0.338 · 300: 0.39 ± 0.277 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Does the market find the exchange rate?

Close to $1.36 on average, with a day-to-day spread of about 1 cent: real exchange rates jiggle around the price that clears the market.
Theory (where D£ = S£): 1.36
Result from a test run: mean 1.36, SD 0.00713, SE 5.04e-4 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 6: Marshall–Lerner (A level)
