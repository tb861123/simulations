Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Where do the terms of trade settle?

What this shows. One machine trades for between 2 clothing (the UK's opportunity cost) and 6 (country B's). When B is small, even all its workers cannot make the clothing the world wants, so the UK keeps making some clothing and the price stays at the UK's opportunity cost, 2. When B is big, the price is pinned at B's opportunity cost, 6. In between, both countries specialise completely and the price is B's clothing output ÷ the UK's machine output (rising in a straight line with B's size). The points follow this closely: workers are identical, so runs differ only in which workers happen to switch jobs first, and the price settles within about 1% of theory. Tick 'Workers differ in skills' in the table (for example 0.1) to see where theory fails: each PPF bows outwards, countries no longer specialise completely, and the terms of trade stay inside the range, away from the straight-line theory.

2 · Who gains most from trade?

What this shows. Both countries gain whenever the terms of trade lie between their opportunity costs. But a small partner pushes the terms of trade to the UK's own opportunity cost, so the UK gains almost nothing and the small country gains the most (grey). As B grows, the terms of trade move in the UK's favour and its gain rises to about 73% (√3 − 1). The measured gains match the theory (√(machines × clothing) consumed, compared with the same workers without trade). With skill differences (set the skills value in the table to 0.1), the gains are smaller than the straight-line theory: some workers are much better at one job, so each country already makes good use of them without trade, and specialisation brings in workers who are poor at the export industry.

3 · Higher tariff, fewer imports?

What this shows. A tariff raises the UK price by the full tariff (the UK is a small buyer in a big world market), so UK firms make more and buyers buy less: imports fall in a straight line, from about 14 thousand tonnes a day under free trade to zero at a tariff of about £200, when the UK price reaches the no-trade price of £700 (a prohibitive tariff). Measured imports scatter a lot (SD about 3 thousand tonnes) because each market has its own 40 random UK firms and buyers: a few extra low-cost UK firms or a few fewer keen buyers change imports by several units, and the last deals of a day go to whoever calls out first. With a high tariff the measured imports stay a little above theory: a foreign seller occasionally sells to a keen buyer early in the day, before UK firms have lowered their asks.

4 · Does the market find the exchange rate?

What this shows. Each run is a new market of 40 buyers and 40 sellers of pounds who know only their own limits. After UK interest rates rise by 2 points, theory says the pound settles where the shifted demand meets the shifted supply, about $1.36. The markets' rates cluster around that value. They scatter because each market's own traders give step-shaped curves that are not exactly the theory lines: with 40 traders a side, the rate where these traders' curves cross moves by a few cents from one market to the next.

5 · Interest rates and the pound

What this shows. Higher UK interest rates attract hot money: demand for pounds rises and supply falls, so the pound appreciates by about 3 cents for each point (a straight line, by design of this model). Lower rates do the opposite. The same 10 markets are used at every step, so the points move together; the gap from the line is each market's own step-shaped curves. In reality the size of the effect depends on what markets expected: a rise that was fully expected may hardly move the pound.

6 · Marshall–Lerner (A level)

What this shows. UK interest rates are cut, so the pound depreciates by about 6%. Whether the current account improves depends on the price elasticities of demand. With PED for imports 0.3, it improves in the long run only once PED for exports is above about 0.7, so that the two add up to more than 1: the Marshall–Lerner condition. The grey line is the first quarter, when demand is still very inelastic: the current account worsens whatever the long-run elasticities (the bottom of the J-curve). The points scatter around the gold line because each market's exchange rate differs a little from the theory rate.
