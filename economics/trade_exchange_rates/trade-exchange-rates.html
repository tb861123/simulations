How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Comparative advantage (view 1). Two countries, two goods (machinery and clothing), one input: labour. Each worker makes a fixed amount of either good a day (constant opportunity cost, so the PPFs are straight lines). Everyone spends half their income on each good. Each day prices adjust towards where demand equals supply (in each country without trade, in the world market with trade) and some workers move to whichever industry pays more. With trade the terms of trade settle where world demand meets world supply, somewhere between the two countries' opportunity costs. No transport costs, no tariffs, full employment, and workers move freely between industries but not between countries.
  • Skills (A level). With the "workers differ in skills" slider, each worker is a little better at one job than the other, so the PPF bows outwards (increasing opportunity cost) and countries may not specialise completely.
  • Gains from trade are measured as the rise in real consumption: the square root of (machines × clothing) each country consumes, compared with no trade.
  • Tariffs and quotas (view 2). 40 UK buyers and 40 UK steel firms, each knowing only their own value or cost, trade in a double auction with up to 40 foreign sellers. The UK is a small country: foreign sellers will sell at the world price (plus any tariff), so the world supply curve is horizontal. A quota lets in only that many foreign units a day. In a customs union (A level) a partner country's firms sell tariff-free at their own (higher) price, while the rest of the world still pays the tariff. UK welfare = consumer surplus + UK producer surplus + tariff revenue; quota profits go to foreign exporters. Left out: retaliation, transport costs, foreign firms' market power, jobs and the long run.
  • The market for pounds (view 3). 40 buyers of pounds (foreign firms and investors buying UK exports and UK assets) and 40 sellers of pounds (UK importers and investors buying abroad) trade pounds for dollars. Higher UK interest rates raise demand for pounds (hot money) and reduce supply; higher UK inflation makes UK exports dearer and imports relatively cheaper; faster UK growth raises spending on imports; speculation shifts both curves. Each point of interest-rate difference moves the rate by about 3 cents, each point of extra inflation by about 2 cents (illustrative). Small random news each day moves the rate a little.
  • Effects (view 3). A UK export priced at £20,000 costs 20,000 × the rate in dollars; a $100 US import costs 100 ÷ the rate in pounds. Volumes respond with the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports. Exports and imports start at £200bn a quarter each (illustrative, close to UK totals of goods and services). In the short run (first quarter) both elasticities are only 0.1, rising to their long-run values over about a year: this gives the J-curve. Inflation: about 0.2 percentage points for each 1% fall in the pound (illustrative pass-through of import prices into the CPI).
  • A fixed exchange rate (A level). The Bank of England buys any pounds left unsold at the peg (or sells pounds if there is excess demand), using its foreign currency reserves (start: $60bn, illustrative). If the reserves run out, the peg cannot be held and the pound floats.
  • Left out: capital flows in detail, expectations of future rates, other currencies, the effect of the exchange rate on interest rates and growth. Real data in view 4 come from the Bank of England and the ONS.
