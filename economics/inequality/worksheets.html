Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Does luck alone create inequality?

What this shows. Each run is a new game of 200 people who all start with £100 and swap money at random for 200 rounds. Every run ends with a Gini close to 0.5 (mean about 0.50, theory 0.4975 for 200 people): the exponential distribution, which is what you get when a fixed total is shared out at random, like energy between gas molecules. Nobody has more skill, yet the richest 10% end up with about a third of all the money. The Gini scatters by about ±0.02 between runs because 200 people is a small sample, and the mean sits very slightly above theory (a couple of standard errors) because the exponential is only the limit for a very large population. Tick 'Share each person keeps back' in the table and sweep it: the Gini falls along the gamma-distribution curve.

2 · How much does redistribution lower the Gini?

What this shows. A flat tax of t on everyone's income, with all the revenue paid back in equal amounts, turns every income y into (1 − t)y + t × mean. Every gap between two people shrinks to (1 − t) of its size while the mean stays the same, so the Gini of disposable income is exactly (1 − t) × the Gini of original income: an identity, which the points follow exactly (gold line). At 30% the Gini falls by 30%, from about 0.40 to 0.27. The points scatter by the spread in ORIGINAL inequality between runs (each population has its own luck), not because the rule fails. The grey line shows that original-income inequality also falls a little at high tax rates, because people save less of a smaller post-tax income and so build up less unequal wealth. A real tax system is not this simple: benefits are targeted and taxes change behaviour (see the Laffer experiment).

3 · How many are in relative poverty?

What this shows. With no taxes, benefits, unemployment or wealth, incomes are log-normal with log SD σ, and the share below 60% of the median is Φ(ln 0.6 ÷ σ): about 15% at σ = 0.5 and 30% at σ = 1. The runs land on the theory curve, a little below it because the median of 200 people is itself uncertain. Relative poverty is about the SPREAD of incomes, not their level: doubling every income changes nothing here. Real UK relative poverty is about 17% (before housing costs), close to σ ≈ 0.55, although the real distribution is not exactly log-normal and taxes and benefits matter a lot.

4 · Returns on wealth: does r concentrate wealth?

What this shows. With the UK tax system, each run follows a population for 80 years with the chosen average return on wealth. The higher the return, the more unequal wealth becomes (Gini from about 0.58 at 0% to 0.66 at 8%), and income inequality rises even faster (grey line: from 0.24 to 0.47) because returns on wealth go mostly to those who already own it. This is Piketty's r > g argument: here g = 0, so any positive r means existing fortunes grow faster than wages. Wealth inequality is high even at r = 0 because young people have not yet saved and inheritances are unequal (some people have several children, some none). There is no simple theory curve; runs scatter by about ±0.03 because each population has its own lucky investors and heirs.

5 · The Kuznets curve (A level)

What this shows. Each run draws 200 incomes: the chosen share in cities (median £30,000, log SD 0.5) and the rest on farms (median £15,000, log SD 0.25). The Gini rises from 0.14 (all on farms) to a peak of about 0.31 when 50–60% live in cities, then falls to 0.27 (all in cities). The gold curve is the exact Gini of the mixture of the two distributions, and the runs follow it within about one standard deviation. So a change in the STRUCTURE of the economy alone produces Kuznets's inverted U, with no change in either sector's own inequality. Runs scatter because 200 people are a small sample of each distribution.

6 · Where does the Laffer curve peak? (A level)

What this shows. Each run is a new population of 200 workers whose earnings fall as the tax rate rises: earnings = wage × (1 − t)^0.5. Revenue per person rises, peaks at about £16,000 at 60–70%, then falls: the Laffer curve. Theory puts the peak at t* = 1 ÷ (1 + e) = 1 ÷ 1.5 = 67%. The runs scatter by about ±5% around the gold curve because each random population has a different mean wage, and they sit slightly above it for the same reason (a few very high earners). The real elasticity, and so the real peak, is uncertain: HMRC's estimate for top earners in 2012 (about 0.48) suggested a revenue-maximising top rate well below 50% once the shape of top incomes is included (t* = 1 ÷ (1 + a × e)). Sweep the elasticity in the table to move the peak.
