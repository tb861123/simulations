How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • People (view 1). Each person is one adult, aged 20 to 85. Every year they get a year older. At 85 they die and a new adult of 20 takes their place, so the population stays the same size. Each new adult has a parent about 30 years older, chosen at random, so some people have two or three children and some have none. When a parent dies their children (then about 55) share the estate after inheritance tax; someone with no children leaves it to a relative of about 55. Without "Children inherit" the estate goes to the government.
  • Earning. A worker's wage = median wage × e^(skill spread × zskill + luck × zyear), where each z is a random number from a bell curve (mean 0, SD 1). So wages follow a log-normal distribution, close to real earnings. A person's skill stays with them for life; a child's skill is linked to the parent's: zchild = ρ zparent + √(1 − ρ²) × new luck. Each year a worker is unemployed with the chance set by the unemployment rate. A full-time year is 37.5 hours × 52 weeks = 1,950 hours; each year a worker is part-time (half the hours) with the chance set by the part-time share (about a quarter of UK employees work part time). Over-66s retire: they get a workplace pension of 30% of a typical wage for their skill (counted as original income, as the ONS does) and the state pension if it is ticked.
  • Minimum wage. A worker whose wage would be below the National Living Wage × 1,950 hours is paid the minimum instead, but loses their job that year with chance (jobs-lost setting) × (gap ÷ minimum). With the setting at 0 nobody loses their job (most UK studies find small employment effects); at 1 the model behaves like the simple competitive labour market.
  • Wealth. Each year wealth earns a return: average return + luck × z, drawn separately for each person. A positive return is income; a negative one is a fall in the value of their assets (not income). Returns are reinvested. From the rest of their disposable income, workers spend everything up to £25,000 a year (living costs) and save the chosen share of the rest; they also spend 3% of their wealth a year. Retired people spend all their other income and 4% of their wealth a year, so most leave an estate. Nobody can borrow: wealth never falls below zero.
  • Taxes. UK income tax 2026/27 (England, Wales and Northern Ireland): personal allowance £12,570 (reduced by £1 for every £2 of income over £100,000), basic rate 20% to £50,270, higher rate 40% to £125,140, additional rate 45% above. Employee National Insurance: 8% of earnings between £12,570 and £50,270, 2% above. Income tax applies to earnings, pensions and returns on wealth (in reality savings and dividends have their own allowances). Proportional: the same % of all income. Poll tax: the same £ from every adult (like the 1990 community charge). VAT: 20% on half of what people spend (the other half is food, rent and other zero-rated or exempt spending: illustrative). Inheritance tax: the chosen rate on estates above £325,000 (UK: 40%; the extra allowance for passing on a home is left out).
  • Benefits. Means-tested: the full amount if you earn nothing, withdrawn at 55p for every £1 earned (the Universal Credit taper rate; work allowances and housing costs are left out). Basic income: everyone gets it. "Share all tax revenue": the government pays out everything it raised (income tax, National Insurance and poll tax this year, VAT and inheritance tax from last year) in equal amounts, so its budget balances. State pension: £12,548 a year (the full new state pension, £241.30 a week, 2026/27) to everyone over 66.
  • Measures. ONS stages of income: original income (earnings, pensions from work + returns on wealth) → gross income (+ cash benefits and the state pension) → disposable income (− income tax and National Insurance) → post-tax income (− VAT). Gini = Σ |xᵢ − xⱼ| ÷ (2 n² × mean), the same as A ÷ (A + B) on the Lorenz curve. Relative poverty: disposable income below 60% of this year's median. Absolute poverty: below 60% of the median in year 1, kept fixed in real terms. Growth raises all wages, the tax thresholds and benefits in real terms (so there is no "fiscal drag" from frozen thresholds).
  • The trading game (view 2): a "kinetic exchange" model from econophysics (Drăgulescu and Yakovenko, 2000; Chakraborti and Chakrabarti, 2000). In a round everyone is paired at random. Each keeps the share λ of their money, and the pair split the rest at random: mᵢ′ = λmᵢ + ε(1 − λ)(mᵢ + mⱼ). Money is never created or destroyed. With λ = 0 the money ends up following an exponential distribution, with a Gini of exactly 0.5; with saving it follows a gamma distribution with shape n = 1 + 3λ ÷ (1 − λ) (Patriarca and others, 2004, a close fit rather than exact), Gini = Γ(n + ½) ÷ (Γ(n + 1) √π).
  • Kuznets (view 3, A level; beyond the spec: 9EC0 does not name the Kuznets curve): two sectors with log-normal incomes. People move from farms to cities along an S-shaped path (5% urban in year 0, 95% by year 100). The Gini of the whole population can be worked out exactly from the two distributions and the urban share, so the theory curve is exact; the model shows how a change in the structure of the economy alone produces Kuznets's inverted U.
  • Equity and efficiency (view 4, A level: the Laffer curve is 9EC0 4.5.2b): everyone pays the same rate t on earnings, and work and effort fall as t rises: earnings = wage × (1 − t)^e, where e is the elasticity of taxable income. Revenue per person = t × mean wage × (1 − t)^e, which peaks at t* = 1 ÷ (1 + e) (the Laffer curve). If the revenue is shared out equally, the Gini of disposable income is exactly (1 − t) × the Gini of earnings.
  • Left out: households (we count adults, not families; the ONS adjusts household income for household size, "equivalisation"), housing costs, house prices, benefits in kind (the NHS, schools), tax avoidance and evasion, people moving abroad, and any feedback from inequality to growth. All parameters are illustrative; real UK rates and statistics are given with their sources.
