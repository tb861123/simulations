The Trading Pit · Teacher notessupply-demand.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The invisible hand

What to look for: Prices converge on the crossing point, the equilibrium, within a few days, although no trader knows where it is. Buyers who overpaid bid less the next day; sellers who failed to sell ask less. Adam Smith called this the invisible hand; Vernon Smith won a Nobel Prize for showing it in classroom experiments like this one.

Investigation 2: Who is left out?

What to look for: Buyers with low values and sellers with high costs are left out: there is no price at which both would gain. That is the market doing its job: goods go to those who value them most, made by those who can make them most cheaply.

Investigation 3: A demand shock

What to look for: Demand shifts right: price and quantity both rise, but price rises by less than £20 because higher prices pull in extra sellers. How the £20 is shared between a higher price and a higher quantity depends on how steep the supply curve is.

Investigation 4: Who really pays a tax?

What to look for: The price rises by about £10: the burden is shared, even though sellers hand over the money. The grey triangle is the deadweight loss: trades that would have benefited both sides but no longer happen. Nobody gets that money, not even the government.

Investigation 5: Tax and elasticity

What to look for: The side that is less able to walk away bears more of the tax. With inelastic demand the price rises by nearly the whole tax, quantity barely falls and the deadweight loss is small: which is why governments tax fuel, alcohol and tobacco.

Investigation 6: Rent control

What to look for: Fewer sellers are willing at £45, so fewer trades happen and there is a shortage. The lucky buyers gain, but who gets the goods is now down to luck, not who values them most: a keen buyer may miss out while a lukewarm one gets served. Queues, waiting lists and side-payments follow.

Investigation 7: A minimum price

What to look for: Sellers who do trade gain from the higher price, but fewer units are bought and there is a surplus of willing sellers who cannot find a buyer. How big that effect is depends on how elastic demand is: try steepening the demand curve.

Investigation 8: PED and total revenue A level

What to look for: Total revenue rises with price while demand is inelastic (|PED| < 1) and falls once it is elastic (|PED| > 1). It is greatest where PED = −1, the midpoint of a straight-line demand curve (here about £50).

Investigation 9: Luxury, necessity or inferior? A level

What to look for: A luxury (YED 2) gains 20% at the old price: demand shifts right a long way. A necessity (0.5) shifts a little. An inferior good (−0.8) shifts left: people switch to better alternatives when they are richer.

Investigation 10: Substitutes and complements A level

What to look for: Substitute dearer: buyers switch to this good, demand shifts right (XED positive). Complement dearer: fewer buy the pair, demand for this good shifts left (XED negative).

Batch experiment 1: Total revenue and price

Revenue is greatest where demand is unit elastic (PED = −1, here at £50). Where demand is elastic (higher prices), a price rise lowers revenue; where it is inelastic, a price rise raises it.
Theory (P × Q on the demand curve): -30: 743 · -20: 750 · -10: 743 · 0: 720 · 10: 683 · 20: 630 · 30: 563
Result from a test run: -30: 850 ± 6.93 · -20: 886 ± 8.3 · -10: 858 ± 8.42 · 0: 831 ± 12.5 · 10: 798 ± 11.4 · 20: 711 ± 8.88 · 30: 626 ± 16.9 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: PED along a straight demand curve

The same slope, but PED grows (more elastic) at higher prices and smaller quantities, because it compares percentage changes. Single markets are noisy: look at the means.
Theory (−P ÷ (100 − P)): -25: -0.961 · -15: -1.17 · -5: -1.44 · 5: -1.78 · 15: -2.23
Result from a test run: -25: -0.988 ± 0.228 · -15: -1.36 ± 0.164 · -5: -1.61 ± 0.235 · 5: -1.61 ± 0.29 · 15: -2.55 ± 0.277 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Tax and the price buyers pay

Prices come from haggling, not from an auctioneer, and each market has its own random set of traders. Individual trades scatter around the equilibrium, especially with only a couple of dozen traders.
Theory (equilibrium (half the tax passed on)): 0: 60 · 5: 62.5 · 10: 65 · 15: 67.5 · 20: 70 · 30: 75 · 40: 80
Result from a test run: 0: 59.9 ± 0.234 · 5: 62.8 ± 0.271 · 10: 64.4 ± 0.259 · 15: 67.6 ± 0.383 · 20: 70 ± 0.288 · 30: 74.6 ± 0.217 · 40: 80.3 ± 0.29 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Deadweight loss of a tax

Even with no tax the pit loses a little, because random haggling sometimes pairs the 'wrong' traders. The loss from the tax grows with the square of the tax: doubling the tax roughly quadruples the damage.
Theory (∝ tax² (the triangle)): 0: 0 · 10: 7.5 · 20: 30 · 30: 67.5 · 40: 120 · 50: 188
Result from a test run: 0: 30.3 ± 3 · 10: 18 ± 1.72 · 20: 30 ± 2.13 · 30: 64.3 ± 6.82 · 40: 130 ± 4.84 · 50: 219 ± 11.7 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Price cap and quantity

Below the equilibrium the quantity is limited by how many sellers can cover their costs at the capped price. Above it, the cap makes no difference at all: it is 'not binding'.
Theory (sellers willing at that price): 30: 3 · 40: 6 · 50: 9 · 60: 12 · 70: 12 · 80: 12
Result from a test run: 30: 2.83 ± 0.128 · 40: 6 ± 0 · 50: 8.88 ± 0.227 · 60: 11.7 ± 0.156 · 70: 13.7 ± 0.107 · 80: 13.9 ± 0.113 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: Thin and thick markets

In a thin market each day's price depends on which few traders happened to meet, so it jumps about. In a thick market those accidents average out. That is one reason trade concentrates in exchanges and marketplaces, and why prices of rarely traded things (houses, paintings) are so uncertain.
Result from a test run: 4: 3.17 ± 0.295 · 8: 1.92 ± 0.147 · 16: 1.26 ± 0.124 · 32: 0.849 ± 0.0859 · 60: 0.63 ± 0.0371 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)
