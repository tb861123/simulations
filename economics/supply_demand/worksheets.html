Supply, Demand and Elasticity · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Do traders find the equilibrium?

What this shows. Nobody in these markets knows the curves, yet the average price on day 5 clusters round the theory equilibrium of £2.50. The spread (SD about £0.33) is mostly not the traders' fault: each market has its own random set of 60 traders, so its own step-shaped curves cross a little above or below £2.50 (the blue curve is that predicted spread). Within a day, prices are very close to each other (SD about £0.05): the traders find THEIR market's equilibrium.

2 · More traders, closer to theory?

What this shows. The bigger the market, the closer its price gets to the theory equilibrium. The average error falls as 1 ÷ √(number of traders): a straight line of gradient −0.5 on log–log axes (16 times more traders → errors 4 times smaller). With a few traders, who happens to be in the market matters a lot; with hundreds, the random values average out and the step curves hug the theory lines. Each dot is one market; the error bars show how much they scatter. The measured gradient comes out nearer −0.43: in tiny markets the steps are so wide that the simple binomial formula (gold line) overstates the scatter, and in big markets the trading itself adds a few pence of noise (prices still vary a little within a day, and four days is not quite long enough to settle). Both effects flatten the line.

3 · How much of the possible gain is made?

What this shows. Market efficiency = the surplus (value − cost) of the trades made ÷ the most these traders could make. Learning traders reach about 99% by day 5: almost every buyer whose value is above the price is matched with a seller whose cost is below it. The small losses are a marginal buyer or seller who misses out, or an extra-marginal trader who sneaks a deal. Theory (100%) is the perfectly competitive ideal.

4 · Even with random traders?

What this shows. Traders who call out random prices (never losing money, never learning) still make about 95% of the possible gains: Gode and Sunder (1993) found the same. The market rules (deals only when a bid meets an ask) do much of the work. But their prices scatter widely within each day, so learning matters for a stable price, and their losses are bigger and more variable than those of learning traders.

5 · Estimating PED from sales data

What this shows. A shop tries a price, then a higher price, with a different random 200 customers each week, and works out PED from its sales. The true PED is −1.00. On average the estimate is right (on a straight-line demand curve the GCSE formula gives exactly the point PED), but small price changes give wild estimates: a 5% rise changes sales by only about 10 customers out of 100, which is easily swamped by week-to-week chance (about ±10 customers). The scatter shrinks roughly as 1 ÷ (size of the price rise). Bigger price rises give a clearer signal. Switch the formula to midpoint to see it drift (grey line) as the change gets bigger.

6 · A rise in demand: how far does the price rise?

What this shows. Every buyer will pay more (e.g. incomes rise), shifting demand up by up to £1. The traders, still knowing only their own values, push the price up along the supply curve. With PED = PES = 1 here, theory says the price rises by half of the shift (gradient 0.5): the other half is lost as some buyers are priced out and stalls extend supply. The best-fit gradient of the dots should be close to 0.5.
