How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Traders. Each buyer wants at most one cup a day and has a private value; each seller can make at most one cup a day and has a private cost. Values and costs are spread evenly (uniformly) between a lowest and a highest figure, so the theory demand and supply curves are straight lines. Each set of traders is a random draw, so its own step-shaped curves wobble around the theory lines.
  • Trading is a continuous double auction, the rules of many real exchanges: a random trader who has not yet traded today calls out a price; a bid above the best bid (or an ask below the best ask) goes on the board; a deal happens when a bid meets the best ask (or an ask meets the best bid), at the price already on the board. After a deal the board is cleared. A day ends when no more deals are possible, or after 10 calls per trader.
  • Learning ("ZIP" traders, Cliff 1997). Each trader keeps a margin (how much below their value they bid, or above their cost they ask) and nudges their price a fraction β (0.1 to 0.5) towards a target just above or below the last price called: after a deal they aim for a better deal; if they were out-bid or under-cut they become more generous. Nobody ever bids above their value or asks below their cost. "Zero intelligence" traders (Gode and Sunder, 1993) call random prices with no learning: they still reach a high share of the possible surplus, but prices jump about.
  • Shifts. A demand cause adds the same amount (£) to every buyer's value (income: enough that, at £2.50, YED is +0.8 for a normal good, −0.8 for an inferior good and +2.0 for a luxury (with PED −1 that is ±£0.20 or +£0.50 per 10% of income); tastes +10%: +£0.10; tea +£1: +£0.50; pastry +£1: −£0.50), so demand shifts up (right) or down (left) in parallel. Population and number of stalls change how many traders there are. A supply cause adds to every seller's cost (beans +100%: +£0.75; wages +10%: +£0.10; technology +10%: −£0.20; a tax per cup adds it to the cost; a subsidy takes it off). These sizes are illustrative, not measured.
  • Elasticity. The curves are drawn through £2.50 (with half the traders trading) with the PED and PES set by the determinants (each choice multiplies the elasticity: few substitutes ×0.5, many ×2; necessity ×0.5, luxury ×1.6; large share of income ×1.4; long run ×1.8; no spare capacity ×0.4, plenty ×2; stocks ×1.6; market period ×0.3, long run ×2.5). Illustrative values, chosen to show the direction of each effect.
  • Point elasticity on a straight line. PED = (ΔQ/ΔP) × P/Q changes along a straight demand curve: elastic above the midpoint, unitary at the midpoint, inelastic below. With the GCSE formula (% change from the start), a straight line gives exactly the point PED at the starting price; the midpoint formula gives the average over the stretch.
  • Surplus. Consumer surplus = Σ(value − price paid) for the buyers who traded; producer surplus = Σ(price received − cost) for the sellers who traded. Market efficiency = the surplus the traders made ÷ the most they could have made (the highest values matched with the lowest costs).
  • Left out: buyers wanting more than one cup, firms selling many units with rising marginal cost, stocks carried over between days, brand differences, buyers comparing shops, information spreading over time, and expectations of future prices. Real markets are rarely this tidy, but Smith's experiments with real students show the same convergence.
