The market

What the government does
Information works on buyers who misjudge their own benefit (merit and demerit goods: not named in 9EC0 or 4EC1; taught via 9EC0 1.3.2d and 1.3.4). Raise the information gap above (A level) to see it.

Cap and trade

Each firm is given permits in proportion to what it emits now (free allocation). A firm must hold one permit for every tonne it emits, so it either cuts emissions (abatement) or buys permits from firms that can cut more cheaply.

A public good

Every household has £10 each round and chooses how much to put towards street lights. The lights are non-excludable and non-rival: everyone benefits, whether they paid or not.

Used cars (A level)

Owners know how good their car is; buyers cannot tell, so they pay what an average car on sale is worth. Good cars are then worth more to their owners than the price, so they leave the market.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • The market (view 1) is a double auction: 40 buyers and 40 sellers, each wanting to trade one unit a day. Each buyer knows only their own value (the most they would pay) and each seller only their own cost. Values and costs are spread evenly, so the population demand and supply curves are straight lines through the reference price with the chosen PED and PES. Traders call out bids and asks and learn from the prices they see ("ZIP" traders, Cliff 1997); prices settle near where supply and demand cross, as in Vernon Smith's classroom experiments.
  • External costs and benefits. Every unit traded gives third parties a fixed external cost (or benefit) per unit: the marginal external cost (MEC) or benefit (MEB) is constant. So MSC = MPC + MEC (negative production externality), MSC = MPC − MEB (positive production), MSB = MPB − MEC (negative consumption) or MSB = MPB + MEB (positive consumption). In reality the harm from pollution usually grows faster than output, and nobody knows the MEC exactly.
  • Welfare for society = what buyers truly value the units at − what they cost to make − external costs (+ external benefits). The social optimum is where MSB = MSC. The welfare loss is the surplus society gives up compared with the optimum: the triangle between MSB and MSC from the optimum to the quantity actually traded.
  • Policies. A tax (or subsidy) per unit puts a wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers keep, exactly as in the taxes simulation; a tax equal to the MEC makes firms pay the full social cost (a Pigouvian tax). Regulation stops trading once the day's limit is reached; who gets the units is first come, first served, so the units may not go to the buyers who value them most. Information campaigns move what buyers think the good is worth towards its true value (they only help with an information gap: merit and demerit goods).
  • Information gap (A level). For merit and demerit goods buyers misjudge their own private benefit: demand (what they would pay) is above (demerit) or below (merit) their true marginal private benefit.
  • Coase bargaining (A level). With clear property rights the two sides bargain. If the gain from moving to the optimum is bigger than the cost of bargaining, they agree to produce the optimal quantity, and whoever does not own the right pays the other. Otherwise nothing changes. Real bargains are harder: many third parties, free riders, and costs that are hard to prove.
  • Cap and trade (view 2). Eight firms each emit a fixed amount now; cutting emissions (abatement) costs more for each extra tonne: the marginal abatement cost (MAC) rises in a straight line. Firms meet in random pairs; if one would pay more for a permit than the other needs to cut a tonne, they trade a lot of 2,000 tonnes at a price between the two. Trading ends when every firm's MAC is about the same: the permit price. Theory: the price where the permits firms want add up to the cap. Free allocation in proportion to current emissions; transaction costs, banking permits and price limits are left out.
  • Public goods (view 3). A "public goods game", as used in many classroom and laboratory experiments: each household has £10 a round and chooses how much to give; every £1 given creates the chosen benefit, shared equally by all households. Free riders never give; altruists give most of their £10; conditional co-operators give a little less than they saw others give last round (Fischbacher, Gächter and Fehr, 2001). A compulsory tax is spent on the good too. Illustrative shares of each type.
  • The market for lemons (view 4, Akerlof 1970). Each round 60 used cars come up, of random quality (spread evenly from worthless to £10,000 to their owner). A buyer values a car at the chosen multiple of its owner's value, but cannot see its quality, so offers what an average car sold last round was worth. Owners whose car is worth more to them than that keep it. Cars with an inspection report or warranty sell at a price based on their known quality.
  • Left out: market power, the long run (firms entering or switching to cleaner technology), pollution building up over time, and the costs of running each policy (except bargaining costs). All market values are illustrative; UK policy rates and dates come from HMRC, DEFRA, DESNZ and the Environment Agency (see "UK examples").
