Market Failure and Externalities · Teacher notesexternalities.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The river

What to look for: The market makes about 20 a day, where private benefit = private cost. But each tonne also costs the town £40, so the social cost (MSC) is £40 higher than the private cost. The best amount for society is where MSB = MSC: 16 a day. The 4 extra tonnes each cost society more than they are worth: the shaded triangle is the welfare loss.

Investigation 2: A tax on pollution

What to look for: A tax equal to the external cost makes the firm pay the full social cost: it 'internalises' the externality. Output falls to 16, the price rises and the welfare loss almost vanishes. A smaller tax leaves some loss; a bigger one makes too little, which is a loss too. This is a Pigouvian tax. The batch run 'Where should the tax be set?' sweeps it.

Investigation 3: Too few flu jabs

What to look for: With an external benefit, the market makes too little: people only count their own benefit. Here people also under-rate the jab, so even fewer are bought. A subsidy lowers the price and raises the quantity towards the social optimum (where MSB = MSC). Information campaigns can also close the gap.

Investigation 4: Sugary drinks

What to look for: Two failures here: an external cost (NHS) and an information gap (people think the drinks are better for them than they are). An information campaign closes only the gap; a tax can deal with both; a quota fixes the quantity but raises no money and is hard to set right. The UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy (2018) taxed sugary drinks by sugar content.

Investigation 5: Cap and trade

What to look for: Firms that find cutting expensive buy permits; firms that can cut cheaply sell them and cut more. Trading stops when every firm's marginal cost of cutting is the same: the permit price (about £40 a tonne here). The cap is met at the lowest total cost, much lower than making every firm cut by the same share.

Investigation 6: Too many permits

What to look for: The price falls to zero: permits are worthless, and nobody cuts anything. This happened in the first phase of the EU Emissions Trading System (2005–07), when too many permits were handed out. A cap only works if it is tight.

Investigation 7: Free riders

What to look for: Each £1 you give brings you back only £4 ÷ 12 = 33p, so keeping your money is always better for you, yet everyone would be better off if everyone gave. Free riders give nothing; others start generous but give less when they see free riders. That is why public goods like street lights are paid for by compulsory taxes.

Investigation 8: A compulsory tax

What to look for: With everyone paying, the pot is £120 and makes £480 of lighting: £40 each, against £10 if they kept their money. Everyone, including the would-be free riders, is better off. Non-excludable goods are underprovided by markets, so the state provides them.

Investigation 9: The market for lemons A level

What to look for: Buyers offer a price based on average quality. Owners of good cars find that too low and withdraw. The average quality falls, so the offer falls, and more good cars leave. The market unravels until only lemons are left (Akerlof, 1970). Inspection reports, warranties and dealers' reputations are fixes: try 'Cars with inspection reports'.

Investigation 10: Cutting costs with permits A level

What to look for: If every firm has the same costs, equal cuts are already the cheapest way and trading changes nothing. The more firms differ, the more there is to gain from letting the cheap cutters do most of the cutting. This is why economists favour permits or a carbon tax over uniform regulation.

Batch experiment 1: Does a free market overproduce?

The market lands near 20 a day, well above the social optimum of 16. Traders' prices wobble a little from day to day, so the quantity varies by a unit or two.
Theory (market theory): 20
Result from a test run: mean 19.9, SD 0.691, SE 0.0489 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 2: Where should the tax be set?

A U shape with its lowest point at a tax equal to the external cost (£40): a Pigouvian tax. The measured losses sit a little above the triangle: when traders misjudge, sometimes the wrong buyers and sellers trade, which wastes a little surplus of its own.
