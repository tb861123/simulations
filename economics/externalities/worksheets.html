Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Does a free market overproduce?

What this shows. Each run is a new market of 40 buyers and 40 sellers who know only their own values and costs. They settle close to where demand meets the PRIVATE supply curve: about 20 units a day (gold line), not the social optimum of 16 units where MSB = MSC. Nobody in the market pays the £40 external cost, so it does not affect what they do: the market overproduces by about 4 units a day. The mean is a little above 20 because real traders sometimes make an extra deal between a buyer and seller just beyond the margin. Runs scatter (SD about 2 units) because each market's own traders give step-shaped curves that are not exactly the theory lines, and because the order in which traders call out changes who trades.

2 · Where should the tax be set?

What this shows. The welfare loss falls as the tax rises towards the marginal external cost (£40: a Pigouvian tax) and rises again when the tax is bigger than the external cost, because now too LITTLE is produced. The theory curve is ½ × (MEC − tax)² ÷ (slope of demand + slope of supply), a U shape with its bottom at zero. The measured losses sit above the curve everywhere, by about £10–£35 a day: these traders are not perfectly efficient. Some deals that should happen are missed, some that should not happen are made, and sometimes a buyer who values the good less gets it before one who values it more. That costs about 2% of the market's total surplus, on top of the triangle. The lowest measured losses are still at about the Pigouvian tax.

3 · Subsidise a positive externality

What this shows. Firms that train apprentices ignore the benefit to other firms, so a free market trains too few: about 20 units, against an optimum of 24 (grey line). A subsidy per apprentice lowers firms' costs and shifts supply down, so the quantity rises in a straight line (gold). It reaches the optimum when the subsidy equals the marginal external benefit, £2,400; a bigger subsidy overshoots. Runs scatter by about one unit because each market has its own traders.

4 · Cap and trade: where does the permit price settle?

What this shows. A tighter cap means firms must cut more, so the permits they need are scarcer and the price is higher: the price falls in a straight line as the cap rises, reaching zero once the cap is at or above what firms emit anyway (700 thousand tonnes). At zero nobody needs to cut: over-allocation, as in the first phase of the EU ETS (2005–07). The trade prices land close to the theory p*, where every firm's cost of cutting one more tonne is equal. They scatter a little because each deal is agreed somewhere between the buyer's and seller's costs, and permits move in lots of 2,000 tonnes; each run also has its own random firms.

5 · Free riders: do people keep giving?

What this shows. Here theory fails at first. Standard theory says a selfish household gives nothing to a public good (red line, 0%), because each £1 it gives brings it back only 33p. People are not that selfish at first: in round 1 the street gives about 40% of its money. But conditional co-operators give a little less than they saw others give, and free riders give nothing, so contributions fall round after round, towards the selfish prediction, far below what is best for the street (100%). This is what classroom and laboratory experiments find (Ledyard, 1995; Fehr and Gächter, 2000). Runs scatter because each street has its own mix of people.

6 · The market for lemons (A level)

What this shows. With asymmetric information buyers only pay for average quality, so the owners of the best cars keep them. If a car is worth less than twice as much to a buyer as to its owner (r < 2), the market unravels round after round: after 8 rounds almost no cars are sold, although every sale would make both sides better off. At r ≥ 2 even the best car's owner accepts the price for an average car, and every car sells. The points follow (r ÷ 2)^8; they scatter because each round brings 60 new cars of random quality, so the average quality buyers see is noisy. Try 'Share of cars with an inspection report' in the table: each inspected car always sells.
