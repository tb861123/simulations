The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Perfect competition: does price fall to the lowest AC?

What this shows. Each run starts with 4 firms in a market with room for about 30. Supernormal profit pulls in entrants until each firm earns about normal profit at the bottom of its AC curve, so the long-run price ends up within about 2% of the lowest AC (ratio ≈ 1). The theory value is a little above 1 because firms come in whole numbers: the 30th firm would make a loss. Runs scatter because demand moves at random each quarter (±3%) and each firm's costs differ slightly, so a few firms leave after bad years and others come in.

2 · Barriers to entry: how many firms survive?

What this shows. One firm starts in a market with room for about 14. An entrant comes in only if five years of its expected profit beat the sunk cost of entering (money it can never get back: a factory, advertising, research). The bigger the sunk cost, the fewer firms enter: from about 14 (competition) down to 1 (monopoly) above about £5.5 million. 4 · Cartel: do more members make it collapse?

What this shows. With 2–4 members each firm's share of future cartel profit is big enough that cheating never pays, so the cartel lasts until the authorities catch it (about 20 years with a 5% chance a year). With 5 or more, each share is small and the gain from cheating (taking the whole market for a year) is large, so it collapses within a year or two. The theory: loyalty pays when (1 + demand change) × (N − 1) < δ′ ÷ (1 − δ′). Lifetimes scatter a lot, as they should: whether a cartel ends is random each year, so lives follow a geometric distribution whose SD is about as big as its mean.

5 · Cartel: does a bigger chance of being caught help?

What this shows. A higher chance of being caught ends cartels directly, but it also makes the future worth less to members (δ′ = δ × (1 − chance)), so above about 12% a year members start to cheat as well, and lives fall sharply. This is one reason for leniency programmes: the first member to report a cartel to the CMA can escape the fine, which raises the chance of detection and the temptation to break ranks. Single lives scatter widely (geometric distribution); the means follow the theory.

6 · Strategy tournament: is tit-for-tat best?

What this shows. Each of six strategies plays every strategy (and a copy of itself) for 50 years. With no mistakes tit-for-tat and grim trigger come top (about £5.4m a year), because they cooperate with cooperators and punish undercutters. With 5% mistakes, tit-for-tat's average falls to about £4.5m: one mistake starts an echo of punishments between two tit-for-tat firms. Win-stay, lose-shift copes better with mistakes. Change 'Strategy to record' to compare. Runs scatter because the mistakes fall at random; the theory is the exact average over every possible history.
