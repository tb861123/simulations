How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • One firm. Average variable cost is U-shaped: AVC = m × (1 + k × ((Q − Qv) ÷ Qv)²), lowest (m) at output Qv. It falls at first (workers specialise, machines are used fully) and then rises because of diminishing returns to the variable factor (more workers sharing the same ovens). TC = FC + Q × AVC; AC = TC ÷ Q; MC = ΔTC ÷ ΔQ. A price taker's AR = MR = the market price. A price maker faces a straight-line demand curve P = a × (1 − Q ÷ Qmax), so MR = a × (1 − 2Q ÷ Qmax). Each day the firm moves part of the way towards the output that best meets its objective, so you can watch MR and MC come together.
  • Costs include normal profit. Economists count the owner's normal profit (what they could earn elsewhere) as a cost, so "profit" on screen is supernormal (abnormal) profit: zero means normal profit.
  • Shutdown and exit (A level). In the short run a firm keeps producing if the price covers average variable cost, because the fixed costs must be paid anyway. Below the lowest AVC it shuts down (losses = fixed costs). In the long run it leaves if the price is below the lowest AC.
  • Price discrimination (A level). Third degree: two groups with different demand curves, which cannot resell to each other; the firm sets MR₁ = MR₂ = MC, charging more to the group with less elastic demand. First degree: each buyer pays the most they would pay, so the firm sells up to where demand = MC and takes all the consumer surplus.
  • Economies of scale. The long-run average cost is a starting cost per unit, reduced by each internal economy you tick (each one gives a set saving that builds up over a range of sizes: technical first, risk-bearing last) and raised by each diseconomy above a large size. Each factory size has its own short-run AC curve, which touches the LRAC at one output only. External economies and diseconomies move the whole LRAC down or up as the industry in the area grows. The sizes of the savings are illustrative.
  • A market of many firms. Each firm has cost C = F + c Q + e Q² (fixed costs F include normal profit), so AC = F ÷ Q + c + e Q is U-shaped with its minimum (the efficient size) at √(F ÷ e). Firm i's price is P = a − b × (its output) − g × (its rivals' output), where g = b for identical products and g is smaller (each firm has loyal customers) when products are differentiated. Price takers produce where P = MC; other firms choose the output that maximises profit given rivals' last outputs (Cournot). Firms adjust 30% of the way each quarter.
  • Entry and exit. Each quarter, possible entrants work out the profit they would make after entering (market research) over five years. If it beats the sunk cost of entry they come in, each with a 30% chance a quarter. Brand loyalty cuts an entrant's demand; a patent or licence stops entry. A firm making a loss for four quarters in a row may leave (a 35% chance each quarter after that: long run); a firm whose price would not cover its average variable cost makes nothing that quarter (short-run shutdown).
  • Contestable market (A level). Entrants can come and go freely ("hit and run"), judging by today's price. Incumbents know this and keep the price close to average cost (limit pricing), so even a monopoly earns little supernormal profit when sunk costs are low.
  • Price game (A level). Each year two firms choose a high or low price. Profits follow the payoff table (a prisoners' dilemma: each does better by undercutting, but both do worse when both undercut). Mistakes flip a move at random. Theory values are worked out exactly (the probability of every possible history), not by simulation.
  • Cartel (A level). Each year demand is up or down at random. A member that cheats takes the whole market for a year; after that the others punish it with a price war (profit falls to zero), so it loses its share of all future cartel profits. A firm cheats when (1 + demand change) × (N − 1) > δ′ ÷ (1 − δ′), with δ′ = δ × (1 − chance of being caught). The cartel also ends if the authorities catch it. In the live view a price war lasts three years, then the cartel forms again.
  • Kinked demand (A level). Rivals match a price cut but not a rise, so demand is elastic above the current price and inelastic below. MR has a gap at the kink, and the price stays put while MC moves inside the gap.
  • Left out: how firms raise money, mergers and takeovers (only mentioned), advertising choices, quality, location, the time it takes to build a factory, and how the CMA decides cases. Numbers are illustrative unless a source is given.
