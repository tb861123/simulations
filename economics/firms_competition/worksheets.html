Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Where is profit greatest?

What to look for: Profit = total revenue − total cost, the gap between the TR and TC curves. It is greatest where the curves are furthest apart, which is where their slopes are equal: marginal revenue = marginal cost. Producing one more beyond that adds more to cost than to revenue.

Investigation 2: Fixed and variable costs

What to look for: Fixed costs (£400 here: rent, machines) do not change with output, so spreading them over more units makes the average fall. Variable costs per unit rise as the factory gets crowded (diminishing returns). Together they give a U-shaped average cost, lowest at 40 units (£30 each).

Investigation 3: Different objectives

What to look for: Revenue maximisation (MR = 0) and sales maximisation (as much as possible without a loss, AR = AC) both mean more output and a lower price than profit maximisation. Managers may be paid by sales or market share, or a firm may want to grow and keep rivals out.

Investigation 4: Profit attracts entry

What to look for: Supernormal profit with no barriers draws new firms in, raising supply and pushing the price down. Entry stops when profit is normal (zero economic profit): the price ends near the lowest average cost (£30). That is productive efficiency, and price = marginal cost is allocative efficiency.

Investigation 5: A monopoly

What to look for: The monopolist restricts output to where MR = MC and charges a high price, well above average cost, so it keeps supernormal profit for ever. Consumers pay more and buy less; there is a deadweight loss. Patents allow this deliberately, to reward invention.

Investigation 6: Barriers to entry

What to look for: High sunk costs (advertising, research, specialist factories) mean newcomers need years of profit to pay back the investment, so only a few firms stay and prices stay above average cost. Low barriers make the market contestable: the threat of entry keeps prices down even with few firms.

Investigation 7: The prisoner's dilemma A level

What to look for: Whatever the rival does, charging Low pays more (15 > 10 if they go High; 5 > 2 if they go Low): Low is dominant, and both Low (5, 5) is the Nash equilibrium. But both High (10, 10) would be better for both. That is why firms are tempted to collude, and why cartels are illegal.

Investigation 8: Tit for tat A level

What to look for: Repeated games change everything: tit for tat cooperates with cooperators and punishes undercutters, so collusion can last without any agreement (tacit collusion). Mistakes can set off long price wars between strict strategies; forgiving ones like win-stay, lose-shift recover.

Batch experiment 4: Barriers and the number of firms

High barriers protect the firms already there, so the market stays concentrated and prices stay higher.

Batch experiment 5: Which pricing strategy earns most?

The ranking depends on who else is playing. Here always-low comes top because it exploits the naive always-high and random players; grim trigger and win-stay, lose-shift come close by cooperating with each other and punishing undercutters. In Axelrod's famous tournaments, with fewer naive players, tit for tat won.
