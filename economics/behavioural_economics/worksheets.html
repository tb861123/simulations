Behavioural Economics · Teacher notesbehavioural-economics.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The power of the default

What to look for: Changing your status takes effort, so many people stick with the default (inertia, bounded rationality). Opt-out raised UK private-sector participation from 42% (2012) to 86% (2019) with no change in the pension itself.

Investigation 2: Who is nudged the wrong way?

What to look for: A default that suits most people catches a few for whom it is wrong (those with debts to pay off first, say). Active choice makes everyone decide: fewer mistakes, but it takes everyone's effort.

Investigation 3: Anchored by a phone number

What to look for: Yes: people with high numbers bid more. In Ariely's experiment, people in the top fifth of numbers bid two to three times as much as the bottom fifth. Shops use anchors too: 'was £99, now £49'.

Investigation 4: Paying not to go to the gym

What to look for: When the time comes, today's effort looms larger than the future benefit (β < 1), so people go less than planned and pay more per visit than the £10 pay-as-you-go price. DellaVigna and Malmendier found US members paid about $17 a visit.

Investigation 5: Follow the crowd

What to look for: Once two more people have chosen one place, it is rational to ignore your own signal and follow: an information cascade. Then nobody's private information is added, so a cascade can lock onto the wrong choice (about one crowd in seven at 70%).

Investigation 6: Rejecting free money

What to look for: Low offers are rejected as unfair, so the proposer earns most by offering 30–40%. Real ultimatum games around the world show the same. With rational responders (fairness 0) the best offer would be almost nothing.

Investigation 7: The boomerang A level

What to look for: High users cut back, but low users learn they are below average and drift up: the boomerang effect. Praise (an injunctive norm, 😊) stops the drift. Real letters (Opower) cut use by about 2%.

Investigation 8: Nudge or regulation? A level

What to look for: A nudge keeps freedom of choice and is cheap, but it works least on people who are most active and may lead some to the wrong choice; its effects can fade; and someone has to decide what the default should be (paternalism).

Batch experiment 1: Opt-in, active choice or opt-out?

The pension is the same; only the default changes. Active choice shows what people would choose if acting cost nothing (88%).
Theory (expected): 1: 55 · 2: 88.5 · 3: 96.7
Result from a test run: 1: 55.1 ± 1.03 · 2: 88.3 ± 0.578 · 3: 96.7 ± 0.302 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Inertia and opt-in

With no inertia people choose rationally (88%). The more effort acting takes, the more stay with the default of not saving.
Theory (expected): 0: 88.5 · 0.25: 74.4 · 0.5: 59.8 · 0.75: 49 · 1: 41.3 · 1.5: 31.3
Result from a test run: 0: 89.6 ± 0.347 · 0.25: 73.8 ± 0.724 · 0.5: 60 ± 0.854 · 0.75: 49 ± 1.25 · 1: 41.8 ± 0.656 · 1.5: 32.1 ± 0.942 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Anchoring: bids by phone number

A rational bidder ignores the anchor (a flat line at £30). Here bids rise by 30p for every point of a meaningless number.
Theory (0.7 × value + 0.3 × anchor): 10: 24 · 30: 30 · 50: 36 · 70: 42 · 90: 48
Result from a test run: 10: 24.6 ± 0.363 · 30: 29.8 ± 0.454 · 50: 36.2 ± 0.574 · 70: 41.6 ± 0.162 · 90: 48.5 ± 0.33 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Present bias and the price of a visit

With β = 1 members go 6 times (£8.33 a visit, cheaper than pay-as-you-go). With strong present bias they pay far more per visit than the £10 they avoided.
Theory (£50 ÷ expected visits): 0.6: 25 · 0.7: 16.7 · 0.8: 12.5 · 0.9: 10 · 1: 8.33
Result from a test run: 0.6: 25.1 ± 0.406 · 0.7: 16.8 ± 0.277 · 0.8: 12.6 ± 0.133 · 0.9: 10.1 ± 0.136 · 1: 8.85 ± 0.028 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: How often does the herd go wrong?

Even with fairly good information a cascade locks onto the wrong place surprisingly often, because after two people agree, nobody else's information is used.
Theory (cascade theory): 0.55: 40.1 · 0.6: 30.8 · 0.7: 15.5 · 0.8: 5.88 · 0.9: 1.22
Result from a test run: 0.55: 38 ± 4.88 · 0.6: 28 ± 4.51 · 0.7: 11 ± 3.14 · 0.8: 6 ± 2.39 · 0.9: 0 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 100 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: Ultimatum: the most profitable offer

The best offer is about 40%: well above the near-zero offer a model of purely selfish, rational people would predict.
Theory ((1 − share) × chance accepted): 0.1: 0.205 · 0.2: 1.27 · 0.3: 3.5 · 0.4: 5.05 · 0.5: 4.89 · 0.6: 3.99
Result from a test run: 0.1: 0.27 ± 0.0722 · 0.2: 1.22 ± 0.131 · 0.3: 3.7 ± 0.215 · 0.4: 5 ± 0.134 · 0.5: 4.94 ± 0.0221 · 0.6: 4 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 7: Social-norm letters across 200 towns

Small: the boomerang from low users cancels part of the saving from high users. Adding praise for low users makes the fall bigger.
Result from a test run: mean -0.806, SD 0.0773, SE 0.00547 from 200 runs
