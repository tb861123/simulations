1 · Who pays? It depends on PED

What this shows. Each dot compares the same 160 traders with and without a £1 tax. When demand is inelastic (|PED| 0.2), consumers pay most of the tax (about 83%); when it is elastic (|PED| 5), producers pay most (consumers about 17%). The dots follow PES ÷ (PES − PED). They scatter because each market's own step curves are not exactly straight lines, so its prices sit a few pence off the theory; with 80 traders each side that moves the share by about ±10 percentage points.

2 · Who pays? It depends on PES

What this shows. Now demand is fixed (PED −1) and supply varies. When supply is inelastic (PES 0.2: sellers cannot easily do anything else), producers pay most of the tax; when supply is elastic, consumers pay most. The rule of thumb: whichever side is LESS able to respond to the price pays more of the tax. Scatter comes from each market's own random traders.

3 · Deadweight loss ∝ tax²?

What this shows. The deadweight loss is a triangle: ½ × tax × fall in quantity, and the fall in quantity is itself proportional to the tax, so the loss grows with the SQUARE of the tax (gradient 2 on log–log axes): doubling a tax makes the loss four times bigger. This is measured as economists do from data: the tax wedge and the fall in the number of trades, each market compared with the same traders untaxed. Small taxes scatter most, because the fall in quantity (a unit or two) is close to the day-to-day noise. Measuring the lost surplus directly ('Surplus lost' outcome) is muddier: traders' own mistakes near the margin in the untaxed market partly hide the loss.

4 · Does a higher tax always raise more?

What this shows. Revenue = tax × quantity. A small tax raises more as it grows, but a big tax shrinks the quantity so much that revenue falls again: a hump. With |PED| 3 and PES 1 the top is at about £1.67 (two-thirds of the £2.50 price), and a £3.33 tax would wipe the market out. With inelastic demand (fuel, tobacco) the hump is much further out, which is one reason governments tax those goods. Scatter: each market's own traders.

5 · Maximum price: how big is the shortage?

What this shows. A maximum price 20% below the free-market price. The shortage (buyers willing to pay the maximum who could not buy) grows in a straight line with the elasticity of demand: more elastic demand means many extra buyers come in at the lower price, on top of the sellers who drop out. Theory: shortage = Q × 20% × (|PED| + PES) = 6 × (|PED| + 1) here. Dots scatter because each market's random traders do not sit exactly on the straight lines.

6 · Queues waste more than the triangle

What this shows. The textbook deadweight-loss triangle (gold) assumes the scarce goods go to the buyers who value them most. In this market they go to whoever calls first, a queue, so some go to buyers who value them only a little above the maximum price. The measured loss follows the grey 'random rationing' line, two to three times the triangle at low maximum prices. This is where the simple theory fails, and part of why price controls can cost more than they seem. A black market would claw some of it back, at the cost of breaking the law.
