Taxes, Subsidies and Price Controls · Teacher notestaxes-price-controls.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Who pays a tax?

What to look for: Between: here consumers pay about two-thirds and the coffee shops about a third. The tax drives a wedge between the price consumers pay and what sellers keep; fewer cups are sold.

Investigation 2: Fuel duty and VAT

What to look for: About 60% of the pump price is tax. Demand for petrol is inelastic (|PED| ≈ 0.3), so drivers bear most of it and the quantity hardly falls: one reason fuel is taxed so heavily.

Investigation 3: The sugar levy

What to look for: Demand for sugary drinks is fairly elastic (many substitutes), so the quantity falls a lot: that was the aim. In practice firms cut the sugar: average sugar in soft drinks fell 44% between 2015 and 2019 (Public Health England).

Investigation 4: VAT: an ad valorem tax

What to look for: An ad valorem tax is a percentage of the price, so the supply curve pivots (the gap grows with price) instead of shifting parallel. VAT has been 20% in the UK since January 2011.

Investigation 5: Energy Price Guarantee

What to look for: Consumers pay much less; with inelastic demand most of the subsidy goes to them, and consumption rises a little. The cost is the subsidy × quantity: tens of millions of pounds a day for electricity alone.

Investigation 6: Rent control

What to look for: A maximum price below equilibrium: more people want flats, fewer are offered, so there is a shortage. Who gets one is down to luck (or connections), not who values it most.

Investigation 7: Ticket touts

What to look for: A shortage at £50, and a black market: tickets end up with keener fans at a much higher price. The theory black-market price is what buyers would pay for the number of tickets available.

Investigation 8: Minimum unit pricing

What to look for: A minimum price above the free-market price of cheap alcohol: drinkers of cheap, strong drinks pay more and buy less. Unlike a tax, the extra money goes to sellers, not the government.

Investigation 9: Farm price support A level

What to look for: Farmers sell more than consumers want; the government must buy the surplus and store or destroy it (the EU's 'grain mountains'). Cost = minimum price × surplus, every day: a government failure.

Investigation 10: Incidence and elasticity A level

What to look for: With |PED| 0.4 and PES 2, consumers pay 2 ÷ 2.4 = 83%. When supply is inelastic, sellers cannot escape and bear most of the tax. Whichever side is less able to walk away pays more.

Investigation 11: Deadweight loss A level

What to look for: It roughly quadruples: the triangle grows with the square of the tax (½ × tax × fall in quantity, and the fall is proportional to the tax).

Batch experiment 1: Who pays? It depends on PED

The more elastic the demand, the less of the tax consumers pay: they can walk away, so sellers must absorb it.
Theory (PES ÷ (PES + |PED|)): 0.2: 83.3 · 0.5: 66.7 · 1: 50 · 2: 33.3 · 5: 16.7
Result from a test run: 0.2: 95.3 ± 2.93 · 0.5: 69.6 ± 1.42 · 1: 47.8 ± 1.46 · 2: 30.6 ± 1.92 · 5: 11.2 ± 1.07 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Who pays? It depends on PES

Elastic supply passes most of the tax on to consumers; inelastic supply (a fixed number of seats, flats or acres) means sellers bear it.
Theory (PES ÷ (PES + |PED|)): 0.2: 16.7 · 0.5: 33.3 · 1: 50 · 2: 66.7 · 5: 83.3
Result from a test run: 0.2: 21.7 ± 3.93 · 0.5: 35.4 ± 1.81 · 1: 47.8 ± 1.46 · 2: 68.9 ± 1.9 · 5: 87.8 ± 1.23 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Does a higher tax always raise more?

Revenue rises, peaks and falls: a high tax shrinks the market so much that it raises less. Governments use elasticities to set tax rates.
Theory (straight-line theory): 0: 0 · 10: 1.85 · 20: 3.4 · 30: 4.65 · 40: 5.6 · 50: 6.25 · 60: 6.6 · 70: 6.65
Result from a test run: 0: 0 ± 0 · 10: 2.12 ± 0.0189 · 20: 3.88 ± 0.0432 · 30: 5.26 ± 0.0826 · 40: 6.38 ± 0.0683 · 50: 7.17 ± 0.0479 · 60: 7.62 ± 0.0346 · 70: 7.63 ± 0.146 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Deadweight loss and the size of the tax

The loss grows roughly with the square of the tax: doubling the tax about quadruples it. Every unit lost was worth more to a buyer than it cost a seller to make: nobody gets that value, not even the government.
Theory (triangle ½ × t × ΔQ): 0: 0 · 10: 0.075 · 20: 0.3 · 30: 0.675 · 40: 1.2 · 50: 1.87
Result from a test run: 0: 0 ± 0 · 10: 0.062 ± 0.0168 · 20: 0.21 ± 0.0395 · 30: 0.639 ± 0.0716 · 40: 1.42 ± 0.213 · 50: 1.81 ± 0.194 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Maximum price: how big is the shortage?

The further below equilibrium, the bigger the shortage; above equilibrium the maximum price does nothing (it is not binding).
Theory (Qd − Qs): 50: 20 · 60: 16 · 70: 12 · 80: 8 · 90: 4 · 100: 0 · 110: 0
Result from a test run: 50: 20.1 ± 0.049 · 60: 16.4 ± 0.0894 · 70: 12.5 ± 0.196 · 80: 9.16 ± 0.172 · 90: 5.8 ± 0.228 · 100: 2.76 ± 0.232 · 110: 0.88 ± 0.242 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)
