How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Traders. Each buyer wants at most one unit a day and has a private value; each seller can supply one unit a day and has a private cost. Values and costs are spread evenly, so the theory curves are straight lines through the free-market price with the PED and PES you choose. Each trader stands for a block of real buyers or sellers (the product's unit says how many).
  • Trading is a continuous double auction with learning ("ZIP") traders, as in the Supply, Demand and Elasticity simulation: nobody knows the curves, but prices settle where the (taxed) supply meets demand.
  • Taxes and subsidies are paid per unit sold, by the seller to the government. The seller needs the consumer price to cover cost + tax, so the supply curve shifts up by the tax (a specific tax: a parallel shift) or pivots (an ad valorem tax: the gap grows with the price). VAT is charged on top of any specific duty, as on UK fuel. A subsidy shifts supply down.
  • Who pays. Consumers' share = (rise in the price they pay) ÷ (tax per unit). On straight-line curves this is exactly PES ÷ (PES − PED) (PED negative), using the elasticities at the free-market price.
  • Maximum price (below the free-market price): no deal can be above it. More buyers want the good than sellers will supply: a shortage. Who gets it is decided by who calls first (a queue), not by who values it most, so the loss of surplus is BIGGER than the textbook triangle (which assumes the keenest buyers are served). With a black market, a buyer who got the good can resell it (illegally) to someone who missed out and values it more, at a price halfway between their two values; each possible resale happens with a chance of 60% (not everyone dares).
  • Minimum price (above the free-market price): no deal can be below it. Sellers want to sell more than buyers will buy: a surplus (glut). If the government buys the surplus at the minimum price, it pays for goods nobody consumes, and the resources used to make them are wasted.
  • Surplus. Consumer surplus = Σ(value − price paid); producer surplus = Σ(amount kept − cost); tax revenue = Σ(tax). Deadweight loss = the most surplus the market could make − (consumer + producer surplus + tax revenue).
  • Real examples use UK rates with their year; the elasticities, costs and quantities are illustrative values in the range of published estimates, chosen to show the idea, not to forecast.
  • Left out: tax evasion and smuggling (except the black market under a maximum price), firms changing their products to avoid a tax (as many drinks makers did with the sugar levy), the cost of collecting taxes and policing controls, quality falling under a maximum price, the long-run entry and exit of firms, and what the government does with the revenue.
