Running the Economy · Teacher notesrunning-the-economy.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Five targets at once

What to look for: The economy moves in a business cycle: booms and slowdowns come and go even when nobody changes policy, because random shocks are amplified by momentum (spending begets spending). It is rare to hit all five targets together, and the budget target is usually missed: the UK government has run a deficit in almost every year since 2002.

Investigation 2: Cooling inflation

What to look for: Higher interest rates make borrowing dearer and saving more attractive, so households and firms spend less; demand falls, firms cut prices and hire fewer people. Inflation falls, but only after a year or two (the lag), and unemployment rises meanwhile. The pound strengthens too, making imports cheaper. Hold the rate too long and you cause a recession: this is why the Bank moves in small steps.

Investigation 3: A credit crunch

What to look for: Spending collapses, the economy goes into recession and unemployment climbs. The Bank cuts Bank Rate sharply, and may hit the floor near zero, at which point it uses quantitative easing. Extra government spending (fiscal stimulus) supports demand directly and shortens the recession, but adds to the deficit and the national debt.

Investigation 4: Energy prices jump

What to look for: A cost shock pushes inflation up and output down at the same time (stagflation), so the objectives conflict. Raise rates to fight inflation and you deepen the slowdown; keep them low and inflation stays high for longer and people may come to expect it. The Bank's answer in 2022–23 was to raise Bank Rate from 0.1% to 5.25%.

Investigation 5: The budget

What to look for: The deficit falls, but by less than 2%, because cutting spending slows the economy: tax receipts fall and benefit payments rise (automatic stabilisers). Unemployment rises. The Bank of England cuts rates to compensate, which softens the blow. This conflict between a sound budget and growth is at the heart of arguments about austerity.

Investigation 6: Supply-side policy

What to look for: In the short run the spending just adds to demand (and the deficit). After about two years it raises the economy's capacity: trend growth rises and the natural rate of unemployment falls, so unemployment can be lower without inflation rising. Supply-side policies are slow and uncertain but are the only lasting way to raise living standards.

Investigation 7: The real UK economy

What to look for: 2008–09: a financial crisis; growth −4.5%, unemployment up to 8%, Bank Rate slashed to 0.5%. 2020: Covid-19; output fell 10% but furlough kept unemployment low. 2022: energy prices; inflation 9.1% for the year (11.1% at its peak), and the Bank raised rates eleven times in a row.

Investigation 8: Banks create money

What to look for: Each bank keeps a fraction as reserves and lends the rest; the loan is spent and deposited at another bank, which lends most of it again. The total grows to several times the original: banks create money when they lend. Keeping more in reserve, or people holding more cash, shrinks the multiplier. In reality (Bank of England, 2014) lending is limited mainly by how many creditworthy people want to borrow.

Investigation 9: The Phillips curve A level

What to look for: With fixed expectations you get a trade-off: lower unemployment, somewhat higher inflation (the short-run Phillips curve). With adaptive expectations, people come to expect the higher inflation, which pushes it higher still, and so on: inflation keeps rising as long as unemployment is held below its natural rate. In the long run there is no trade-off: the long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate.

Investigation 10: Time lags A level

What to look for: With long lags the Bank is always reacting to yesterday's economy: it raises rates in a boom that, by the time they bite, has already turned. Policy can then add to the swings instead of smoothing them. This is one argument for rules, forecasting, and caution about fine-tuning.

Investigation 11: Crowding out A level

What to look for: With the rate fixed, the output gap rises by about 1.6% (a multiplier of about 0.8). When the Bank reacts to the extra demand by raising rates, private spending falls, and output rises by only about half as much: higher interest rates crowd out private investment and consumption. Fiscal and monetary policy need to work together.
