How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • One step is a quarter. The economy is described by a few numbers: the output gap (how far real GDP is above or below its potential), CPI inflation, unemployment, Bank Rate, the budget balance, government debt and the current account.
  • Business cycles. Real GDP grows along a trend of 1.5% a year (potential growth). Random changes in demand (confidence, house prices, world demand) push it above or below; the economy has momentum, so these shocks turn into booms and recessions about six years apart. A recession is two quarters in a row of falling real GDP.
  • Monetary policy. A higher Bank Rate (relative to expected inflation) makes borrowing dearer, saving more attractive and the pound stronger, so spending falls, but only gradually: the effect builds up over the time lag (about a year by default; the Bank of England says up to two years for the full effect on inflation). The neutral Bank Rate, at which policy neither speeds up nor slows the economy, is 3% (1% real + 2% inflation): illustrative. QE: each £100bn of bonds bought lowers longer-term interest rates by about 0.5 percentage points (illustrative).
  • The Bank of England on autopilot uses a Taylor-style rule (beyond the spec: 9EC0 does not name the Taylor rule): Bank Rate target = 1% + inflation + 0.5 × (inflation − 2%) + 0.5 × output gap, moved 40% of the way each quarter, never below 0.1%. The real Monetary Policy Committee votes on forecasts and judgement, not a formula, and usually moves in steps of 0.25.
  • Fiscal policy. Extra government spending raises the output gap by 0.8 × the change (a multiplier below 1, as the OBR assumes for the UK); tax rises lower it by 0.5 × the change. Both act after a shorter lag. The budget balance also moves with the cycle (automatic stabilisers).
  • Unemployment follows Okun's law: it moves towards the natural rate (4.5%) minus 0.5 × the output gap.
  • Inflation follows an expectations-augmented Phillips curve: it moves towards expected inflation + κ × output gap, plus random cost shocks (energy, food, import prices) that fade away. Expected inflation stays near 2% when the Bank is credible; with low credibility it follows past inflation, so a boom pushes up expected inflation and the short-run trade-off shifts (the long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate).
  • Supply-side policy (training, infrastructure, research) costs money now; after about two years it raises potential growth (by up to 0.3 percentage points a year for each 1% of GDP) and lowers the natural rate of unemployment. Illustrative values.
  • Budget, debt, current account. Budget balance = −2.5% of GDP (illustrative: the UK's deficit was nearer 5% in 2024–25) − extra spending + extra taxes − supply-side spending + 0.5 × output gap − the extra cost of interest. Debt starts at 95% of GDP and changes with borrowing and with nominal GDP growth. The current account starts at −2.5% of GDP and worsens in booms and when the pound is strong.
  • Money and banks (view 2, beyond the spec: enrichment): the textbook deposit multiplier. Banks keep a share of deposits as reserves and lend the rest; part of each payment is kept as cash. The Bank of England explains that in reality loans create deposits, and lending is limited by demand, risk and regulation rather than by reserves.
  • Left out: house prices, wages separately from prices, the world economy (except as shocks), financial markets, forecasts, and many more details. All model numbers are illustrative; the real UK data in view 3 come from the ONS and the Bank of England.
