Running the Economy · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Does the Bank of England hit 2%?

What this shows. Each run is ten years of random shocks to demand and costs, with the Bank setting Bank Rate by a Taylor-style rule. Average inflation lands very close to the 2% target (the mean of 300 futures), but single decades scatter by about 0.2 percentage points either way: a run of bad luck (an energy shock, a crash) moves inflation before policy can catch up, because policy works with a time lag. Targeting works on average, not every year: UK annual CPI has been more than 1 point from target in about half the years since 2008.

2 · The short-run Phillips curve (A level)

What this shows. Policy aims for an unemployment rate, and expected inflation stays at 2% (full credibility). Aiming below the natural rate (4.5%) needs a positive output gap, which keeps inflation above 2%; aiming above it keeps inflation below 2%. The points fall along a downward-sloping line: a short-run Phillips curve, inflation = 2% + κ × (4.5 − u) ÷ 0.5. Runs scatter because each future has its own shocks; at 3% the economy struggles to get there in 15 years, so inflation is a little below the line.

3 · Long run: expectations catch up (A level)

What this shows. Now expected inflation follows actual inflation. Holding unemployment below the natural rate pushes inflation up, then expectations up, then inflation up again: after ten years inflation is far above the short-run Phillips curve (gold), and still rising (grey: the model with no shocks). Only at the natural rate (4.5%) does inflation stay at 2%. This is the vertical long-run Phillips curve (Friedman and Phelps): there is no lasting trade-off. Runs scatter widely because every shock to inflation is built into expectations and never undone.

4 · Fiscal stimulus, Bank Rate fixed

What this shows. With Bank Rate held at its neutral level (3%), each extra 1% of GDP of government spending raises the output gap by the multiplier, 0.8 percentage points, after the time lags have passed. Scatter: each run has its own random shocks, averaged over the last two years only; the same 100 random futures are used at every step, so any luck in them shifts every point by the same small amount. The grey line shows what happens if the Bank of England reacts instead (next experiment).

5 · Fiscal stimulus when the Bank reacts

What this shows. Now the Bank of England raises Bank Rate as the extra demand pushes up inflation and the output gap. Higher rates reduce private spending (a kind of crowding out), so the stimulus raises output by only about half as much as with rates fixed (grey): the gold line is the long-run theory, F ÷ (1 + kr(1.5κ + 0.5)). Fiscal and monetary policy can pull in different directions: one reason fiscal policy is most powerful when Bank Rate is stuck at its floor.

6 · Banks create money: the money multiplier

What this shows. £100m of new reserves is lent, spent, re-deposited and lent again. With 10% kept as cash, the money created follows 1 + (1 − rr) ÷ (rr + c − rr c): about 9 times the reserves at rr = 2%, falling to about 3.9 at 20%. With no cash it would be the textbook 1 ÷ rr (grey): 50 times and 5 times. Cash leaking out of the banks flattens the log–log line (gradient much less steep than −1). Runs scatter a little because how much cash each payment keeps is random. But the Bank of England (2014) points out that in reality the causation runs the other way: banks lend when creditworthy borrowers want loans, and the central bank supplies the reserves needed. Lower 'demand for loans' in the table to see far less money created.
