Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Does hiring find the market wage?

What this shows. Nobody can see the supply and demand curves, yet the wage settles close to where they cross (£12). Runs scatter because each market's people are drawn at random: the number of employers with an MRP above £12 and of workers willing at £12 each varies by a few, which moves the wage that clears THESE people's market by about £1.70 either way (the blue curve is the spread theory predicts from that alone). Traders' own bargaining adds a little more.

2 · Minimum wage in a competitive market

What this shows. Below the market wage (£12) the minimum wage does not bite: employment stays at about 30. Above it, every rise cuts jobs along the demand curve for labour: at £16 (a third above the market wage) about 25, a fall of about 17%, because the elasticity of demand is 0.5. The grey line is what the monopsony model predicts with the same curves: there, the same minimum wages raise employment up to £12. Which model fits a real labour market is why economists disagree about minimum wages. Dots scatter because each run's people are drawn at random.

3 · Minimum wage in a monopsony

What this shows. With one employer, a minimum wage below the monopsony wage (about £7.80) changes nothing. Between £7.80 and the competitive wage (£12) employment RISES, from about 22 to 30, because the minimum wage makes hiring an extra worker cost only the minimum wage (MCL is flat). Above £12 it falls again, like the competitive model (grey). The measured mean falls a little short at the peak: there employment is the smaller of two random counts (workers willing, and jobs worth the wage), and the smaller of two is lower on average.

4 · A union in a monopsony

What this shows. With no bargaining power the employer pays the monopsony wage. As the union's power grows the wage rises towards the MRP of the monopsony's workers, and employment first RISES (the union wage acts like a minimum wage below the competitive wage) and then falls back, ending where it started when the union takes the whole gap between wage and MRP. So a union facing a monopsony can raise both pay and jobs, up to a point.

5 · Elastic labour demand: more jobs lost

What this shows. The National Living Wage (£12.71, April 2026) is 6% above this market's wage. The share of jobs lost is about the elasticity of labour demand × 6%: under 1% when demand is very inelastic (workers hard to replace), about 12% when it is elastic (machines or cheaper places are easy alternatives). Each run compares the people hired under the minimum wage with the number the same people would hire in an ideal market with no floor, so the difference is the minimum wage's effect. Single runs scatter a lot (a few people either way is several per cent); the means follow the theory.

6 · Skill shortages: fewer workers, higher pay

What this shows. 60 skilled jobs. When only 15 people have the skills, employers compete for them and the wage is far above the £18 that would clear the market with 60; with 120 qualified people it falls well below. Scarce skills are one reason for wage differentials (doctors, software developers, HGV drivers in 2021). Training more people shifts supply right and lowers the premium. Scatter: each run's people are drawn at random.
