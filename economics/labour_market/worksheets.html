The Labour Market · Teacher noteslabour-market.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: How wages are set

What to look for: The wage settles near the crossing of labour demand (MRP) and labour supply. Employers whose jobs are worth less than the wage do not hire; workers whose reservation wage is above it do not work (they are not unemployed: they do not want the job at that wage).

Investigation 2: The National Living Wage

What to look for: If the market wage is below £12.71 the minimum wage binds: those in work earn more, but fewer are hired and more want to work: unemployment (the gap between the curves). How big depends on the elasticity of labour demand.

Investigation 3: Derived demand: a boom

What to look for: Each worker's MRP (= extra output × price) rises, so labour demand shifts right: higher wages and more jobs. The demand for labour is derived from the demand for the product.

Investigation 4: Lorry driver shortage

What to look for: Supply shifts left; with inelastic supply (it takes months to train) wages rise sharply: HGV pay rose by about 10–20% in 2021 and firms offered bonuses.

Investigation 5: A trade union

What to look for: A union wage acts like a minimum wage: higher pay for members, fewer jobs. Demand for nurses is inelastic (|PED| ≈ 0.3), so employment falls only a little; unions are strongest where demand for labour is inelastic.

Investigation 6: Why wages differ: skills

What to look for: Some move, but training takes time and money (occupational immobility), so a gap about the size of the cost of moving remains. Higher skills mean higher MRP and scarcer supply.

Investigation 7: London and the North East

What to look for: Higher MRP (productivity) in London raises wages; housing costs and family ties make moving expensive (geographical immobility), so the gap persists at about the cost of moving.

Investigation 8: Monopsony A level

What to look for: To hire one more worker it must raise the wage for everyone, so MCL = w + L × Δw/ΔL > w. It hires where MRP = MCL: fewer workers, at a wage below their MRP (the monopsony wage gap).

Investigation 9: Minimum wage in a monopsony A level

What to look for: Employment RISES: the minimum wage removes the reason to hold wages down (MCL becomes flat at the minimum wage up to the supply curve). This is why some economists find small or no job losses from minimum wages.

Investigation 10: Elasticity of labour demand A level

What to look for: The more elastic the demand for labour (easy substitutes: machines, self-service), the bigger the fall in employment for the same rise in wage.

Batch experiment 1: Minimum wage and unemployment

Below the market wage a minimum wage does nothing. Above it, unemployment grows with the gap between labour supply and demand. Haggling adds a little frictional unemployment even with no minimum wage.
Theory (Ls − Ld): 90: 0 · 100: 0 · 105: 2 · 110: 4 · 120: 8 · 130: 12
Result from a test run: 90: 1.44 ± 0.312 · 100: 3.24 ± 0.293 · 105: 5.56 ± 0.293 · 110: 6.48 ± 0.273 · 120: 9.32 ± 0.287 · 130: 13.2 ± 0.245 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Minimum wage in a monopsony

In the monopsony, employment rises with the minimum wage up to the competitive wage (where supply meets MRP), then falls. In a competitive market (theory line) any binding minimum wage cuts jobs.

Result from a test run: 0: 14.3 ± 0 · 10: 16 ± 0 · 10.5: 17 ± 0 · 11: 18 ± 0 · 11.5: 19 ± 0 · 12: 20 ± 0 · 12.5: 19.3 ± 0 · 13: 18.7 ± 0 · 14: 17.3 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Derived demand

A higher product price raises MRP, so firms want more workers and wages rise: the demand for labour is derived from the demand for the product.
Theory (MRP = supply): 0.7: 10.2 · 0.85: 11.2 · 1: 12 · 1.15: 12.7 · 1.3: 13.2
Result from a test run: 0.7: 10.3 ± 0.0747 · 0.85: 11.3 ± 0.0557 · 1: 12 ± 0.0443 · 1.15: 12.7 ± 0.0567 · 1.3: 13.3 ± 0.0433 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Does mobility close the wage gap?

With free movement wages converge; the dearer it is to move (housing, retraining, family), the bigger the gap that survives. Mobility is a key reason wages differ.
Theory (≈ the cost of moving (cheapest movers)): 0: 0 · 1: 0.8 · 2: 1.6 · 3: 2.4 · 4: 3.2 · 6: 4.8
Result from a test run: 0: 0.373 ± 0.178 · 1: 0.242 ± 0.157 · 2: 1.19 ± 0.129 · 3: 1.26 ± 0.503 · 4: 2.64 ± 0.14 · 6: 4.49 ± 0.104 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Does haggling find the market wage?

Close to the crossing of labour demand and supply, with some spread from the luck of who meets whom: the invisible hand works for wages too.
