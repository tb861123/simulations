How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Employers. Each employer has one job. What a worker in that job is worth to the employer is the marginal revenue product: MRP = MPP × MR (here the product's price, for a firm in a competitive product market). Values are spread evenly, so the demand curve for labour is a straight line through the reference wage with the elasticity you choose. A rise in the product's price or in productivity raises every MRP by the same percentage: demand for labour is a derived demand. Each employer and worker stands for a block of real people (the job's "each dot" says how many).
  • Workers. Each has a reservation wage: the lowest wage they would accept (it reflects what else they could do: other jobs, study, caring, benefits, leisure). Reservation wages are spread evenly, so the supply curve is a straight line with the elasticity you choose. Migrants join with the same spread of reservation wages, so supply shifts right.
  • Hiring rounds are a continuous double auction (one round = a week): employers call out wage offers, workers call out the wages they want, and a job is filled when an offer meets an ask. Nobody knows the curves, but wages settle where supply meets demand, as in the Supply, Demand and Elasticity simulation. Each week starts afresh (as if everyone were on weekly contracts).
  • Minimum wage and union wage are wage floors: no job may pay less. With a floor above the market wage, more people want work than there are jobs: those willing to work at the floor but not hired are unemployed (ringed red). The theory predicts the fall in jobs from the elasticity of labour demand.
  • Monopsony (A level). One employer owns every job and must pay all its workers the same wage. Each week it tries a slightly higher or lower wage and keeps whichever made more profit (total MRP − wage bill), so it learns its way to MRP = MCL. A minimum wage or union wage between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage makes MCL flat at that wage, so hiring rises; above the competitive wage, jobs fall. A union's wage is set by its bargaining power, from the monopsony wage (no power) to the MRP of the monopsony's workforce (all the power).
  • Two markets. Each market has straight-line demand and supply; the wage in each clears its own market each week. Each week a share of workers (the mobility setting) compares the two wages and moves if the gap is bigger than the cost of moving. Only workers with the skills can work in market B. Discrimination lowers employers' valuation of group B workers by a percentage (it is not about productivity). A union wage in A causes unemployment there; unemployed A workers can look for work in B. Migrants join B.
  • Division of labour. Making a chair takes four tasks. Generalists do every task themselves, switching between them (time lost each switch) and practising each task only a quarter as often. Specialists do one task each; practice cuts the time per task (up to the learning setting). Boredom slowly slows specialists after a long run.
  • Real UK rates are the National Minimum Wage and National Living Wage with their dates (GOV.UK, Low Pay Commission). The reference wages, elasticities and numbers of workers for each job are illustrative values in the range of published estimates, not forecasts.
  • Left out: hours (everyone works the same hours), training and promotion, efficiency wages, search and matching frictions (jobs here are filled within a week), benefits and taxes, firms closing down, prices of the product changing because of the minimum wage, and non-wage benefits of a job (except the cost of moving).
