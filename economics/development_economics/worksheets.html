Development Economics · Teacher notesdevelopment-economics.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Fifty years of a copper exporter

What to look for: Copper earns most of the foreign currency. When its price falls, income and saving fall, so investment and growth fall too; if export earnings fall far enough the country cannot even afford imported machines (the foreign currency gap, shaded on the commodity chart). Dependence on one primary product makes growth volatile.

Investigation 2: Saving for growth

What to look for: More saving → more investment → more capital → more output. The Harrod–Domar model says growth ≈ s ÷ v − depreciation. But saving is hard when people are poor: a savings gap.

Investigation 3: From village to city

What to look for: While there is surplus labour on the farms, factories can hire at the same low wage, so profits (and reinvestment) are high. When the surplus runs out (the Lewis turning point) firms must pay more to attract workers: wages rise.

Investigation 4: Schools

What to look for: Education builds human capital: workers are more productive, families have fewer children (slower population growth), and the HDI rises on two counts. The payoff is slow: decades, not years.

Investigation 5: Aid that works, aid that leaks A level

What to look for: Aid fills the savings gap and the foreign currency gap, but only if it becomes investment. With poor governance (corruption, capital flight, aid spent on consumption) much of it leaks away.

Investigation 6: Diversify! A level

What to look for: More industry means less dependence on copper: smaller swings, and no Prebisch–Singer drag. Diversification takes time, because the factories start small.

Investigation 7: The resource curse avoided A level

What to look for: It saves a high share (like Botswana's revenue saving), invests in schools and keeps governance good, so commodity income becomes lasting capital and human capital rather than a boom and bust.

Investigation 8: Microfinance A level

What to look for: Small loans let poor households save and invest (raising s a little) and farmers buy better seed, raising farm productivity. A modest but real effect: microfinance is not a cure for poverty on its own.

Batch experiment 1: Harrod–Domar: saving and growth

A straight line: each extra 3% of GDP saved adds about 1% to growth. Below s = 15% the capital stock barely keeps up with depreciation: a savings gap.
Theory (s ÷ v − δ): 0.05: -3.33 · 0.1: -1.67 · 0.15: 0 · 0.2: 1.67 · 0.25: 3.33 · 0.3: 5 · 0.35: 6.67
Result from a test run: 0.05: -3.37 ± 0.0327 · 0.1: -1.67 ± 0.0135 · 0.15: 0.0668 ± 0.0435 · 0.2: 1.72 ± 0.03 · 0.25: 3.36 ± 0.0324 · 0.3: 5 ± 0.0539 · 0.35: 6.7 ± 0.0328 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Commodity dependence and volatile growth

The more a country depends on one primary product, the more its growth swings with world prices.
Result from a test run: 0.05: 1.71 ± 0.0527 · 0.15: 2.32 ± 0.102 · 0.25: 2.95 ± 0.191 · 0.35: 3.41 ± 0.216 · 0.45: 4.59 ± 0.28 · 0.55: 5.51 ± 0.322 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Aid and growth

Aid only raises growth if it becomes investment. Copper-price luck adds a lot of scatter between runs.
Theory (no price shocks): 0: 2.05 · 0.2: 2.3 · 0.4: 2.51 · 0.6: 2.7 · 0.8: 2.88 · 1: 3.05
Result from a test run: 0: 2.13 ± 0.053 · 0.2: 2.35 ± 0.0744 · 0.4: 2.6 ± 0.0461 · 0.6: 2.68 ± 0.0463 · 0.8: 2.98 ± 0.0304 · 1: 3.14 ± 0.069 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: When is the Lewis turning point?

Faster capital accumulation absorbs the surplus labour sooner. After the turning point, wages rise with productivity.
Theory (no price shocks): 0.15: 15 · 0.2: 13 · 0.25: 12 · 0.3: 11 · 0.35: 10
Result from a test run: 0.15: 15 ± 0 · 0.2: 13 ± 0 · 0.25: 12 ± 0 · 0.3: 11 ± 0 · 0.35: 10 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Copper luck: 200 exporters

Identical policies, very different outcomes: luck in commodity prices matters a great deal for primary-product exporters.
Result from a test run: mean 1620, SD 74.2, SE 5.25 from 200 runs
