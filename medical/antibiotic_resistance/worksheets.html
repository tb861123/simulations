1 · Does stopping early cause resistance?

Does stopping early cause resistance? Each dot is the share of 40 patients. Stopping after only 1–2 days lets the infection come back, but with ordinary bacteria: without the drug they out-grow the resistant ones, which pay a fitness cost. From day 3 the drug and the immune system have already cleared it, so finishing the full 7 days makes no difference here. This matches recent medical thinking: the danger is under-dosing or missing many doses, not a slightly shorter course (doctors are now testing shorter courses). Real infections vary much more than this model, which is why doctors still give a safety margin.

2 · Where is the mutant selection window?

Where is the mutant selection window? Very low doses do nothing: ordinary bacteria win because resistance has a fitness cost. The orange band is predicted from theory using only the drug-level and dose–response equations. Lower edge: the drug gives partly resistant cells enough of a growth advantage to take over during the course. Upper edge: normal bacteria now die off faster than the mutants can grow, so the infection shrinks to a size the immune system can mop up first. Inside the band, the drug clears the competition while the infection stays big enough to swamp the immune system, so resistance takes over. Above it nearly every patient is cured.

3 · Is a resistant mutant there before treatment? Test 1 − e^(−μN)

Is a resistant mutant there before treatment? Test 1 − e^(−μN) As an infection grows to N cells there are about N divisions, each with a chance μ of a resistance mutation, so the number of mutation events is a Poisson count with mean μN. The chance of at least one is 1 − e−μN (gold). With μ = 1 in 100 million, a 10⁸ infection has a 63% chance. Dots scatter around the curve by about one standard error, as chance predicts.

5 · Do missed doses matter?

Do missed doses matter? Missing the odd dose (10%) hardly matters: the next doses catch up. Missing many doses (30–40%) leaves long gaps. Some patients relapse with ordinary bacteria, and about 1 in 4 get a resistant infection, because the drug keeps knocking back the normal bacteria while the mutants recover between doses. Missing over half the doses is like hardly being treated: ordinary bacteria win again.

6 · Luria–Delbrück: how many mutants are there before treatment?

Luria–Delbrück: how many mutants are there before treatment? Most infections have no mutants or just a few, but a few have hundreds: a mutation early in the growth gives a 'jackpot' family. Here N = 10⁸ and μ = 10⁻⁸, so on average one mutation happens. Each of the log₂(N/N₀) ≈ 20 generations adds ½μN mutants on average, so the theory mean is ½μN log₂(N/N₀) ≈ 10. That mean is pulled up by very rare, very early jackpots that 1000 patients will hardly ever include, so the model's mean usually lands below the theory and the SD is huge. Luria and Delbrück used this lumpy pattern in 1943 to show mutations happen at random, before the drug arrives.

7 · How long a course does a weak immune system need?

How long a course does a weak immune system need? With a weak immune system (30%) the drug has to do almost all the work. Courses shorter than 5 days fail, and even with a full course 1 in 4 patients end up with a resistant infection, because the immune system cannot mop up the mutants the drug leaves behind. Longer courses help. Doctors give longer courses, or two drugs, to patients with weak immune systems (e.g. during chemotherapy).
