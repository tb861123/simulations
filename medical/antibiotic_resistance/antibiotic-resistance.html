Key ideas

  • MIC: the lowest drug level that stops a strain growing.
  • Therapeutic window: drug levels high enough to work but low enough to be safe.
  • Mutant selection window: levels above the MIC of normal bacteria but below the MIC of resistant ones. Here the drug kills the competition and the resistant cells take over.
  • Natural selection: the antibiotic does not cause the mutations. It removes the bacteria that are not resistant.
How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • One infection site, well mixed. All the bacteria see the same drug level and the same immune response. The dish picture shows a sample: up to 40 bacteria it shows every one; above that each bacterium drawn stands for many (the number drawn grows with the logarithm of the real count).
  • Growth: each cell divides at rate ln2 ÷ 3 h × (1 − N/K), where K = 1010 is the most the tissue can hold. Natural death rate 0.05 per hour. Bacteria in the body divide far more slowly than in a lab flask (about every 20 minutes there).
  • Random events: in each 0.1-hour step the number of divisions is a Poisson random number and the number of deaths a binomial one. With small numbers this randomness decides whether the last few cells survive.
  • Immune system: kills each bacterium at rate 0.3 × strength × 30 000 ÷ (30 000 + N) per hour. With few bacteria it wins; with millions the immune cells are swamped. A healthy patient can clear about 20 000 bacteria alone, but not a big infection. A weak one (below about 60%) can never win alone, so the drug must do all the work.
  • Drug level (one-compartment model): a tablet is absorbed from the gut at rate ka; the blood level C (mg/L) falls with the drug's half-life: C halves every t½. Peak after one dose ≈ F × dose ÷ Vd × (a factor below 1 because removal starts before absorption finishes).
  • Killing (Emax / Hill dose–response): kill rate = Emax × (C/MIC)h ÷ ((C/MIC)h + E), with E chosen so that at C = MIC killing exactly balances growth.
  • Strains: sensitive; partly resistant (MIC × 8); fully resistant (MIC × 64). A sensitive cell can mutate to partly resistant, and a partly resistant cell to fully resistant, each with chance μ per new cell. With a second drug, a separate mutation (chance μ) gives resistance to that drug (MIC × 64). Each resistance step slows growth by the fitness cost (full resistance costs 1.5 times as much).
  • Plasmids (horizontal gene transfer): when switched on, fully resistant cells pass a resistance plasmid to other cells at a rate proportional to how many carriers there are (5 per hour when the tissue is packed with carriers).
  • Before treatment the infection grows from 100 cells to the starting size, so resistant mutants may already be there (the Luria–Delbrück idea). The chance of at least one is close to 1 − e−μN.
  • Two drugs combine by Bliss independence: if drug 1 alone gives a fraction f₁ of its maximum kill and drug 2 gives f₂, together they give 1 − (1 − f₁)(1 − f₂) of the larger maximum. So a second drug mainly fills the gaps (between doses, and against cells resistant to the first drug) rather than doubling the speed. Missed doses are decided at random, independently for each dose.
  • Outcome (checked 7 days after the course should have ended): cured = no bacteria left; resistant infection = bacteria remain and more than half are resistant; otherwise still infected (the infection came back with ordinary bacteria).
  • Virus mode: antibiotics do nothing to viruses. The model follows harmless bacteria that normally live in you (109, 1% already resistant). The immune system leaves them alone, and about 100 new sensitive bacteria arrive each hour from food and the surroundings.
  • Not modelled: where the bacteria hide (abscesses, biofilms, inside cells), drug reaching tissues more slowly than blood, bacteria that survive without being resistant ("persisters"), the immune response building up over days, side-effects, and drugs that only stop growth rather than kill (here every drug kills, at different speeds). Drug values are realistic but simplified, so do not use them for real dosing.
