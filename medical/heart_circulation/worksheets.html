Heart and Circulation · Teacher notesheart-circulation.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Follow the blood

What to look for: Twice: this is a double circulation. Left side → body, where the blood gives up oxygen (red to blue) → right side → lungs, where it picks oxygen up again (blue to red) → left side. Passing through the heart a second time gives the blood a fresh push at high pressure after the lungs, which a single loop (as in a fish) cannot do.

Investigation 2: One-way doors

What to look for: Filling: the valves between atria and ventricles (tricuspid and bicuspid/mitral) are open and the two exit valves (pulmonary and aortic) are shut. Squeezing: the other way round. Each valve is pushed open or shut by the blood itself, by whichever side has the higher pressure. The 'lub' is the atrial-side valves snapping shut; the 'dub' is the exit valves shutting. A valve that does not shut lets blood slosh backwards (try 'Leaky mitral valve').

Investigation 3: Left and right

What to look for: The left ventricle reaches about 120 mmHg, the right only about 25. The left has to push blood all the way round the body through a high resistance; the right only through the lungs, which are close by and delicate (high pressure there would force fluid into the air sacs). The left wall is about three times thicker. Both pump exactly the same volume each beat: they are in series.

Investigation 4: Exercise

What to look for: Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume. In hard exercise the rate can go from 70 to about 180 and each beat pumps more, because the heart squeezes harder (adrenaline) and the working muscles squeeze blood back to it. Output rises from 5 to over 20 litres a minute. Blood pressure rises only modestly, because the small arteries in the muscles widen, lowering the resistance.

Investigation 5: A narrowed coronary artery

What to look for: At rest the narrowed artery still delivers enough. In exercise the heart muscle needs several times more oxygen, the artery cannot supply it, and the muscle weakens: the output falls short and the person feels chest pain (angina). If a clot blocks the artery completely, that part of the muscle dies: a heart attack. Risk factors include smoking, a diet high in saturated fat, obesity and high blood pressure.

Investigation 6: Blood loss

What to look for: With less blood returning, the ventricles fill less, so each beat pumps less (stretch the muscle less and it contracts less strongly: Starling's law) and pressure falls. Pressure sensors in the arteries detect this; the brain speeds up the heart and narrows the small arteries, bringing the pressure back up. A fast, weak pulse is a classic sign of serious bleeding.

Investigation 7: High blood pressure

What to look for: Narrower small arteries raise both numbers. Stiffer large arteries cannot stretch to absorb each pulse, so the top number (systolic) rises and the bottom one (diastolic) falls: a bigger pulse. The left ventricle must generate more pressure every beat, for life; its wall thickens and it may eventually fail, and the high pressure damages arteries in the brain, kidneys and heart.

Investigation 8: The cardiac cycle on a graph A level

What to look for: Ventricle rises above atrium: the mitral valve shuts (first heart sound). Ventricle rises above aorta: the aortic valve opens and blood is ejected. Ventricle falls below aorta: the aortic valve shuts (second sound; the small notch on the aortic line). Ventricle falls below atrium: the mitral valve opens and filling begins. Between the first two, and between the last two, both valves are shut: isovolumetric contraction and relaxation, the vertical sides of the pressure–volume loop.

Investigation 9: Starling's law of the heart A level

What to look for: More filling stretches the ventricle (the loop's right-hand side moves right), and it responds by ejecting more: the loop gets wider. This is the Frank–Starling mechanism. It automatically matches the two sides: if the right ventricle pumps a little more than the left, the extra blood arrives at the left, stretches it, and the left pumps more on the next beat.

Investigation 10: Faulty valves A level

What to look for: Narrowed (stenosed) aortic valve: the ventricle must generate far more pressure than reaches the aorta, so it does extra work for less output. Leaky mitral valve: part of each beat goes backwards into the atrium, so the forward output falls and pressure backs up into the lungs, causing breathlessness. In both, the pressure–volume loop changes shape in a way a cardiologist can recognise.

Investigation 11: Heart failure A level

What to look for: The weak ventricle ejects a smaller fraction of its contents (normal is 55 to 70%), so blood dams up behind it: the ventricle dilates and the pressure in the left atrium and lung veins rises. Above about 18 to 25 mmHg, fluid is forced out of the lung capillaries into the air sacs, so gas exchange suffers. With the reflexes on, the body's attempt to restore blood pressure (faster rate, tighter arteries) makes the failing heart work harder still.
