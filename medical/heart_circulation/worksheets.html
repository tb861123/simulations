Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Does a faster heart always pump more blood?

Does a faster heart always pump more blood? The naïve idea CO = HR × SV assumes each beat pumps the same. In fact the time to fill shrinks as the rate rises, so stroke volume falls. Output rises at first, peaks, then falls: very fast heart rhythms make people faint. The error bars come from random patient differences.
Highest cardiac output: 5.97 L/min at heart rate = 100.0 beats/min.

2 · Is mean pressure = cardiac output × resistance?

Is mean pressure = cardiac output × resistance? Ohm's law for blood: pressure difference = flow × resistance. The pink line assumes the heart keeps pumping the resting output. The model bends below it because a heart pumping against narrow arterioles ejects less each beat — but MAP = CO × R still holds exactly at every step (see the check).
Straight-line fit: mean arterial pressure = 53.97 × arteriole resistance + 36.65, R² = 0.9870.
Check: using the model's own cardiac output at each step, MAP − CVP = CO × R holds to within 0.2% — the "Ohm's law" of the circulation. The straight theory line misses because the heart pumps less against a higher resistance.

3 · How spread out is blood pressure in a population?

How spread out is blood pressure in a population? 500 random 'patients' aged 18–80 with normal differences in resistance, artery stiffness, heart rate, heart strength and blood volume. The pink curve is an illustrative adult distribution (mean 126, SD 17 mmHg), not taken from one dated survey: UK surveys give means of roughly 124–131 mmHg depending on year, sex and age group (e.g. Health Survey for England 2006: men 130.8, women 124.0). Older patients have stiffer arteries, which stretches the high tail. Real populations also include treatment, obesity and kidney disease, which the model leaves out.
Theory: 126.0 mmHg. Model mean differs by 2.51 (2.0%, 3.6 standard errors).
Model: 25% of patients at 140/90 or above. Real adults in England: roughly 1 in 4 to 1 in 3 have high blood pressure (about 20% would read 140 or more systolic on the reference curve).

4 · When does blood loss cause shock?

When does blood loss cause shock? Reflexes are switched on. As blood is lost, the heart fills less and output falls, but the baroreceptor reflex speeds the heart and tightens vessels, so systolic pressure falls only slowly at first. After about 30% loss it can't keep up and pressure collapses: shock. Try it again with reflexes off (untick them on the left and use Run batch).
In the model, systolic pressure falls below 90 mmHg at about 28% blood loss. Doctors' guide: pressure is usually held up until about 30% is lost (class III).

5 · Why do stiff arteries widen the pulse pressure?

Why do stiff arteries widen the pulse pressure? A stretchy aorta stores part of each beat and lets it out during diastole (the Windkessel effect). Pulse pressure ≈ stroke volume ÷ compliance, so if stiffness doubles the theory says pulse pressure doubles. The model rises a little less, because some blood runs off to the body during ejection and stroke volume falls slightly.
Straight-line fit: pulse pressure = 32.69 × artery stiffness + 12.18, R² = 0.9998.
