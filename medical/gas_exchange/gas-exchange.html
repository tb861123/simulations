What's happening

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Steady state: the model works out the levels the body settles at for the settings chosen. The animation shows that state; it does not model the minutes it takes to get there.
  • Air pressure falls with altitude (West's formula, fitted to pressures measured on high mountains): 101 kPa at sea level, 72 kPa at 3000 m, 33.7 kPa on the summit of Everest, as measured there. (The 'standard atmosphere' gives 31.4 kPa there; it underestimates pressure near the equator.) Air is always 20.9% oxygen. Inhaled air is warmed and saturated with water vapour (6.3 kPa), so inspired PO₂ = 0.209 × (Pair − 6.3).
  • Ventilation: minute ventilation = rate × tidal volume. The first 0.15 L of each breath stays in the airways (dead space), so alveolar ventilation = rate × (tidal volume − 0.15 L).
  • Alveolar gas: PACO₂ = 0.115 × CO₂ made (mL/min) ÷ alveolar ventilation (L/min). PAO₂ = inspired PO₂ − PACO₂ × (0.209 + 0.791 ÷ R), where R (0.8 at rest, up to 1.1) is CO₂ made ÷ O₂ used.
  • Automatic control: the brain sets breathing to keep CO₂ near 5.3 kPa, a little lower in very hard exercise, and breathes more when arterial PO₂ falls below about 9 kPa (stronger after acclimatisation). Breaths get deeper first, then faster. Cardiac output rises with oxygen use (5 L/min at rest, up to 22, or 35 for an athlete), and a little in anaemia.
  • Diffusion (Fick's law): the lung's diffusing capacity D = 3.0 × area (m²) ÷ thickness (µm) mL O₂ per minute per kPa, using 45% of the capillaries at rest and all of them in hard exercise. Along a capillary, oxygen moves in at a rate D × (PAO₂ − PcapillaryO₂), worked out in 80 small steps. CO₂ crosses 20 times faster.
  • Transit time = capillary blood volume (75 mL at rest, up to 150 mL; more in athletes) ÷ cardiac output: about 0.9 s at rest, 0.3–0.45 s in hard exercise.
  • Haemoglobin: each gram carries up to 1.34 mL O₂. Saturation follows the Severinghaus equation S = 1 ÷ (23 400 ÷ (P³ + 150P) + 1) (P in mmHg), half-saturated at P50 = 3.6 kPa. Plus 0.0225 mL/dL dissolved per kPa.
  • Bohr shift: each 1 kPa of extra CO₂ lowers pH by about 0.06 and P50 rises by 100.48 × ΔpH; warm, acid exercising muscle shifts it further, helping oxygen unload.
  • Carbon monoxide blocks a share of the haemoglobin and shifts the rest of the curve to the left. A pulse oximeter cannot tell carboxyhaemoglobin from oxyhaemoglobin, so it reads falsely high.
  • Mismatch: some blood passes alveoli that get little air (2% normally; more with asthma, emphysema or fibrosis) and stays venous. It mixes with the rest to give arterial blood.
  • Tissues use 250 mL O₂/min at rest, up to 3 L/min (5 L/min for an athlete) at full exercise. Blood returning to the lungs has lost that oxygen (Fick principle: uptake = cardiac output × (arterial − venous content)). If muscles cannot get enough, uptake is limited and they would switch to anaerobic respiration.
  • Lung volumes: total 6 L (athlete 7 L), residual 1.2 L. Emphysema and asthma trap air (bigger residual volume, smaller vital capacity); fibrosis makes the lungs small and stiff.
  • Smoking adds 6% COHb, 10% airway narrowing and 10% less surface area.
  • Not modelled: the time it takes to adapt, differences between the top and bottom of the lung, the reaction time of haemoglobin, kidney changes at altitude other than a smaller pH effect, carbon dioxide stored in the body, and blood flow to different organs. Real patients vary more than the batch run's random variation.
