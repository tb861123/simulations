1 · Does diffusion follow Fick's law? (membrane thickness)

Does diffusion follow Fick's law? (membrane thickness) The rate at which O₂ crosses where blood enters the capillary. Fick's law says double the thickness, half the rate (gold curve; gradient −1 on a log–log plot). The model follows it closely for thin membranes. For thick ones it sits above the curve: blood comes back from the body with less oxygen, so the difference in PO₂ is bigger, which partly makes up. Dots scatter because each person has a slightly different lung.

2 · Why does SpO₂ stay normal until the membrane is badly damaged?

Why does SpO₂ stay normal until the membrane is badly damaged? During moderate exercise (50%). SpO₂ hardly changes up to about 0.6 µm (normal is 0.5 µm), then falls steeply beyond about 1 µm. Two reasons: blood normally fills up in the first third of the capillary (a safety margin), and the top of the S-shaped dissociation curve is flat. Once the margin is used up, saturation drops fast. This is why fibrosis patients first notice breathlessness on exercise.

3 · Does O₂ uptake follow Fick's law? (surface area, emphysema)

Does O₂ uptake follow Fick's law? (surface area, emphysema) During moderate exercise. If uptake were set by diffusion alone it would rise in proportion to area (gold line). It doesn't: above about 40 m² uptake equals what the muscles need, because the blood is already full (perfusion-limited). Only when emphysema removes most of the surface does diffusion become the limit and uptake falls.

4 · SpO₂ at altitude: does the model match real measurements?

SpO₂ at altitude: does the model match real measurements? Air pressure falls with height, so less O₂ is pushed into the alveoli. Saturation holds up at first (flat top of the curve), then falls faster above about 3000 m. Pink diamonds: typical values measured in healthy visitors at rest. The model is within a few % of them; real people vary more, partly because some breathe much harder than others at altitude.

5 · Everest check: do we match blood taken from climbers at 8400 m?

Everest check: do we match blood taken from climbers at 8400 m? In 2009 Grocott and colleagues took arterial blood from four acclimatised climbers at 8400 m on Everest (air pressure 36.3 kPa), on their way down from the summit, breathing air. Mean PaO₂ was 3.28 kPa (range 2.55–3.93), PaCO₂ 1.77 kPa and SaO₂ about 54% (34–70%). The model's climbers breathe so hard that CO₂ falls to about 1.7 kPa; by the alveolar gas equation that raises the alveolar PO₂ to about 4.2 kPa, and the arterial PO₂ comes out about 3.2 kPa (switch Record to SpO₂ or PaCO₂ to compare those too). On the summit itself (33.7 kPa) the model predicts about 45%: no blood has ever been taken there.

6 · How much do people vary? SpO₂ at rest

How much do people vary? SpO₂ at rest 500 healthy people at rest at sea level, each slightly different. Nearly all read 96–98%, matching the normal range doctors use (95–99%). The gold line is the average person with no variation. The crowd's mean sits a little below it, by many standard errors: that is real, not chance. Saturation can rise only a little (the curve is flat near 100%) but can fall more, so differences between people pull the average down.

7 · …and during hard exercise

…and during hard exercise The same people running at 80% of their maximum. Most stay above 95%: the safety margin copes with the faster blood flow. People whose lungs are small for their fitness drop lower (some athletes do this in real life: exercise-induced hypoxaemia). The spread is wider than at rest because blood has less spare time in the capillary.

8 · How much air do you need for exercise?

How much air do you need for exercise? Gold: the alveolar ventilation equation, breathing just enough to keep CO₂ at 5.3 kPa. Up to about 60% of maximum the model follows it: ventilation rises in step with the CO₂ made. Above that, lactic acid builds up and drives extra breathing, so the model curves above the line (the 'ventilatory threshold' seen in real exercise tests).
