Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Breathing in and out

What to look for: Breathing in: the diaphragm contracts and flattens, the external intercostal muscles pull the ribs up and out. The chest volume increases, so the pressure inside falls below the air outside and air flows in. Breathing out at rest is mostly passive: the muscles relax and the lungs spring back.

Investigation 2: Inside an alveolus

What to look for: At rest the blood is full within about the first third of the capillary. Diffusion is fast because the wall is only about 0.5 µm thick (two flattened cells), the total area is about 70 m² (a tennis court), breathing keeps the alveolar oxygen high and blood flow carries oxygen away, so the concentration gradient stays steep.

Investigation 3: Exercise

What to look for: Breathing gets faster and deeper and the heart pumps much more blood, so each red cell spends only about a third of a second in the capillary. In a healthy lung that is still just enough: there is a big safety margin. More capillaries open, increasing the surface area.

Investigation 4: Fibrosis: a thicker wall

What to look for: Diffusion is inversely proportional to thickness, so it is four times slower. At rest the blood still just fills before the end of the capillary; in exercise the faster blood leaves before it is full, and the oxygen saturation falls. People with fibrosis become breathless on exertion first.

Investigation 5: Emphysema

What to look for: Fewer, larger air sacs have far less surface area, so less oxygen diffuses per second. The blood may not fill, especially in exercise. Damaged airways also make breathing out hard. Smoking also adds carbon monoxide and paralyses the cilia.

Investigation 6: Altitude

What to look for: The air pressure is about half, so each breath holds about half as much oxygen and the partial pressure of oxygen in the alveoli falls. Haemoglobin is less saturated. After acclimatising, breathing is much deeper (driving the CO₂ down, which lets the alveolar oxygen rise) and the body makes more red blood cells over weeks.

Investigation 7: Carbon monoxide

What to look for: CO binds to haemoglobin about 250 times more strongly than oxygen, so 30% of the haemoglobin carries nothing, and the rest holds on to its oxygen more tightly. Oxygen content falls by a third or more. A pulse oximeter cannot tell carboxyhaemoglobin from oxyhaemoglobin, so it reads nearly 100%: dangerously reassuring.

Investigation 8: Shallow breathing

What to look for: The first 0.15 L of every breath only fills the airways (dead space), where no gas exchange happens. Shallow breaths waste a larger share: 40 × (0.3 − 0.15) = 6 L/min reaches the alveoli against 12 × 0.85 = 10.2 L/min. CO₂ builds up and oxygen falls.

Investigation 9: The dissociation curve A level

What to look for: At rest the venous blood is still about 75% saturated: only a quarter is unloaded. In exercise the muscles' PO₂ is lower and the Bohr shift (more CO₂, acid and heat) moves the curve to the right, so much more is unloaded. The steep part of the curve is at the PO₂ of working tissues.

Investigation 10: No Bohr shift A level

What to look for: Without the right shift in working muscle, haemoglobin holds on to more of its oxygen at the same PO₂, so less is unloaded and the venous blood is more saturated (or the muscles must work at a lower PO₂). The Bohr effect helps deliver oxygen exactly where CO₂ is being made.

Batch experiment 1: Altitude and oxygen saturation

Almost no change up to about 2000 m, because the top of the dissociation curve is flat. Above that, saturation falls steeply. People differ more and more as they climb.
Theory (average person): 0: 97.1 · 1000: 95.6 · 2000: 92.8 · 3000: 90.2 · 4000: 86.9 · 5000: 80.7 · 6000: 70.2 · 7000: 54.7
Result from a test run: 0: 97.1 ± 0.00236 · 1000: 95.6 ± 0.00258 · 2000: 92.7 ± 0.0265 · 3000: 90.1 ± 0.139 · 4000: 86.6 ± 0.222 · 5000: 80.5 ± 0.43 · 6000: 69 ± 0.883 · 7000: 52.8 ± 2.62 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Acclimatisation

Breathing much harder blows off CO₂, which raises the alveolar oxygen (the alveolar gas equation). Acclimatised climbers have reached the summit of Everest without bottled oxygen, with an arterial saturation of only about 50–60%.
Theory (not acclimatised): 0: 97.1 · 2000: 92.8 · 4000: 86.9 · 5000: 80.7 · 6000: 70.2 · 7000: 54.7 · 8000: 38.2
Result from a test run: 0: 97.1 ± 0.0053 · 2000: 92.8 ± 0.00433 · 4000: 89.1 ± 0.0428 · 5000: 84.7 ± 0.907 · 6000: 79.1 ± 0.463 · 7000: 70.7 ± 0.853 · 8000: 54.2 ± 2.31 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: A thicker wall

In exercise the blood moves through too fast for a thick wall: saturation falls well before it does at rest. Diffusion rate ∝ 1 ÷ thickness (Fick's law).

Batch experiment 6: How much do people differ at 4000 m?

On the steep part of the dissociation curve, small differences between people give big differences in saturation: some cope well, some do not. That is one reason altitude sickness is hard to predict.
Theory (average person): 86.9
Result from a test run: mean 86.7, SD 0.63, SE 0.0364 from 300 runs
