Nerve Impulse · Teacher notesnerve-impulse.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: All or nothing

What to look for: Below threshold nothing travels: the little bump dies away. Above it, the spike is the same height (about +40 mV) however hard you push. Strength of a sensation is coded by how often a nerve fires, never by spike size.

Investigation 2: What makes the spike?

What to look for: Na⁺ channels (m gate) snap open first: Na⁺ floods in and the inside goes positive. A fraction of a millisecond later they inactivate (h falls) and the slower K⁺ channels (n) open: K⁺ leaves and the voltage falls back, overshooting below rest.

Investigation 3: Refractory period

What to look for: Just after a spike the Na⁺ channels are still inactivated (h is low) and K⁺ channels are still open. Until they recover, a second impulse is impossible (absolute) or weaker (relative). This caps the firing rate and makes impulses one-way.

Investigation 4: Head-on collision

What to look for: They annihilate. Each impulse leaves refractory membrane behind it, so when they meet there is no excitable membrane ahead of either. A nerve impulse is not an ordinary wave: it does not obey superposition.

Investigation 5: Why is the squid's axon giant?

What to look for: Speed ∝ √diameter: to double the speed you need four times the diameter. Squid use this for their escape reflex. Warmth speeds the channels (part of why 'cold-blooded' reflexes are slow in the cold), but above about 30 °C this axon fails altogether.

Investigation 6: Myelin

What to look for: The impulse jumps from node to node (saltatory conduction). Myelin insulates, so current flows quickly along the inside to the next node, and only the nodes have to do the slow business of opening channels. Vertebrates get speed without giant axons.

Investigation 7: Multiple sclerosis

What to look for: The impulse dies in the lesion. The membrane under myelin has almost no Na⁺ channels, and without insulation the current leaks away before it can bring the next node to threshold. Signals fail: numbness, weakness, loss of vision.

Investigation 8: Pufferfish poison

What to look for: With fewer working Na⁺ channels the spike rises more slowly, so the impulse travels more slowly and is smaller. Beyond roughly 80% block it cannot regenerate at all. No pain signals at the dentist; no breathing after badly prepared fugu.

Batch experiment 1: Speed and diameter

A little random current is injected everywhere to imitate the noisy opening of individual channels. It shifts the moment each patch reaches threshold very slightly.
Theory (speed ∝ √diameter): 100: 5.61 · 200: 7.93 · 400: 11.2 · 600: 13.7 · 800: 15.9 · 1000: 17.7
Result from a test run: 100: 5.54 ± 0.00192 · 200: 7.89 ± 0.00373 · 400: 11.2 ± 0.00751 · 600: 13.7 ± 0.00681 · 800: 15.9 ± 0.00911 · 1000: 17.7 ± 0.00985 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: All or nothing

Close to threshold, random channel noise decides whether the axon fires, so the same stimulus can give either result. That really happens in neurons.
Result from a test run: 10: -64.4 ± 0.02 · 25: -64.4 ± 0.0322 · 40: -64.4 ± 0.0478 · 46: -23.6 ± 25 · 52: 37.9 ± 0.0388 · 60: 37.9 ± 0.0377 · 80: 37.8 ± 0.07 · 120: 37.8 ± 0.0406 · 160: 37.8 ± 0.0188 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Speed and temperature

Speed rises with temperature because the Na⁺ gates open sooner. But the spike also gets shorter and smaller, and above about 30 °C this model axon stops conducting (speed 0): 'heat block'.
Result from a test run: 0: 9.45 ± 0.00455 · 5: 11.6 ± 0.00487 · 10: 14 ± 0.0123 · 15: 16.6 ± 0.00863 · 20: 19.2 ± 0.0115 · 25: 21.4 ± 0 · 30: 22 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Refractory period

Around the end of the absolute refractory period, noise decides whether the second spike starts at all.
