Stripes of a fixed fineness are sampled at different pixel spacings. What frequency appears in the image?
A stripe pattern of 0.4 cycles per original pixel, point-sampled every s pixels with a random phase and a little noise; the strongest frequency in the samples is found (a Fourier transform). Theory: the true frequency, folded back below the Nyquist limit 0.5 ÷ s.

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: How many pixels for a print?

What to look for: Print width (inches) = pixels across ÷ dpi. 6 megapixels (3000 × 2000) gives a 10 × 6.7 inch print at 300 dpi; 24 megapixels about 20 × 13 inches. On a screen you only need as many pixels as the screen has: beyond that, extra megapixels matter for big prints and cropping.

Investigation 2: Moiré on the fence

What to look for: The fence posts repeat every 5 original pixels. If the camera samples less than twice per repeat (the Nyquist limit), each sample lands on a different part of the pattern and the result is a false, coarse pattern: aliasing, or moiré. Averaging over each pixel's area (the anti-aliasing filter) blurs the detail it cannot record, so it turns grey instead of making false stripes.

Investigation 3: Banding in the sky

What to look for: Smooth gradients, like the evening sky, show steps (banding or posterisation) first, because neighbouring pixels differ by less than one level. With b bits there are 2ᵇ levels: 256 at 8 bits, 8 at 3 bits. Dither adds a little noise before rounding, which hides the bands at the cost of grain; raw files use 12–14 bits so heavy editing does not cause banding.

Investigation 4: How JPEG saves space

What to look for: JPEG keeps the coarse patterns in each 8 × 8 block and rounds off the fine ones. Smooth areas compress well; fine detail (the fence, the stripes, the text) costs most. At low quality you see the blocks, blotchy colour and ringing round sharp edges. The difference image shows exactly what was thrown away.

Investigation 5: Rule of thirds

What to look for: Placing the subject off-centre, on a thirds point, leaves space for the eye to move and usually feels more dynamic; a centred square crop feels calm and formal. The 'rules' are starting points: breaking them on purpose is also a compositional choice. The readout shows how close the tree is to a strong point.

Investigation 6: Re-saving JPEGs A level

What to look for: Each save rounds the DCT numbers again. If nothing changes between saves the loss soon stops, but a small edit (here a slight brightening each time) means the blocks are quantised afresh each time, so errors build up (generation loss). Professionals edit raw or lossless (TIFF, PNG) files and save a JPEG only at the end.

Batch experiment 1: JPEG quality and file size

Typical photos compress 10 to 30 times at quality 75 with no visible loss. Patches with lots of fine detail compress far less than smooth sky, which is why the points scatter: file size depends on the picture as much as the setting.
Result from a test run: 10: 28.1 ± 0.757 · 25: 21.6 ± 1.28 · 50: 19.9 ± 1.05 · 75: 14.6 ± 1.29 · 90: 9.94 ± 1.09 · 95: 10.7 ± 1.66 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: JPEG quality and image quality

PSNR rises steadily with quality. High settings (90+) give bigger files for little visible gain; very low settings save little more space but look much worse. Detailed patches lose more than smooth ones.
Result from a test run: 10: 31.4 ± 0.975 · 25: 32.7 ± 1.46 · 50: 37.4 ± 1.32 · 75: 38.9 ± 1.94 · 90: 40.7 ± 1.11 · 95: 48.1 ± 1.94 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Bit depth and rounding error

Each extra bit halves the rounding error. At 8 bits it is about 0.1% of full scale, too small to see in a single pixel, but a smooth gradient stretched by editing can still show bands.

Batch experiment 4: Aliasing: what pattern do you see?

With spacing 1 (Nyquist limit 0.5) the true 0.4 is recorded. With coarser sampling the stripes reappear at a false, lower frequency: that is moiré. Exactly at the right spacings (2.5 here) the pattern vanishes into a flat tone.
Theory (folded (alias) frequency): 1: 0.4 · 1.5: 0.267 · 2: 0.1 · 2.5: 0 · 3: 0.0667 · 4: 0.1 · 5: 0 · 6: 0.0667
Result from a test run: 1: 0.396 ± 0 · 1.5: 0.264 ± 0 · 2: 0.099 ± 0 · 2.5: 0.099 ± 0.0283 · 3: 0.066 ± 0 · 4: 0.099 ± 0 · 5: 0.0385 ± 0.0195 · 6: 0.066 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Saving again and again

The first save loses the most; each later save, after an edit, loses a little more (generation loss). Keep the original raw or lossless file and export a JPEG once, at the end.
Result from a test run: 1: 40 ± 1.98 · 2: 37.9 ± 3.8 · 4: 38.9 ± 1.43 · 8: 31.9 ± 1.67 · 12: 28.7 ± 0.471 · 20: 26.9 ± 1.37 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)
