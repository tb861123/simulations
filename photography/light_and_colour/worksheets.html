Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Tungsten light, daylight setting

What to look for: Tungsten light has much less blue than daylight, so with the daylight setting everything looks orange. Matching the white balance to the light divides out its colour and the grey card becomes grey again. Photographers often keep a little warmth on purpose, for mood.

Investigation 2: Cold shade

What to look for: Shade lit only by blue sky is bluer than direct sun, so skin looks cold and grey. Setting the white balance to 'shade' (about 7500–8000 K) warms it up. Mixed light (sun and shade, or a window and a bulb) cannot be corrected everywhere at once.

Investigation 3: Fooling auto white balance

What to look for: Auto white balance assumes the whole scene averages to grey ('grey world'). A frame full of green leaves is not grey, so the camera thinks the light is green and adds magenta: the grey card turns pinkish. A grey card in the shot (or a custom white balance) avoids this.

Investigation 4: Adding light

What to look for: Red + green = yellow, green + blue = cyan, blue + red = magenta; all three = white. This is additive mixing: every screen and camera sensor builds colours from red, green and blue. Dimming blue turns the white yellowish: exactly a warm colour cast.

Investigation 5: Subtracting light

What to look for: Cyan absorbs red, magenta absorbs green, yellow absorbs blue. Cyan + magenta lets only blue through; all three absorb everything: black. Printers use cyan, magenta, yellow and black (CMYK) inks for this reason.

Investigation 6: Inverse square law

What to look for: Twice the distance spreads the same light over four times the area, so E = I ÷ d² falls to a quarter: 2 stops. That is why a small move of a close light makes a big difference, and why a light far away gives an even light across a group.

Investigation 7: Lighting ratio

What to look for: The lit side gets key + fill, the shadow side only fill. A 4:1 ratio needs the fill to give a third of the key's light. With equal lamps that is √3 ≈ 1.7 times as far away as the key. Low ratios (2:1) look bright and open ('high key'); high ratios (8:1 or more) look dramatic.

Investigation 8: Hard and soft light

What to look for: The edge of a shadow is blurred where part of the light source is hidden (the penumbra); width ≈ source size × (distance from object to wall) ÷ (distance from light to object). A big source close to the subject gives soft, flattering light; the same softbox far away looks smaller, so the light gets harder.

Batch experiment 1: The inverse square law

Close to the lamp, a 2 cm error in position makes a big difference, so the scatter is largest there. A graph of E against 1/d² is a straight line through the origin with gradient I.
Theory (E = I ÷ d²): 0.5: 3200 · 0.75: 1420 · 1: 800 · 1.5: 356 · 2: 200 · 3: 88.9 · 4: 50
Result from a test run: 0.5: 3200 ± 165 · 0.75: 1480 ± 29.2 · 1: 797 ± 15.4 · 1.5: 350 ± 4.84 · 2: 197 ± 2.85 · 3: 87.9 ± 0.876 · 4: 49.7 ± 0.63 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Fill distance and lighting ratio

With the fill as close as the key, the ratio is 2:1 (1 stop). Moving the fill back makes the ratio rise with the square of its distance: 3 m gives 5:1.
Theory (1 + (d_fill ÷ d_key)²): 1.5: 2 · 2: 2.78 · 2.5: 3.78 · 3: 5 · 4: 8.11 · 5: 12.1
Result from a test run: 1.5: 2.02 ± 0.023 · 2: 2.82 ± 0.0368 · 2.5: 3.81 ± 0.0489 · 3: 4.9 ± 0.0464 · 4: 8.29 ± 0.197 · 5: 12.5 ± 0.291 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Soft and hard shadows

A straight line through the origin: the penumbra is proportional to the size of the light as seen from the subject. A bare bulb gives a sharp edge; a metre-wide softbox, a very gradual one.
Theory (similar triangles): 2: 2 · 10: 10 · 20: 20 · 40: 40 · 60: 60 · 100: 100
Result from a test run: 2: 2.21 ± 0.184 · 10: 10.1 ± 0.17 · 20: 20.1 ± 0.148 · 40: 39.8 ± 0.14 · 60: 60.1 ± 0.263 · 100: 100 ± 0.249 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Auto white balance and a green scene

With a varied scene the cast is small, though it wanders with the scene's colours. As green leaves take over, the camera decides the light is green and removes green from everything: the grey card turns magenta, and the cast grows to tens of percent. A grey card in the shot, or a custom white balance, fixes it.
Result from a test run: 0: 16.2 ± 1.34 · 10: 13.7 ± 1.12 · 20: 20.9 ± 0.997 · 30: 20 ± 1.98 · 40: 23.4 ± 1.55 · 60: 34.1 ± 1.14 · 80: 46.6 ± 0.445 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Colour temperature and the blue–red balance

Hotter sources give out relatively more blue light (Wien's law: the peak wavelength ∝ 1/T), so the ratio climbs steeply from candlelight to blue sky. The steps that look equal to the eye are roughly equal steps in mireds, not kelvin.
Theory (black body): 2000: 0.00225 · 2700: 0.128 · 3200: 0.273 · 4000: 0.527 · 5500: 1 · 6500: 1.3 · 8000: 1.85 · 10000: 2.27
Result from a test run: 2000: 0.00226 ± 1.09e-5 · 2700: 0.128 ± 4.95e-4 · 3200: 0.276 ± 0.00238 · 4000: 0.536 ± 0.00424 · 5500: 0.994 ± 0.0118 · 6500: 1.29 ± 0.0116 · 8000: 1.86 ± 0.0126 · 10000: 2.25 ± 0.0286 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)
