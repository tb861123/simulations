Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Sunny 16

What to look for: f/16 at 1/ISO seconds in bright sun: the Sunny 16 rule. Halving the time loses one stop of light, so open up one stop (f/16 → f/11). Each full stop of aperture multiplies N by √2 (1.4): light ∝ 1/N².

Investigation 2: Same exposure, different picture

What to look for: Each stop narrower doubles the shutter time, so the brightness stays the same (reciprocity). But the background and the flowers get sharper (more depth of field), and the cyclist gets more blurred (longer time). There is no free lunch: every setting has a side effect.

Investigation 3: A blurred background

What to look for: Widest aperture (f/1.4): the zone of sharpness is only a few centimetres deep at 3 m, so everything else melts away. Portrait photographers use this to separate the subject from a busy background (bokeh).

Investigation 4: Freeze or blur the cyclist

What to look for: The blur length on the image = speed × time × magnification. At 1/1000 s the cyclist moves 6 mm: frozen. At 1/30 s, 20 cm: a streak. Freezing needs short times (and so wide apertures or high ISO); slow shutter speeds can express movement creatively.

Investigation 5: Handheld at dusk

What to look for: The camera chooses a long shutter time, and hand shake blurs everything (rule of thumb: at least 1/focal length seconds, here 1/85 s). A tripod fixes shake but not a moving subject. Raising the ISO allows a short time, but fewer photons means more noise: the image looks grainy, especially in the shadows.

Investigation 6: Where does noise come from?

What to look for: Light arrives as random photons. With n photons the random variation is about √n, so the signal-to-noise ratio is √n. The short exposure collects 64 times fewer photons, so it is 8 times noisier; ISO only turns up the volume, noise and all.

Investigation 7: Read the histogram

What to look for: A histogram shows how many pixels have each brightness. A spike at the right means highlights are clipped: no detail can be recovered. 'Expose to the right' (as bright as possible without clipping) gives the least noise in a digital image.

Investigation 8: Developing film A level

What to look for: Longer development (or a warmer developer) builds more density in the exposed parts than in the shadows, so the slope γ (contrast) rises, approaching a limit. Pushing (underexposing, then developing longer) recovers mid-tones and highlights but not shadow detail, and raises contrast and grain.

Batch experiment 1: Reciprocity: equal exposures

Each stop closed (N × √2) halves the light, and each doubled time doubles it back, so the level stays at 18%. This is reciprocity. (Film fails it at very long times; sensors do not.)
Theory (mid-grey (18%)): 2: 18 · 2.8: 18 · 4: 18 · 5.6: 18 · 8: 18 · 11: 18 · 16: 18 · 22: 18
Result from a test run: 2: 18.1 ± 0.0134 · 2.8: 18.1 ± 0.0242 · 4: 18 ± 0.0112 · 5.6: 18 ± 0.0209 · 8: 18.1 ± 0.0254 · 11: 18.1 ± 0.0261 · 16: 18.1 ± 0.0216 · 22: 18.1 ± 0.0196 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Noise and ISO

Doubling the ISO halves the light collected, so the SNR falls by √2 each time: from about 85 at ISO 100 to about 5 at ISO 25600. At very high ISO the read noise adds to the shot noise.

Batch experiment 3: Depth of field and autofocus misses

At f/1.4 the depth of field at 3 m is only about ±5 cm, so a small focusing error loses the eyes. Stopping down to f/4 makes almost every shot sharp. (The tiny difference from theory: the zone of sharpness is a little deeper behind the subject than in front.)

Batch experiment 4: Shutter speed and motion blur

Blur grows in proportion to the time: a straight line through the origin, gradient = mean speed. Halving the time halves the blur, at the cost of one stop of light.

Batch experiment 5: Handheld: the 1/focal length rule

At 1/85 s (12 ms, the 'one over focal length' rule) about two thirds of shots are sharp; at 1/250 s almost all are. Image stabilisation reduces the shake, which shifts this curve to the right.

Batch experiment 6: Film: development time and contrast

Contrast rises quickly at first, then levels off: the developer runs out of silver halide to reduce in the most exposed areas. A one-degree error changes the rate by about 10%, which is why darkrooms control temperature so carefully.
