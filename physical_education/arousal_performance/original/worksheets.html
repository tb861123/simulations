The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Is there an inverted-U?

What this shows. Ten athletes take 50 free throws at each level of arousal. The success rate rises to a peak at moderate arousal (about 40% for this free-throw shooter) and then falls: the inverted-U. Each dot is one athlete's 50 attempts, so even at the same arousal the rates scatter by about ±7 percentage points (the binomial spread, √(p(1 − p) ÷ 50)): one session is not enough to find an athlete's optimum. Real research finds the shape but the curve differs from person to person (see the ZOF).

2 · Does the best arousal fall for complex skills?

What this shows. For each skill, an athlete makes 30 attempts at each of 21 arousal levels; a curve through the results finds where success peaks. The more complex the task, the lower the best arousal: a complex skill needs more attention, and high arousal narrows attention and adds tension. Try sweeping 'Gross or fine' instead: the optimum rises for gross skills. The estimates scatter by several points because 30 attempts per level still leave a lot of luck, and the top of the curve is flat.

3 · Catastrophe: is the way down the same as the way up?

What this shows. With high cognitive anxiety, arousal is first pushed to 100% (past the catastrophe), then lowered to each setting. The dots (falling arousal) stay on the low branch until arousal drops below about 22%; the dashed line shows the same athlete with arousal RISING, who performs well right up to about 54%. Between them, the same arousal gives completely different performance depending on the path: hysteresis, the key prediction of catastrophe theory. The spread of the dots is the luck of 50 attempts.

4 · An audience: help or hindrance?

What this shows. A beginner (skill 15%) performs in front of more and more spectators. The audience raises arousal (Zajonc), and for a beginner whose skill is not yet grooved a big crowd makes performance much worse (social inhibition); a small crowd hardly matters. The dashed line is an expert doing the same task, who improves with a moderate crowd (social facilitation) before also being over-aroused by a huge one. The effect grows with the logarithm of the crowd size, so the first few hundred spectators matter as much as the next 50 000. Each dot is one session of 50 attempts.

5 · Ringelmann: does effort per person fall?

What this shows. Teams of 1 to 8 pull a rope five times and the total is compared with the sum of their solo pulls. Effort per person falls by about 7% for each extra person, matching Ringelmann's 1913 rope-pulling data (dashed) closely, because the model's motivation and coordination losses were set from it. Each team is a new set of people and each pull varies by 4%, so the dots scatter by a couple of per cent. Tick 'Identifiable' or raise cohesion and practice: the losses shrink (Steiner's model). Tick 'Pseudo-group' to see the motivation loss alone.

6 · How much does one session vary?

What this shows. 500 identical athletes each take 50 free throws at 75% arousal (over-aroused for this task). The true chance is the same for all of them, yet their scores spread out in a bell shape with an SD of about 6 percentage points: pure luck, the binomial spread √(p(1 − p) ÷ 50). So a coach should not judge an athlete's arousal from one session: differences of 10 points between sessions can happen by chance.
