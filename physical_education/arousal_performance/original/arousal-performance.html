How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Arousal runs from 0% (asleep) to 100% (panic); heart rate is shown as 60 + 1.2 × arousal bpm (illustrative). Each attempt succeeds with a chance set by the theory chosen, so the same arousal does not always give the same result.
  • Inverted-U: success = floor + (peak − floor) × e^(−½((A − A*) ÷ w)²). The optimum A* = 30 + 30 × gross − 25 × complexity + 15 × skill + 8 × personality (−1 introvert, +1 extrovert); width w = 18 + 10 × skill (experts cope with a wider range); peak = 35 + 55 × skill %, floor = 15% of the peak. These numbers are illustrative: real optimums are found by testing individual athletes.
  • Drive theory: performance = habit × drive, a straight line through the middle of the range, rising for skilled performers (the dominant response is right) and falling for beginners (the dominant response is wrong).
  • Catastrophe theory: with cognitive anxiety C ≥ 30%, performance rises (up to 8% higher than the inverted-U peak) until arousal passes A* + w (1.6 − 1.2 C/100), then drops to near the floor; it only recovers when arousal falls 40 × C/100 points lower (hysteresis). With little worry it is a gentle inverted-U.
  • Zone of optimal functioning: success is at the peak inside the athlete's zone (width 12–20 points), falling off in a straight line outside it.
  • Competition: somatic arousal and cognitive anxiety move towards targets (time constants 8 s and 15 s) set by trait anxiety, the importance of the event, the crowd, pressure building towards the end, and recent misses (each miss adds worry, fading over 30–45 s). Overall arousal = somatic + 0.3 × (cognitive − 30). Techniques take a few seconds to do (progressive muscle relaxation 30 s) and then lower (or psyching up raises) arousal or worry for a while; sizes are illustrative.
  • Audience: extra arousal = k × 10 × log₁₀(1 + n ÷ 5) for n people (Zajonc's drive from the mere presence of others), with k = 0.6 for co-actors, 1 for passive spectators, 1.4 for an evaluating audience (Cottrell's evaluation apprehension), 0.9 for a home crowd and 1.25 for an away crowd; home and away crowds also lower or raise worry.
  • Groups: force = Σ (solo force × motivation × coordination). Motivation and coordination each fall by 4.3% per extra person, which matches Ringelmann's data (1913, as reported by Kravitz and Martin, 1986). Being identifiable cuts the motivation loss by 80%, task cohesion by up to 50%, practice cuts the coordination loss by up to 70%; leadership that suits the situation helps both. Solo forces vary between people (600 ± 80 N) and each pull varies by 4%.
  • Self-efficacy (A level): changes after each match by ±7 points × a weight for the attribution (internal and stable 1, internal and unstable 0.55, external 0.2), drifting back towards 50, plus 1.5 for verbal persuasion, 1.2 for vicarious experience and 0.6 for controlling emotional arousal per match. Confidence adds to performance; cognitive anxiety (worry) falls as confidence rises.
  • Left out: individual differences in how arousal is interpreted (facilitative or debilitative), aggression, motivation in detail, the many other personality theories, team tactics, and fatigue.
