Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The inverted-U

What to look for: Success is highest at a moderate arousal (the optimum). Too low: the player is under-aroused, lacks focus and effort. Too high: muscles tense, attention narrows too much, and they make mistakes. The dots on the graph trace out an upside-down U.

Investigation 2: Fine or gross skill?

What to look for: A putt is a fine, complex skill needing precise control and calm: its optimum is low. A tackle is a gross, simple skill using big muscle groups and aggression: its optimum is high. Experts also cope with higher arousal than beginners.

Investigation 3: Beginners and experts

What to look for: Drive theory: performance = habit × drive. Arousal brings out the dominant (well-learned) response. For an expert that response is correct, so performance rises; for a beginner it is often wrong, so performance gets worse. This is why beginners should learn in calm conditions.

Investigation 4: Catastrophe!

What to look for: With high worry, going past the optimum causes a sudden, dramatic drop: a catastrophe (a choke). Performance does not recover just by calming down a little: arousal has to fall well below where it collapsed (hysteresis). Low cognitive anxiety gives a gentle inverted-U instead.

Investigation 5: Mental preparation

What to look for: Techniques that lower arousal and worry (imagery and mental rehearsal, deep breathing, positive self-talk) bring the player back into their zone. Selective attention cuts out the crowd. Psyching up only helps if arousal is too low, as in a dull friendly. The batch run compares them over 100 competitions.

Investigation 6: The audience

What to look for: An audience raises arousal (Zajonc). For experts doing well-learned skills this helps (social facilitation); for beginners it hurts (social inhibition). An evaluating audience (scouts, coaches) raises arousal most: evaluation apprehension.

Investigation 7: Pulling together?

What to look for: No: in Ringelmann's 1913 rope-pulling study, eight people pulled at only about half their individual effort each. Part is coordination loss (not pulling at the same moment), part is motivation loss: social loafing. Making each person's effort identifiable reduces loafing.

Investigation 8: Zone of optimal functioning A level

What to look for: The inverted-U says the optimum is a single point that depends on the task. ZOF says each performer has their own band, which may be low, middle or high, and within it performance is at its best (a plateau, not a peak). Coaches help each athlete learn where their zone is.

Investigation 9: Steiner's model A level

What to look for: Steiner: actual productivity = potential productivity − losses due to faulty processes (coordination losses + motivation losses). Practice cuts coordination losses; identifiability and cohesion cut motivation losses (social loafing).
