Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Faster impulses, more force

What to look for: At 2 Hz each twitch relaxes before the next. At 10 Hz they start to add up (wave summation). At 50 Hz they fuse into one smooth, strong contraction: tetanus. More force without extra motor units.

Investigation 2: Slow and fast fibres

What to look for: Type IIx twitches are about three times quicker, so they relax between impulses at frequencies where type I twitches already fuse. Type I units are weaker but use oxygen and hardly tire.

Investigation 3: Recruitment by size

What to look for: The size principle: small type I units first (fine control at low force), then IIa, and the big IIx units only near maximal effort. Light jogging uses mostly type I fibres; a sprint start recruits everything.

Investigation 4: Sprinter or marathon runner?

What to look for: The fast-fibre muscle is stronger but its IIx fibres tire within seconds. The slow-fibre muscle cannot produce as much force but sustains moderate forces for a long time. Fibre type suits the event.

Investigation 5: PNF stretching A level

What to look for: During the 6–10 s isometric contraction the Golgi tendon organs sense high tension and inhibit the muscle (autogenic inhibition), overriding the muscle spindles' stretch reflex. The relaxed muscle can then be stretched further.

Investigation 6: The stretch reflex A level

What to look for: Rapid stretch fires the spindles, which trigger the stretch reflex: the muscle contracts against the stretch. Bouncing works against it and can cause strains; static and PNF stretches avoid triggering the reflex strongly.

Batch experiment 1: Force against impulse frequency

An S-shaped curve: little gain until twitches overlap, a steep rise, then a plateau at tetanus.
Theory (summed twitches): 1: 24.5 · 5: 26.8 · 10: 37.6 · 20: 60.9 · 30: 76.9 · 50: 93.7 · 80: 98.5
Result from a test run: 1: 24.3 ± 0.379 · 5: 26.4 ± 0.326 · 10: 37.7 ± 0.5 · 20: 61.7 ± 0.673 · 30: 78.5 ± 0.899 · 50: 95 ± 1.1 · 80: 99.2 ± 1.25 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Recruitment and force

Early on, each extra unit adds little force (small units), so many units are needed for low forces; near maximum, the last few big units add a lot. That is the size principle giving fine control at low force.
Result from a test run: 10: 46 ± 0.365 · 20: 68 ± 0 · 40: 89 ± 0 · 60: 103 ± 0.167 · 80: 112 ± 0 · 100: 120 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Fibre type and endurance

More type I fibres, much longer endurance: they are fatigue-resistant because they make ATP aerobically.
Result from a test run: 0.2: 49.7 ± 0.333 · 0.35: 58 ± 0.516 · 0.5: 70.3 ± 0.715 · 0.65: 100 ± 1.19 · 0.8: 191 ± 2.06 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Which stretch gains most range?

PNF gives the largest gains (autogenic inhibition lets the muscle relax into a deeper stretch); ballistic the least, and it carries the most risk.
Theory (typical): 1: 6 · 2: 9 · 3: 13
Result from a test run: 1: 5.95 ± 0.369 · 2: 9.12 ± 0.474 · 3: 14.2 ± 0.632 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)
