Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Spot the best team

What to look for: Early tables are mostly noise: a mid-table side can top the league after five games through luck. The table becomes more trustworthy as games accumulate, but even after 30 the champion is often not the most skilful club.

Investigation 2: Does the best team win?

What to look for: With a realistic spread of skill, the best club wins only about half the time. Being the best gives you the best chance, not a guarantee. One title does not prove a team was the best, and one bad season does not prove it was not.

Investigation 3: League or cup?

What to look for: The cup winner plays only four matches, so there is far less time for skill to outweigh luck: outsiders win much more often. Short competitions are exciting because they are unpredictable; long leagues are fairer because luck averages out.

Investigation 4: A league of equals

What to look for: Pure chance still produces a 'dominant' champion and a 'hopeless' bottom club, with a gap of 20 points or more between them. People are very good at inventing explanations for patterns that are only luck.

Investigation 5: Sack the manager!

What to look for: Results improve sharply after a sacking, although nothing has changed. A run of 5 terrible games is partly bad luck, and luck does not persist: the next 5 games are closer to the team's true level. This is regression to the mean, and it flatters new managers, new training methods and 'miracle cures' alike.

Investigation 6: Fortress

What to look for: Over a league season every club plays everyone home and away, so home advantage cancels out. In a one-match cup tie it does not: the draw (who plays at home) becomes another piece of luck.

Batch experiment 1: Does the best team win the league?

Each run is one whole season with a yes/no outcome, so many runs are needed. With no skill differences the 'best' club is best in name only and wins 1 time in 16 (0.0625). A realistic league (bookmakers' odds suggest a spread around 0.4 to 0.5) gives the best club roughly an even chance.
Result from a test run: 0: 0.0167 ± 0.0167 · 0.15: 0.267 ± 0.0576 · 0.3: 0.5 ± 0.0651 · 0.45: 0.583 ± 0.0642 · 0.6: 0.617 ± 0.0633 · 0.8: 0.617 ± 0.0633 · 1: 0.75 ± 0.0564 (mean ± SE, 60 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: How long should a season be?

Luck averages out only slowly, like 1/√(games). Even a 240-game season does not make the best club certain to win, especially if the second-best club is nearly as good.
Result from a test run: 30: 0.58 ± 0.0705 · 60: 0.68 ± 0.0666 · 120: 0.78 ± 0.0592 · 240: 0.9 ± 0.0429 (mean ± SE, 50 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: League against cup

Yes/no outcomes again, so the standard error bars matter: compare the gap between the two means with the size of the bars.
Result from a test run: 0: 0.6 ± 0.0401 · 1: 0.22 ± 0.0339 (mean ± SE, 150 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: The sacked-manager bounce

The whole distribution sits well above zero: a 'bounce' of roughly 0.7 points per game appears from nothing. Clubs are picked for sacking because their recent luck was bad. To test whether sackings truly help, compare with equally bad runs where the manager stayed.

Result from a test run: mean 0.716, SD 0.15, SE 0.0106 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 5: How much of the table is skill?

With only 16 clubs, a correlation worked out from one season is itself quite uncertain.
