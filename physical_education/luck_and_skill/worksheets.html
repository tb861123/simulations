Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Pure luck: how spread out is the table?

What this shows. Every team is exactly as good as every other, yet each season the final points spread out with an SD of about 8 points (mean about 8.0, theory 8.1): from luck alone a 'champion' finishes about 15 points clear of a mid-table team. The real Premier League in 2024/25 spread out with an SD of 18.6 points, more than twice as much, so most of the real spread must be skill. Because skill and luck add as variances, luck accounts for roughly (8 ÷ 18.6)² ≈ 19% of the real variation. The SD itself varies from season to season (about ±1.3 points) because it is calculated from only 20 teams.

2 · League or cup: does the best team win?

What this shows. Each run is 20 seasons. Even with no extra edge, the strongest team (with the usual spread of strengths) wins the league in about 60% of seasons but the knockout cup in only about 27% (grey line: exact cup theory). Give it an edge of 0.4 and it wins about 95% of leagues but still under half of cups (43%). A league is 38 matches, so luck averages out; a cup is four one-off matches, and one bad day or a penalty shoot-out ends it. The league theory (gold) is a normal approximation that treats teams' totals as independent; it comes out a few points below the simulated seasons, mostly because the real totals are not independent and the leader's rivals take points off one another. Change 'Record' to the cup to see measured cup wins against the exact theory. The % scatters (about ±10) because 20 seasons is still a small sample.

3 · Is there a manager of the month curse?

What this shows. The award goes to the team with the best record in a 'month'. In its award month the winner averages 2.5 to 3.0 points per game (grey: with short months the winner usually won every match). The month after, it averages only about 1.9 to 2.0: the 'curse'. But that is exactly what its true strength predicts against its next opponents (gold), so there is no curse at all, just regression to the mean. Winning a month needs skill AND luck; the luck does not carry over. The longer the month, the less luck is needed to win it, so the fall is smaller. Points scatter (about ±0.15) because each run is only 5 seasons of awards.

4 · How many matches until the table is fair?

What this shows. After each number of matches, how closely does the table (points per game) match the teams' true strengths? After 1 match the correlation is only about 0.35; after 5 matches about 0.66; after a full season of 38 about 0.90. The gold curve is the classical test-theory prediction r = √(σq² ÷ (σq² + σe² ÷ n)): the luck in each result (σe²) is divided by the number of matches. The measured correlations sit slightly below the curve at large n, because a correlation calculated from only 20 teams is biased a little low, and who each team happened to play adds extra noise. Conclusion: a few matches of 'form' tell you little; judge over a long run.

5 · A positive test: guilty?

What this shows. 5,000 athletes are tested with a test that catches 80% of dopers and wrongly flags 1% of clean athletes. When only 0.2% dope, just 12–14% of positive tests are real dopers: the other positives are clean athletes, because there are so many more of them. As doping becomes more common the share rises (about 55% at 1.5%, 95% at 20%). The points follow Bayes' theorem (gold), a little below it on average because a ratio of two small random counts is biased slightly low; they scatter most when doping is rare and there are only a few true positives. Tick 'Re-test positives' in the table: an independent second test raises the PPV dramatically. The real share of athletes who dope is unknown, which is exactly why the meaning of a positive test is hard to judge.

6 · How big is home advantage?

What this shows. With no home advantage, home and away wins are equally likely (about 39% each, the rest draws). As h rises, home wins climb in a curve that the seasons follow closely; h = 0.3 gives about 47%, the Premier League's 47.6% in 2018/19. The 2020/21 season without crowds had 37.9% home wins, close to h = 0. Each season scatters by about ±2 points because 380 matches is a sample: the real 2020/21 drop (about 10 points) is far bigger than that, so it is unlikely to be luck.
