Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Responders and non-responders

What this shows. Everyone does the same plan, but VO₂ max rises by very different amounts: some athletes gain twice the average, a few gain almost nothing or even lose a little (a missed block or an injury on top of a low response). The spread comes mainly from responsiveness (genes), set here to vary by ±40% as in the HERITAGE Family Study, where 20 weeks of identical training raised VO₂ max by about 0% to over 40%. The blue curve is the spread predicted from responsiveness alone; the real spread is wider and lopsided because missed sessions and injuries only ever take gains away.

2 · Best taper length for race day

What this shows. With no taper the athlete races tired: fatigue is still high. A taper that halves the volume lets fatigue (time constant 7 days) fall faster than fitness (42 days), so performance rises, peaking with a taper of about 8–16 days, close to published reviews (8–14 days best, volume cut by 40–60%: Bosquet et al. 2007). Longer tapers lose fitness. The dots sit about 2% below the theory line because missed sessions and injuries (left out of the theory) only ever cost performance. The theory line is the fitness–fatigue model for an average athlete; the dots scatter because athletes respond differently and miss different sessions.

3 · Reversibility: how fast are gains lost?

What this shows. After the training, the athlete stops completely. The gain in VO₂ max fades: about half is gone after 3–4 weeks and nearly all after 2–3 months, in line with detraining studies. The theory line is a simple exponential loss with the model's 35-day time constant. The model loses gains a little more slowly at first (the body's adaptations level off at high training loads, so the first part of the loss changes VO₂ max less), then goes below zero: by stopping everything, the athlete also loses the fitness from the training they did before the plan.

4 · Overload: how much is too much?

What this shows. A little progressive overload is safe, but adding much more than about 10% a week makes this week's load far more than the athlete is used to (an acute:chronic load ratio above 1.3–1.5), and injuries rise quickly: about six times as many at +20% a week as with no overload. The theory line is the expected number of injuries from the risk model; individual athletes scatter around it because injuries are chance events (0, 1, 2…): the risk model only says how likely they are.

5 · Specificity: which method raises VO₂ max?

What this shows. Continuous, fartlek, interval and HIIT training raise VO₂ max; circuit training a little; weight and plyometric training hardly at all, and here VO₂ max even falls because the athlete stops the running they did before. Switch the outcome to strength to see the opposite pattern. Training improves the systems it uses: specificity. Results scatter because athletes respond differently.
