Training and Adaptation · Teacher notestraining-adaptation.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Q = SV × HR

What to look for: Heart rate rises with intensity. Stroke volume rises too at first (the heart fills and contracts more strongly), then levels off at about 40–50% effort. So cardiac output rises mostly through heart rate at high intensity. Q goes from about 5 L/min at rest to over 20 L/min.

Investigation 2: Warm-up and cool-down

What to look for: The anticipatory rise: adrenaline lifts the heart rate before exercise. The warm-up raises heart rate and breathing gradually, warming muscles and preparing the body. The cool-down keeps blood flowing to remove lactic acid and prevent blood pooling, bringing the heart rate down gradually.

Investigation 3: Interval training

What to look for: Work periods push into the anaerobic zone (80–90% of HR max); rest periods let it fall back to the aerobic zone. Intervals let you do more high-intensity work than one continuous effort.

Investigation 4: A trained heart

What to look for: After training: lower resting HR (bradycardia) and a larger resting stroke volume (the heart muscle is bigger and stronger: hypertrophy). The same pace now takes a lower heart rate, because VO₂ max is higher and the pace is a smaller share of it.

Investigation 5: A 12-week plan

What to look for: VO₂ max rises by about 10–15% over 12 weeks for an untrained teenager; resting HR falls by several beats. Gains are fastest at first and then level off: diminishing returns.

Investigation 6: Fitness and fatigue

What to look for: Each session adds fatigue (fast to build, fast to fade, about a week) and fitness (slow to build, slow to fade, about six weeks). Performance = fitness − fatigue. Just after training, fatigue wins; over weeks, fitness accumulates. This is why rest days matter.

Investigation 7: Reversibility

What to look for: Reversibility: 'use it or lose it'. Gains fade over a few weeks once training stops, the fastest losses first. A little maintenance training keeps most of them.

Investigation 8: Too much, too soon

What to look for: Injury risk rises steeply when this week's load is much more than the athlete has been used to over the past month (ratio above about 1.3–1.5). Progressive overload should be gradual: about 5–10% per week.

Investigation 9: Specificity

What to look for: Specificity: training must match the demands of the activity. Running builds aerobic fitness, which matters little for the shot put; weights (and plyometrics) build the strength and power it needs.

Investigation 10: Tapering A level

What to look for: Fatigue fades in about a week but fitness lasts about six weeks, so a short taper sheds fatigue while keeping fitness: race-day performance rises. Too long and fitness starts to fade. Studies suggest 8–14 days is best (Bosquet et al., 2007).

Investigation 11: Cardiovascular drift A level

What to look for: Sweating reduces blood plasma volume, so less blood returns to the heart: stroke volume falls, and heart rate rises to keep cardiac output up. Drinking during exercise reduces drift.

Batch experiment 1: Heart rate and intensity (Karvonen)

A straight line: the Karvonen method. The gradient is the heart-rate reserve, HR max − HR rest.
Theory (HR rest + % × (HR max − HR rest)): 40: 122 · 50: 136 · 60: 150 · 70: 163 · 80: 177 · 90: 190
Result from a test run: 40: 123 ± 1.15 · 50: 136 ± 0.897 · 60: 150 ± 0.608 · 70: 162 ± 0.514 · 80: 177 ± 0.465 · 90: 191 ± 0.244 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Responders and non-responders

A wide spread: some athletes gain twice as much as others from the same training, and a few hardly respond. Genetics matters.
Theory (average athlete, no missed sessions): 15.2
Result from a test run: mean 13.5, SD 4.23, SE 0.299 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 3: Frequency

More sessions, more gain, but with diminishing returns: the fifth or sixth session adds little, and adds fatigue and injury risk.
Result from a test run: 1: 4.64 ± 0.178 · 2: 9.04 ± 0.589 · 3: 11.5 ± 2.22 · 4: 16.9 ± 0.0944 · 5: 15.6 ± 1.29 · 6: 18.8 ± 1.13 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Reversibility: how fast are gains lost?

Gains fade over weeks. The model loses them a little more slowly at first than a simple exponential, because the body takes time to respond in both directions.
Theory (simple exponential loss (35 days)): 0: 100 · 1: 81.9 · 2: 67 · 3: 54.9 · 4: 44.9 · 6: 30.1 · 8: 20.2 · 10: 13.5 · 12: 9.07
Result from a test run: 0: 100 ± 0 · 1: 101 ± 0.323 · 2: 96.1 ± 0.297 · 3: 90.6 ± 0.559 · 4: 67.7 ± 14.9 · 6: 45.3 ± 14.2 · 8: 33.6 ± 3.42 · 10: 21 ± 0.784 · 12: 10.3 ± 0.37 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Overload: how much is too much?

Injuries climb steeply above about 10% per week, as the acute:chronic load ratio rises. Gradual overload is safer.
Theory (expected from the risk model): 0: 0.168 · 5: 0.238 · 10: 0.356 · 15: 0.554 · 20: 0.878 · 25: 1.39
Result from a test run: 0: 0.1 ± 0.0557 · 5: 0.4 ± 0.132 · 10: 0.333 ± 0.13 · 15: 0.733 ± 0.209 · 20: 1.27 ± 0.253 · 25: 1.93 ± 0.299 (mean ± SE, 30 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: Best taper length

A peak at about one to two weeks: long enough for fatigue to fade, short enough that fitness is kept (Bosquet et al., 2007: 8–14 days).
Result from a test run: 0: 104 ± 0.33 · 3: 104 ± 0.2 · 6: 106 ± 0.185 · 9: 106 ± 0.206 · 12: 105 ± 0.36 · 15: 106 ± 0.316 · 20: 105 ± 0.253 · 25: 105 ± 0.181 · 30: 105 ± 0.0989 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 7: Specificity

Aerobic methods (continuous, interval, fartlek, HIIT) help a 5 km runner most; weights and plyometrics help little. Try the shot put in the simulation to see the reverse.
Result from a test run: 1: 105 ± 0.117 · 2: 105 ± 0.26 · 3: 104 ± 0.27 · 4: 101 ± 0.136 · 5: 98.5 ± 0.274 · 6: 98 ± 0.187 · 7: 105 ± 0.372 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)
