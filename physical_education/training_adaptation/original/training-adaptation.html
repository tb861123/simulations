Key ideas

• Cardiac output Q = stroke volume × heart rate.
• Minute ventilation VE = tidal volume × breathing rate.
• HR max ≈ 220 − age. Aerobic training zone 60–80% of HR max; anaerobic 80–90%.
• Heart rate rises just before exercise (anticipatory rise, adrenaline).
• Stroke volume levels off at moderate intensity; after that only heart rate rises.
• A warm-up raises pulse, breathing and muscle temperature gradually; a cool-down brings them down gradually and helps remove lactic acid.
• Starling's law: more venous return → more stretch → stronger contraction → bigger SV.
• a-vO₂ diff = oxygen taken from each 100 ml of blood; VO₂ = Q × a-vO₂ diff.
• Cardiovascular drift: in long sessions HR creeps up while SV falls (sweating, less blood plasma, more blood to the skin).
• Specificity: train the systems your sport uses.
• Progressive overload: gradually do more than you are used to.
• Reversibility: stop training and the gains are lost (detraining).
• Tedium: vary training to avoid boredom.
• FITT: Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type.
• Long-term effects: lower resting HR (bradycardia), bigger stroke volume, higher VO₂ max, more capillaries; strength training → hypertrophy.
• Too much, too soon → overtraining and injury; rest days let the body adapt.
• Periodisation: a macrocycle (a season or year) is split into mesocycles (blocks of 2–8 weeks) and microcycles (usually a week). Phases: preparation, competition, transition.
• Tapering: cut the volume (not intensity) before a major event so fatigue fades while fitness stays.
• Altitude training: less O₂ → more EPO → more red blood cells; but harder to train hard, and the benefit fades in weeks.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Heart rate follows the Karvonen relation used by ACSM: HR = HR rest + I × (HR max − HR rest), where I is the fraction of the heart-rate (and VO₂) reserve. HR max = 220 − age. HR moves towards its target with a time lag (about 25 s going up, 55 s coming down). It rises by up to 12 bpm in the minute before exercise (anticipatory rise) and, with drift switched on, by about 1 bpm every 10 min after 20 min (cardiovascular drift, with stroke volume falling a little).
  • Stroke volume at rest = 5000 ml/min ÷ resting HR (resting cardiac output stays about 5 L/min, so a lower resting HR means a bigger SV). It rises to 1.35 × that by about 45% intensity, then levels off.
  • Breathing: oxygen uptake rises from 3.5 ml/kg/min with intensity up to VO₂ max. Minute ventilation ≈ 25 × VO₂ (L/min), rising faster above the lactate threshold. Tidal volume rises from 0.5 L towards 55% of a 4.8 L vital capacity; breathing rate = VE ÷ TV.
  • Sessions: continuous (steady), interval (3 min hard, 2 min jog), fartlek (random changes of pace), circuit (45 s stations, 15 s changeovers), weights (40 s sets, 90 s rests), plyometrics (10 s bounds, 50 s rests) and HIIT (30 s flat out, 30 s easy). The pictures are stylised.
  • Training load: Banister's TRIMP, minutes × HRr × 0.64 e^(1.92 HRr), × a factor for the method. Progressive overload multiplies it each week; recovery weeks use 60%; the taper cuts the volume; at altitude sessions are 10% easier.
  • Fitness–fatigue (Banister impulse–response model): fitness fades with a time constant of 42 days and fatigue of 7 days; performance = 100 + k₁ × fitness − k₂ × fatigue (k₁ = 0.02, k₂ = 0.04, each × the athlete's responsiveness for fitness), in % of the starting level. Only training specific to the target event adds to fitness; all training adds fatigue.
  • Adaptations come from a pool of specific training with a time constant of 35 days and diminishing returns: VO₂ max (up to about +35% for an untrained, very responsive athlete; less for a fitter one), resting HR down (bradycardia), stroke volume up, lactate threshold up, strength and power up. Responsiveness varies between people as in the HERITAGE Family Study (same 20-week plan, VO₂ max gains from about 0% to over 40%). Altitude adds up to 5% haemoglobin (about +3% VO₂ max), lost over 3–4 weeks.
  • Tedium: the chance of missing a session rises by 2% for every week on the same method after 4 weeks, unless the methods are varied. Injury: a daily risk that rises steeply when the acute (7-day) load is more than about 1.0–1.3 × the chronic (28-day) load (Gabbett, 2016), more for high-impact methods (plyometrics) and when overtrained; an injury stops training for 1–3 weeks. Overtraining: fatigue far above fitness for two weeks → adaptation stops, resting HR rises, injury risk doubles.
  • Left out: nutrition, sleep and illness in detail, heat, the heart and lungs as organs (see the medical simulations), muscle fibre types, skill learning, the psychology of training, menstrual cycle and growth. All the rates are illustrative, chosen to give realistic sizes of change; real athletes vary far more.
