Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Critical power: how long can you last?

What this shows. The harder the intensity, the shorter the time to exhaustion. The critical power (CP) model says t = W′ ÷ (P − CP): W′ is a fixed anaerobic store (PC, glycolysis up to the H⁺ limit, stored O₂) used up at the rate (P − CP). The theory line comes from two tests only (2 and 12 minutes), as a coach would measure it, yet it predicts the model's results well from about 2 to 15 minutes. It fails at both ends: in short, very hard efforts the athlete cannot use W′ fast enough (PC and glycolysis have maximum rates), so they last less long than predicted; near CP (about 92% of VO₂ max for this athlete) the model lasts far longer than predicted, and glycogen, not H⁺, ends the run (below CP the theory predicts no limit at all). The log–log gradient printed below changes along the curve, so a simple power law does not fit: the CP model's curve does. Runs scatter because each one is a different athlete from a squad that differs by ±5%.

2 · How fast does PC recover?

What this shows. After a 6 s sprint the PC store is about half empty. During rest the aerobic system rebuilds it: half of what is missing returns in each half-life (30 s here), so about 75% after 30 s, 94% after 90 s and 98% after 3 minutes (the EPOC fast component). The theory curve uses each athlete's PC level at the end of the sprint and the squad's half-life of 30 s; the dots scatter because each athlete's half-life and PC store differ (±10%). Try a recovery intensity of 40%: an active recovery slows PC resynthesis, because the oxygen is needed for the jogging.

3 · How much rest between sprints?

What this shows. With short rests, sprint power falls through the set (percentage decrement = 100 × (1 − mean ÷ best)): PC does not recover in time. With 2 minutes of rest the sprints hardly slow. The theory line asks: what if only the PC store mattered, and glycolysis and the aerobic system gave the same in every sprint? It predicts a BIGGER fall than the model, because in later sprints the aerobic system is already running fast (oxygen uptake stays high between sprints) and covers more of the energy. Studies of repeated sprints find exactly this. Scatter: each athlete's PC store, half-life and VO₂ kinetics differ.

4 · Energy continuum: how aerobic is a best effort?

What this shows. For each length of effort the model finds the fastest even pace the athlete can hold to the end, then adds up where the energy came from (measured as oxygen uptake, as in the studies). The aerobic share rises from under 10% in 10 s to over 70% in 4 minutes, crossing 50% at about 75–90 s, close to Gastin's (2001) published values (yellow). The model is a few % below the published shares for longer efforts: its anaerobic capacity is on the large side for a middle-distance runner. Real values vary with the athlete and the test, and these runs scatter for the same reason (a squad differing by ±8%).

5 · A squad runs 1500 m

What this shows. Every athlete in the squad differs a little (±10%) in VO₂ max, lactate threshold, PC store, glycolysis, lactate tolerance and VO₂ kinetics, but they all run at the same % of their own VO₂ max. Race times spread out around the average athlete's time. The spread is lopsided: an athlete who is a bit worse in several ways at once fades badly (they pass their limit and slow down), while being better than average helps less. That is why a coach should set each athlete's pace from their own tests rather than the squad's.

6 · Active or passive recovery?

What this shows. After a 400 m lactate is about 15 mmol/L. Resting (0%), it is still about 5 mmol/L after 15 minutes. Gentle jogging keeps blood flow and oxygen supply up, so lactate is oxidised faster: at 30–40% of VO₂ max it is back to about 1 mmol/L. Jogging harder than about half the lactate threshold starts to make new lactate, so the benefit falls away. Published studies put the best active-recovery intensity at about 30–40% of VO₂ max (higher in trained athletes). There is no simple theory line; the scatter comes from athletes differing in lactate removal and tolerance.
