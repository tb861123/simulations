Energy Systems · Teacher notesenergy-systems.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The 100 m

What to look for: Almost all of it comes from the ATP store and the ATP–PC system: they are the only ones fast enough. The PC store is nearly empty by the line. The aerobic system has hardly started, because oxygen uptake takes over a minute to rise. The athlete runs almost the whole race on an oxygen deficit, repaid afterwards (EPOC).

Investigation 2: The 400 m: why it hurts

What to look for: PC is used up within about 10 seconds, so glycolysis takes over: fast, but making lactate and hydrogen ions. Blood lactate passes OBLA (4 mmol/L) early and climbs past 12 mmol/L; the acid slows glycolysis and the athlete fades over the last 100 m. Nobody can sprint 400 m: the trick is to ration the effort.

Investigation 3: The marathon

What to look for: The aerobic system supplies nearly everything, using both carbohydrate (glycogen) and fat. Lactate stays low because the pace is below the lactate threshold. If the glycogen runs out (around 30 km if the pace is too hard), fat alone cannot supply energy fast enough, and the runner is forced to slow sharply: 'hitting the wall'. Try 85% of VO₂ max.

Investigation 4: The energy continuum

What to look for: About 10% for the 100 m, around 50–60% for the 800 m and over 90% for the 5000 m. All three systems are always working together; what changes is the share. Events up to about 75 seconds are mainly anaerobic, longer ones mainly aerobic. This is the energy continuum.

Investigation 5: Repeated sprints

What to look for: PC is rebuilt during the rests, but only partly: half of it returns in about 30 seconds, all of it takes 2 to 3 minutes. So each sprint starts with less PC, uses more glycolysis, and is slower. This is what happens in football, hockey and rugby. Training raises the PC store and speeds up its recovery.

Investigation 6: Recovery: oxygen debt

What to look for: Excess post-exercise oxygen consumption (EPOC). The fast part, in the first two or three minutes, restores ATP and PC and refills myoglobin. The slow part, over the next hour or so, removes lactate, brings down the heart rate, breathing and temperature. The EPOC is larger than the oxygen deficit that caused it.

Investigation 7: Active or passive recovery?

What to look for: A light jog removes lactate about twice as fast, because working muscles take it up and use it as fuel, and the raised blood flow carries it away. PC, on the other hand, recovers fastest at complete rest. So: walk or jog lightly after hard anaerobic work, rest completely between short sprints.

Investigation 8: Training makes a difference

What to look for: The trained runner has a higher VO₂ max (more oxygen delivered and used each minute) and a higher lactate threshold, so a larger share of the energy comes aerobically, less lactate is made, and the race is much faster. Aerobic training improves the heart, the capillaries and the mitochondria; anaerobic training enlarges the PC store and the tolerance of lactate.

Investigation 9: OBLA and pacing A level

What to look for: OBLA (onset of blood lactate accumulation, 4 mmol/L) comes at a much lower percentage of VO₂ max in the untrained student. Above it lactate keeps rising and the race is lost. Endurance athletes train to raise their lactate threshold, and pace so as to stay just under it for as long as possible.
