The energy continuum

The three energy systems

Recovery strategies (A level)

• Active cool-down at about 30–40% of VO₂ max keeps blood flow and oxygen supply up, so lactate is removed faster (try the slider and the batch experiment).
• Work:relief ratios: ATP–PC training 1:3 or more (PC needs about 3 min to refill fully); glycolytic 1:2; aerobic 1:1.
• Cooling aids (ice baths) bring body temperature and metabolic rate down faster and reduce soreness.
• Nutrition: carbohydrate restores glycogen (24–48 h), creatine supplements raise PC stores; rehydrate.
• Training raises VO₂ max and the lactate threshold, delays OBLA and speeds up VO₂ kinetics and PC recovery.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Energy demand. The activity sets how much energy the muscles need, as a % of the energy the athlete's VO₂ max can supply (1 ml O₂ ≈ 20.9 J). Running speed comes from the ACSM running equation (VO₂ = 0.2 × speed in m/min + 3.5 ml/kg/min), plus an illustrative extra cost for flat-out sprinting. "Flat out" means as much power as the energy systems can give at that instant. Resting metabolism is 3.5 ml/kg/min (1 MET).
  • Aerobic system. Oxygen uptake rises towards what is needed with a time constant (VO₂ kinetics, 12–30 s), up to VO₂ max. Above the lactate threshold the oxygen cost creeps up slowly (the "slow component"). Fats alone can give only about 55% of VO₂ max, so when glycogen runs out the athlete slows ("hitting the wall").
  • Oxygen deficit. Whatever oxygen uptake cannot yet cover is met first by oxygen stored on myoglobin (about 2 ml/kg), then by glycolysis (which takes about 5 s to speed up), then by PC, then by the small ATP store.
  • ATP–PC system. PC can be broken down very fast, but its rate falls as the store empties (most is gone in about 8–10 s flat out). PC is rebuilt only when oxygen is spare, exponentially with a half-life of about 30 s at rest (slower during active recovery): 50% back in 30 s, about 98% in 3 minutes.
  • Glycolytic system and lactate. 1 mmol/L of blood lactate is counted as 62.7 J/kg of anaerobic energy (3 ml O₂/kg). Lactate is removed by a process that speeds up with light exercise but has a maximum rate, so above the lactate threshold it piles up (OBLA is taken as 4 mmol/L). H⁺ from glycolysis slows glycolysis down (it inhibits PFK); the athlete is exhausted when the systems together can no longer meet the demand.
  • EPOC. After exercise oxygen uptake stays above the recovery level. The FAST component is the oxygen used to rebuild ATP and PC and refill myoglobin; the SLOW component is the oxygen used to remove lactate (taken as 50% of its energy) and to keep heart rate, breathing and body temperature raised (temperature rises with work and falls over about 15 minutes, 5 with a cooling aid). These split values are illustrative.
  • Glycogen is used by aerobic carbohydrate burning (the share rises from about 10% of the fuel at low intensity to almost 100% near VO₂ max) and by glycolysis, which needs about 12 times more glycogen for the same energy (2–3 ATP per glucose instead of 38).
  • Athletes: the five starting athletes have typical published values (VO₂ max 40–72 ml/kg/min, lactate threshold 55–82% of VO₂ max). Rates such as the fastest PC breakdown and glycolysis are illustrative, chosen so that race times, peak lactate (12–18 mmol/L after 400 m), the energy shares (Gastin, 2001) and the PC half-life are realistic.
  • Left out: muscle fibre types, pacing tactics and a final sprint, wind and bends, heat and dehydration, the heart and lungs in detail, protein as a fuel, the delay between lactate in the muscle and in the blood, and lactate shuttling between muscles. Blood lactate here is one number for the whole body.
