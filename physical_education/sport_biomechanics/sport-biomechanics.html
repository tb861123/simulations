How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • The athlete is 13 body segments plus the head. Segment lengths and masses are standard fractions of height and body mass (Dempster's cadaver data as tabulated by Winter, 2009: e.g. trunk 49.7%, each thigh 10%, head 8.1%). The centre of mass is the mass-weighted average of the segments' own centres of mass. The moment of inertia adds m r² for every segment plus each segment's own inertia (treated as a cylinder). This gives about 14 kg m² for a straight body with arms overhead, 7.6 in a pike and 4.5 in a tight tuck (about the somersault axis), close to published values. The drawings are stylised.
  • Levers: the joint is held still, so the moments balance: effort × effort arm = load × load arm. The weight of the bones themselves, the angle the muscle pulls at, and the fact that several muscles share the work are left out. In the calf-raise lever the fulcrum is taken as the ball of the foot, as at GCSE; anatomists often model the ankle joint as the fulcrum instead, which gives a much larger calf force.
  • Planes and axes: each action is drawn as a simple, idealised movement in one plane. Real sporting actions mix planes (a golf swing also has flexion, a discus turn also travels across the circle).
  • Stability: the push needed to topple = weight × (distance from the line of gravity to the edge of the base) ÷ (height of the centre of mass). The athlete is treated as rigid; real athletes react by moving their feet, lowering or leaning into the push.
  • Sprint: speed follows v = v_max (1 − e^(−t/τ)) after the blocks. Each ground contact has a braking then a propulsive impulse (half-sine shapes, contact time 0.20 s falling to 0.09 s at top speed), sized so the net impulse equals the change in momentum. After 100 m the athlete slows down. Air resistance, the bend and fatigue are left out.
  • Jump and landing: the force plate measures the vertical force F; acceleration = (F − weight) ÷ m. The force shapes are idealised half-sines. Landing peak force ≈ weight + π m v ÷ (2 × stopping time).
  • Projectiles: weight, drag ½ρv²C_dA (against the motion through the air) and lift ½ρv²C_lA (at right angles to it), stepped every 2–4 ms. Spin lift uses C_l = 1.5 S ÷ (1 + 2S) with spin parameter S = rω/v, an illustrative fit to wind-tunnel data; the spin does not decay. Discus: its attitude stays fixed in flight (held by its spin), so the angle of attack changes along the path; C_l = 1.6 α, stalling above about 29°. Javelin: a fixed angle of attack. The long jumper is treated as a point (the centre of mass), landing when it falls to 0.45 m. With "real athletes" the release speed falls by 0.04 m/s for every degree above 37° (and rises below), the pattern measured for shot putters (Linthorne, 2001).
  • Flow pictures use ideal (potential) flow round a circle or an ellipse, with circulation for spin or angle of attack, so they show the faster and slower air and the pressure difference correctly but cannot show drag by themselves; the turbulent wake is drawn on top. Drag and lift forces use the equations above with typical published coefficients (cyclist C_dA 0.40, 0.32, 0.24 m²; swimmer glide C_dA 0.018–0.036 m²; ski jumper lift and drag from a flat-plate model).
  • Angular motion: angular momentum L = I ω is fixed at take-off and stays constant in the air, because the only outside force (weight) acts through the centre of mass. I is worked out from the changing body shape every 5 ms. For the skater a small friction torque slowly reduces L. Air resistance and the twisting that real divers add are left out.
  • Bouncing balls (Physics option C): the ball falls with weight and drag ½ρC_dAv² (C_d = 0.5), in 1 ms steps. At the floor it leaves with e × its arrival speed, where e (the coefficient of restitution) is the ball's typical value from the governing bodies' bounce tests (approximate) times a factor for the surface (illustrative: concrete 1, sports-hall floor 0.97, artificial grass 0.85, natural grass 0.75, gym mat 0.45). Real e also falls a little at higher impact speeds and depends on temperature (see the squash balls), which is left out except for the squash ball choice. The contact force is a half-sine lasting the ball's contact time. Measuring the bounce height by eye against a metre rule: SD 2 cm, read to 1 cm; from a video frame by frame: SD 0.5 cm, read to 0.5 cm; the drop height is set to ±0.5 cm.
  • I part by part: the same segment model, with each segment's m r² (plus its own inertia) added into five groups. Rotational kinetic energy = ½Iω² = L²/2I: with L fixed, tucking (smaller I) raises it; the athlete's muscles do that work pulling the limbs in.
