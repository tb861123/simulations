The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Best release angle vs release height

What this shows. Released from ground level (h = 0) the best angle is 45°. The higher the release, the lower the best angle: from 2.1 m (a typical shot putter's hand) it is about 42°, because the shot has extra time to fall and travel forwards. The model (with air resistance) sits a fraction of a degree below the no-air theory: drag on a heavy shot is tiny. There is no scatter because every throw here is identical.

2 · Why do shot putters release at about 37°?

What this shows. With the same release speed at every angle (gold line) the best angle is about 42°. But athletes can push the shot faster when it leaves at a flatter angle (here 0.04 m/s faster for every degree lower, as measured for elite shot putters). With that speed change (grey dashed line, and the dots) the best angle drops to about 37°, the angle elite throwers actually use. The curve is flat near the top, so a few degrees either side cost only a few centimetres. Dots scatter because each throw's speed varies a little (SD 0.1 m/s).

3 · How far do small release errors scatter a throw?

What this shows. Nobody releases exactly the same way twice. Small random errors in speed (SD 0.25 m/s) and angle (SD 2°) spread the distances into a bell-shaped curve. The blue curve is the spread predicted by error propagation: SD of distance = √((∂R/∂v · σᵥ)² + (∂R/∂θ · σθ)²). Almost all of it comes from the speed: at 37° the distance hardly depends on the angle (the top of the curve is flat). The mean is a little below the no-air theory because of drag.

4 · Spin rate vs swerve

What this shows. More sidespin gives a bigger Magnus force, so the ball curls further: a few metres over 25 m at 6–10 revolutions per second, as in a curled free kick. The simple theory (a constant sideways force, lift coefficient 1.5 × spin parameter, no drag) predicts a straight line. The model curls less than that at high spin because the lift coefficient levels off and because drag slows the ball (a slower ball has less Magnus force, though it is in the air longer). Dots scatter because the kick speed varies (SD 0.5 m/s).

5 · Tucking: does L = Iω hold?

What this shows. With the same angular momentum (50 kg m²/s), halving the moment of inertia doubles the angular velocity: on log–log axes the points fall on a straight line of gradient −1 (ω = L ÷ I). A tight tuck (about 4 kg m²) spins about three times faster than a straight layout (about 14 kg m²). Points scatter because each take-off gives slightly different angular momentum (SD 5%) and each tuck is slightly different (SD 3%).

6 · Bend the knees: landing force vs stopping time

What this shows. The momentum to remove is fixed by the drop height (m × √(2gh)). Impulse = force × time, so spreading the stop over a longer time (bending the knees, rolling, landing on a mat) lowers the peak force sharply: from about 15 × body weight with stiff legs (0.04 s) to about 2 × with a deep, soft landing (0.5 s). Doubling the stopping time roughly halves the extra force above body weight. The theory line is the half-sine force shape the model uses. Dots scatter because the drop height varies (SD 5 cm).
