How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Reaction time of the simulated performer: simple RT a = 0.20 s for a light, 0.16 s for a sound, 0.155 s for a touch (typical young-adult values, as summarised by Kosinski, 2008), slower for children (+3.5% per year under 20) and older adults (+0.35% per year over 25), with fatigue (up to +25%) and with too little or too much arousal (up to +30%), and 10% quicker with experience.
  • Hick's law: mean RT = a + b log₂N, with b = 0.15 s per bit for a novice (Hick, 1952, found about 0.15 s per bit), falling to 0.06 s per bit with experience. Single reactions are skewed: an ex-Gaussian (a normal curve plus an exponential tail) with SD about 22% of the mean. Wrong choices: about 2% plus 2% per bit.
  • Anticipation (A level): temporal anticipation removes 35 ms; spatial anticipation makes the response 30% quicker than simple RT when the guess is right, but adds 80 ms (stop, then choose again) and more errors when it is wrong. Values are illustrative.
  • Information processing: input (detection and perception) takes 40% of simple RT, plus up to 0.12 s for distractions (a quarter of that with selective attention); decision takes the other 60% plus b log₂N; output is the movement time for the situation (tennis 0.32 s, goalkeeper dive 0.55 s, cricket 0.26 s, tackle 0.3 s), 15% less with experience. Each stage varies by about 15% each time. The ball travels at a constant speed (in reality a tennis ball slows after the bounce).
  • Single-channel hypothesis and PRP: the decision stage handles one stimulus at a time. The real move's decision cannot start until the dummy's decision has finished, so the delay = (dummy's input + decision) − gap − real move's input, if positive. Gaps under 0.04 s are treated as one stimulus (grouping). If the performer has started moving the wrong way, changing direction adds 0.08 s.
  • Learners: each learner has a level from 0 to 1 for each part of the skill, for linking the parts, and for adaptability. Each attempt raises the parts being practised by a learning rate (which varies between learners) × guidance × feedback × attention load × (1 − 0.6 × fatigue) × what is still left to learn. Practising many parts at once overloads a beginner in a complex skill (the load falls as the parts are learned); a part practised alone transfers less well to the whole when the skill is highly organised or serial; linking is learned only by doing the parts together. Skill level = the geometric mean of the parts × (1 − organisation + organisation × linking); success chance = 5% + 90% × skill level.
  • Practice types: part practice = each part in turn for 70% of the attempts, then the whole; progressive part = each new part alone, then linked to the chain. Fatigue builds with each attempt in proportion to how tiring the skill is and recovers with a time constant of 40 s, so massed practice of a tiring skill learns less. 60–75% of each gain is stable (more with longer rests: spacing); the rest fades by 30% overnight and by 65% before the retention test a week later. Variable practice slows learning the basic movement by 25% but builds adaptability, which counts in open skills (in a test the skill level is reduced by 50% × openness × (1 − adaptability)).
  • Guidance and feedback multipliers depend on the stage of learning (cognitive, associative, autonomous: skill level below 0.35, below 0.7, above): visual guidance and positive knowledge of results help beginners most; verbal guidance, knowledge of performance and negative feedback help more skilled performers. Frequent extrinsic feedback speeds learning in practice but creates dependence: retention × (1 − 0.3 f²) for concurrent feedback (0.55 of that for terminal), the guidance hypothesis. Manual and mechanical guidance cost 3% in retention. All these sizes are illustrative, chosen to match the direction of the published findings, not measured values.
  • Schema (A level): distance ≈ effort ÷ 10. The learner's rule is effort = α + β × distance, updated after each throw by Bayesian least squares from a starting rule (past experience). Each throw's effort varies by 5% + 1 unit (×1.6 with the other hand). Without knowledge of results, each throw tells the learner three times less.
  • Left out: motivation and boredom, individual learning styles, mental practice, the coach's skill, memory decay over months, and differences between sports in detail. Real learners vary far more than these do.
