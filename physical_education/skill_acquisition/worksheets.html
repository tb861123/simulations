Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Simple reaction time

What to look for: Simple reaction times are usually about 0.2 s. They vary from try to try and the spread is skewed (a few slow ones), so take the average of several.

Investigation 2: Hick's law

What to look for: Each doubling adds about the same time (about 0.12 s): RT = a + b log₂N. This is why good players disguise their shots: more options for the opponent means slower reactions. (Hick's law is beyond the OCR specs.)

Investigation 3: Response time

What to look for: Response time = reaction time + movement time = 0.40 s. Reaction time improves with practice, anticipation and concentrating on the right cue; movement time with power training.

Investigation 4: The dummy

What to look for: Single-channel hypothesis: the brain processes one stimulus at a time, so the real move waits until the dummy has been dealt with: the psychological refractory period. A well-timed dummy (about 0.1 s before the real move) gives the biggest delay.

Investigation 5: Guidance

What to look for: Beginners (cognitive stage) need a clear picture of the skill, so visual guidance (a demonstration) works best; too much verbal information overloads them. Manual and mechanical guidance help with safety and the feel of a movement.

Investigation 6: Feedback

What to look for: 100% feedback gives the best practice performance but learners come to depend on it, so they remember less without it (the guidance hypothesis). Fading the feedback helps retention.

Investigation 7: Classify the skills

What to look for: Difficulty: simple (few decisions, e.g. a dart) to complex (many decisions and parts, e.g. a gymnastics routine). Environment: closed (stable, predictable, e.g. a golf putt) to open (changing, e.g. a pass in a game).

Investigation 8: Massed or distributed? A level

What to look for: Distributed practice: less fatigue and time to think, so better learning and retention, especially for complex, demanding skills and beginners. Massed practice suits simple, discrete skills and experienced performers.

Investigation 9: Whole or part? A level

What to look for: Low-organisation skills (separate parts) can be taught in parts; highly organised skills (cycling, a dive) lose their timing when split, so whole (or progressive part) practice is better.

Investigation 10: Schema and variable practice A level

What to look for: Variable practice builds a stronger schema (the rule linking effort and distance), so performers adapt better to new situations: recall and recognition schema (Schmidt). Fixed practice suits closed skills.

Batch experiment 1: Does Hick's law hold?

Each doubling of choices adds the same time: equal steps at 1, 2, 4 and 8. (Hick's law is beyond the OCR specs.)
Theory (0.221 + 0.123 log₂N): 1: 0.221 · 2: 0.344 · 3: 0.416 · 4: 0.467 · 5: 0.507 · 6: 0.539 · 7: 0.566 · 8: 0.59
Result from a test run: 1: 0.218 ± 0.00136 · 2: 0.342 ± 0.00164 · 3: 0.417 ± 0.00173 · 4: 0.467 ± 0.00212 · 5: 0.506 ± 0.00208 · 6: 0.54 ± 0.00172 · 7: 0.567 ± 0.00204 · 8: 0.586 ± 0.00198 (mean ± SE, 40 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: How much does a single reaction vary?

Skewed: most reactions near 0.2 s, a tail of slow ones and none much below 0.1 s (faster counts as a false start). One reaction is a poor measure: average several.
Theory (mean): 0.221
Result from a test run: mean 0.218, SD 0.0358, SE 0.00113 from 1000 runs

Batch experiment 3: The psychological refractory period

Very short gaps are grouped and treated as one; from about 0.05 s the response waits for the dummy's decision, falling 1 ms for every 1 ms of gap until the dummy has been processed.
Theory (single-channel model): 0.02: 0.36 · 0.06: 0.5 · 0.1: 0.46 · 0.15: 0.41 · 0.2: 0.36 · 0.25: 0.34 · 0.35: 0.34 · 0.5: 0.34
Result from a test run: 0.02: 0.364 ± 0.00321 · 0.06: 0.499 ± 0.00385 · 0.1: 0.464 ± 0.00423 · 0.15: 0.415 ± 0.00462 · 0.2: 0.363 ± 0.00443 · 0.25: 0.337 ± 0.00545 · 0.35: 0.339 ± 0.005 · 0.5: 0.336 ± 0.00449 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Massed or distributed practice?

Distributed practice wins for a demanding, continuous skill: less fatigue and better retention.
Theory (model expectation): 0: 64.6 · 5: 64.6 · 15: 73.9 · 30: 78.2 · 60: 78.2
Result from a test run: 0: 62.5 ± 2.98 · 5: 56 ± 2.28 · 15: 75.5 ± 1.81 · 30: 74.5 ± 2.83 · 60: 78.8 ± 2.4 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Part or whole practice?

Part practice helps low-organisation skills and harms highly organised ones, where the parts must flow together.
Theory (model expectation): 0: 13.1 · 0.25: 10.4 · 0.5: 2.9 · 0.75: -4.74 · 1: -12.6
Result from a test run: 0: 16 ± 2.94 · 0.25: 9.25 ± 2.7 · 0.5: 7.5 ± 3.33 · 0.75: -7.75 ± 4.14 · 1: -12.3 ± 4.08 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: Variable practice and schema

Variety lets the learner work out the rule linking effort and distance (a schema), so they cope with a distance they have never practised.
