Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Does Hick's law hold?

What this shows. Each point is the mean of 20 reactions by a simulated performer. Reaction time rises with the number of choices, but each extra choice adds less: the curve is RT = a + b log₂N, so DOUBLING the choices adds the same time (about 0.13 s here). Tick 'log x' and the points fall on a straight line. The means scatter because single reactions vary a lot (SD about 0.05–0.1 s) and the slow tail pulls some means up; a mean of 20 has an SD about √20 ≈ 4.5 times smaller. The same random numbers are used at every step, so if they happen to be a little quick, every point sits a little below the line together. Real data (Hick, 1952; Hyman, 1953) fit the same law, with slopes of about 0.15 s per bit for unpractised people.

2 · How much does a single reaction vary?

What this shows. One reaction is never exactly the average. The histogram is skewed: a steep rise on the fast side (nobody can react much faster than about 0.12 s) and a long tail of slow reactions (a lapse of attention). So the normal curve (blue) does not fit well even though the mean matches the theory: reaction times follow an ex-Gaussian shape. This is why tests take the mean (or median) of several trials, and why World Athletics can set 0.100 s as the false-start limit: genuine reactions to the gun are almost never that fast.

3 · The psychological refractory period

What this shows. The defender's reaction to the REAL move after a dummy. With a short gap, the real move waits while the dummy is still being decided (the single channel is busy), so reaction time is long; the wait shrinks by 1 ms for every extra ms of gap, until the dummy has been dealt with (about 0.15 s here) and reaction time is back to normal (dashed line). Below 0.04 s the two are taken as one stimulus (grouping), so a dummy that is too quick does not work either. Points scatter because each stage varies by about 15% from trial to trial; near the corner the average sits a little above the theory line, because sometimes the dummy is slow and sometimes the real move is.

4 · Massed or distributed practice?

What this shows. New learners practise a tiring skill (front crawl) for 200 attempts with different rests, then do a retention test a week later. With no rest they tire, learn less from each attempt and keep less of it, so retention is lowest; longer rests help, with diminishing returns after about 30 s. The dashed line is the end-of-practice level: the gap down to the retention line is what fades. Each dot is one learner's score out of 20, so dots scatter widely (learners learn at different rates, and 20 attempts is a small test). Set 'How tiring' to 0.02 (a golf putt): the benefit of rest shrinks. Distributed practice takes far longer, which is the cost.

5 · Part or whole practice?

What this shows. The same learners learn a complex skill by part practice and by whole practice; the dots show how much better (above 0) or worse (below 0) part practice is in the retention test. When organisation is LOW (the parts are separate, like a tennis serve's toss and swing) part practice wins: a beginner cannot attend to every part at once. When organisation is HIGH (the parts run together, like cycling or a swimming stroke's timing) whole practice wins: parts practised alone do not fit together. The crossover is the classification rule for choosing practice. Learners differ in learning rate, so the dots spread round the theory line.

6 · Variable practice and schema

What this shows. Learners practise 30 throws at targets spread round 5 m (spread 0 = fixed practice at 5 m), then throw 5 times at a new 9 m target with no feedback. With fixed practice the learner knows only one point, so their rule for other distances is still the vague starting guess, and the new target is missed by about a metre. A wider spread pins down the rule (the schema), and the error falls towards the limit set by how much each throw varies. This is Schmidt's schema theory: variable practice transfers to new situations. Errors scatter because every throw varies by about 5%.
