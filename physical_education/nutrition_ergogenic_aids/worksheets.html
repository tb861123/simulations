Nutrition and Ergogenic Aids · Teacher notesnutrition-ergogenic-aids.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Hitting the wall

What to look for: Around 30–33 km the stores run out: the runner 'hits the wall' and can only keep a pace fat can fuel, so the last few km are much slower. Starting a little slower would make the glycogen last.

Investigation 2: Carbo-loading and gels

What to look for: Loading raises the stores by up to 60%, and carbohydrate during the race tops them up: the wall moves later or disappears, and the finish time improves by several minutes.

Investigation 3: Drink enough, not too much

What to look for: Losing more than about 2% of body mass slows the runner. Drinking to roughly match sweat prevents it. Drinking far more than you sweat dilutes the blood's sodium (hyponatraemia), which can be fatal.

Investigation 4: Which aid for which event?

What to look for: 800 m: bicarbonate buffers the acid from anaerobic glycolysis; EPO raises oxygen delivery (but is banned and dangerous). Repeated sprints: creatine raises PC stores and speeds recovery between sprints. Match the aid to the energy system the event uses.

Investigation 5: False positives A level

What to look for: About 18 true positives and 5 false positives: about 4 in 5 positives are real. If only 0.5% dope, false positives can outnumber true ones. That is why a B sample, a biological passport and very specific tests matter.

Investigation 6: EPO: the cost A level

What to look for: About 7% more VO₂max can save several minutes. But thicker blood raises the risk of clots, strokes and heart attacks, especially when dehydrated; it is unfair and against the spirit of sport.

Batch experiment 1: Carbohydrate during the marathon

Gels delay the wall; once it is avoided, extra carbohydrate gives little more.
Theory (average day): 0: 206 · 15: 202 · 30: 199 · 45: 195 · 60: 191 · 90: 191
Result from a test run: 0: 205 ± 0.846 · 15: 202 ± 0.731 · 30: 198 ± 0.633 · 45: 196 ± 0.596 · 60: 190 ± 1.1 · 90: 189 ± 1.03 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Dehydration and pace

Little effect below about 2%, then about 2% slower for each extra 1% lost.
Theory (model): 0: 100 · 1: 100 · 2: 99 · 3: 97 · 4: 95 · 5: 93
Result from a test run: 0: 100 ± 0.184 · 1: 100 ± 0.17 · 2: 99.1 ± 0.148 · 3: 96.8 ± 0.14 · 4: 95.1 ± 0.263 · 5: 93.3 ± 0.249 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Creatine and repeated sprints

Creatine helps most with short rests, when PC has least time to recover.
Result from a test run: 10: 0.444 ± 0.148 · 20: 0.569 ± 0.17 · 30: 1.08 ± 0.133 · 45: 0.778 ± 0.0809 · 60: 0.386 ± 0.0596 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: How trustworthy is a positive test?

When doping is rare, a large share of positives are false. Re-testing the B sample and using several independent tests protects clean athletes.
Theory (Bayes' theorem): 0.5: 47.5 · 1: 64.5 · 2: 78.6 · 5: 90.5 · 10: 95.2 · 20: 97.8
Result from a test run: 0.5: 44.3 ± 5.7 · 1: 66.4 ± 2.72 · 2: 81.1 ± 2.41 · 5: 90.4 ± 1.58 · 10: 96.4 ± 0.369 · 20: 97.4 ± 0.273 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: 200 marathon runners, no fuel plan

A long tail: those whose glycogen runs out early lose a lot of time in the last kilometres.
Result from a test run: mean 204, SD 4.53, SE 0.32 from 200 runs
