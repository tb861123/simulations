Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Moral and natural evil

What to look for: Red/orange flashes are hardships (natural evil); purple are harms chosen by people (moral evil). With no freedom, moral evil vanishes, but so does every chosen act of help and the virtue it builds: the free will defence in miniature.

Investigation 2: The inconsistent triad

What to look for: With two hidden premises (a good being removes evil as far as it can; there are no limits to what an omnipotent being can do), the three claims contradict each other.

Investigation 3: A vale of soul-making

What to look for: With no hardship there is nothing to help with or endure, so virtue hardly grows: Hick's point that a hedonistic paradise could not make souls.

Investigation 4: Too much suffering

What to look for: Up to a point, harder challenges build more; beyond it, suffering crushes people rather than making them: dysteleological suffering, the strongest objection to Hick.

Investigation 5: Epistemic distance A level

What to look for: If God's presence were overwhelming, people would do good from fear or for reward, not freely. Hick: an epistemic distance makes genuine faith and goodness possible.

Investigation 6: Augustine or Irenaeus? A level

What to look for: Augustine: natural evil is the result of the Fall (hard to square with evolution). Irenaeus/Hick: natural evil is part of a soul-making environment (looks forward, not back), but must explain its excess.

Investigation 7: Rowe's fawn A level

What to look for: If each case is 3 times more likely without God, 10 cases make God's existence very improbable (well under 1%). Rowe: this is evidence, not proof.

Investigation 8: Sceptical theism A level

What to look for: If apparently pointless suffering is just as expected with God as without, seeing it is no evidence either way: the posterior stays at the prior. The debate turns on that likelihood.

Batch experiment 1: How much hardship makes souls?

An inverted U in this model: some challenge builds virtue, too much crushes it. Whether our world sits on the rising or falling side is exactly what Hick and his critics dispute.
Result from a test run: 0: -0.00545 ± 1.22e-4 · 0.25: 0.0835 ± 0.00198 · 0.5: 0.154 ± 0.00277 · 0.75: 0.182 ± 0.00371 · 1: 0.136 ± 0.00748 · 1.5: 0.012 ± 0.00766 · 2: -0.0841 ± 0.00865 · 3: -0.175 ± 0.00321 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Freedom and virtue

With no freedom there is no moral evil, but no virtue grows either; more freedom means more chosen good and more chosen harm. Whether the good outweighs the harm is the free will defence's central (and disputed) claim.
Result from a test run: 0: -0.103 ± 0.0035 · 0.2: -0.0399 ± 0.00184 · 0.4: 0.0273 ± 0.00254 · 0.6: 0.0942 ± 0.00445 · 0.8: 0.168 ± 0.00557 · 1: 0.251 ± 0.00766 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Rowe: belief after apparently pointless suffering

Whatever the starting belief, the evidence pulls everyone the same way, and priors matter less as evidence mounts. This is the shape of the evidential problem.
Theory (Bayes from a 50% prior): 0: 50 · 1: 25 · 2: 10 · 4: 1.22 · 6: 0.137 · 10: 0.00169
Result from a test run: 0: 53.6 ± 6.29 · 1: 29 ± 4.2 · 2: 14.3 ± 2.64 · 4: 1.88 ± 0.544 · 6: 0.17 ± 0.047 · 10: 0.00124 ± 2.72e-4 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Sceptical theism weakens the evidence (A level)

Full sceptical theism makes apparently pointless suffering exactly as likely with God as without: no evidence at all. Critics reply that it also undermines our moral judgements and knowledge of God's goodness.
