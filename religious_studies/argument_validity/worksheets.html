Arguments: Validity and Soundness · Teacher notesargument-validity.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Valid and true

What to look for: After shading for both premises, every Greek left is inside 'mortal': the conclusion is already drawn, so it is valid (the form 'Barbara', AAA-1). Its premises are true, so it is sound.

Investigation 2: Valid but false

What to look for: Exactly the same form as the Greeks, so it is valid; but 'all fish can fly' is false, so it is unsound. Validity is about the form, soundness also about the truth of the premises.

Investigation 3: True premises, invalid argument

What to look for: Undistributed middle: both premises only say dogs and cats are inside 'mammals'; they could be different parts of it. The Venn diagram leaves a region of dogs that are not cats.

Investigation 4: Spot the fallacy

What to look for: Illicit major: the conclusion talks about all believers, but the premise 'all theists are believers' doesn't. Some believers (e.g. in fate) need not be theists, so some atheists could be believers.

Investigation 5: Hunt for valid forms

What to look for: Only 15 of the 256 forms are valid (5.9%), 24 if every term is assumed to name something. Most arguments that 'sound right' are not valid.

Investigation 6: The cosmological syllogism A level

What to look for: Valid (Barbara). But it only shows each thing in the universe has a cause; concluding that the universe as a whole has one cause is the fallacy of composition (Russell), and the cause need not be God.

Investigation 7: How strong are the premises? A level

What to look for: Five premises each with 10% doubt allow up to 50% doubt in the conclusion (and 59% belief if independent). A deductive argument can be valid yet weak if its premises are doubtful.

Investigation 8: Paley and Darwin A level

What to look for: Inductive arguments only make conclusions probable; if natural selection is a rival explanation, the probability of design drops sharply. This is why many see Darwin's work as the strongest challenge to Paley.

Batch experiment 1: How many syllogisms are valid?

About 6%: valid forms are rare. Medieval logicians named them (Barbara, Celarent, Darii, Ferio…) to remember them.
Theory (15 of 256): 5.86
Result from a test run: mean 5.71, SD 2.18, SE 0.154 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 2: With existential import (A level)

Nine more forms become valid (e.g. 'All S are M, all M are P, so some S are P'). Whether 'all unicorns…' implies unicorns exist matters for arguments about God's existence too.
Theory (24 of 256): 9.38
Result from a test run: mean 9.13, SD 2.84, SE 0.201 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 3: Long chains of premises

Each extra premise multiplies in more doubt: a five-step argument with 90% premises guarantees its conclusion only 59% of the time. Longer arguments (like the Third Way) need very secure premises.
Theory (100 × 0.9ⁿ): 1: 90 · 2: 81 · 3: 72.9 · 4: 65.6 · 5: 59 · 6: 53.1 · 8: 43
Result from a test run: 1: 90.5 ± 1.05 · 2: 80.9 ± 1.11 · 3: 74.1 ± 0.87 · 4: 68.8 ± 1.28 · 5: 57.6 ± 1.37 · 6: 54.2 ± 0.863 · 8: 44 ± 2.2 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Students judging validity (belief bias)

People judge by the conclusion, not the form. That is why philosophers set arguments out formally: a believable conclusion (e.g. 'God exists' to a believer, or 'God doesn't' to an atheist) can hide an invalid argument.
Theory (Evans et al. (1983)): 71
Result from a test run: mean 71.4, SD 7.31, SE 0.517 from 200 runs
