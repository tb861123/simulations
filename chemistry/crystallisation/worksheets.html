Crystallisation · Teacher notescrystallisation.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Granite or basalt?

What to look for: Slow cooling: few nuclei form, and each has a long time to grow: a few big crystals (granite, several mm). Fast cooling: the melt gets far below its freezing point quickly, so many nuclei form at once and none has time to grow big: many small crystals (basalt, under 1 mm).

Investigation 2: Obsidian: a glass

What to look for: The melt reaches 650 °C, where the atoms can hardly move, before more than a little has crystallised. The rest is frozen in a disordered arrangement: a glass. Lava quenched in water or air forms obsidian.

Investigation 3: Porphyry

What to look for: A few big crystals (formed slowly underground) in a fine-grained matrix (formed quickly after eruption): a porphyry. The two crystal sizes record two stages of cooling.

Investigation 4: Impurities

What to look for: Crystals start much more easily on a surface (a speck of dust, a scratch) than in the pure melt: each speck can start a crystal. More specks, more crystals, so smaller crystals. That is why you scratch a glass to start crystals, or add a seed crystal.

Investigation 5: Copper sulfate crystals

What to look for: Crystals appear once the solution is supersaturated: when the temperature falls below the point where the dissolved amount equals the solubility (the solubility curve). As crystals grow, the solution's concentration falls back towards the solubility.

Investigation 6: Salt by cooling?

What to look for: Salt's solubility barely changes with temperature (36 g at 20 °C, 38 g at 80 °C), so cooling only pushes out a little. Instead, evaporate the water: switch on evaporation and the crystals appear (as in salt pans).

Investigation 7: Seed crystal

What to look for: The seed grows without needing new nuclei, so you get one large crystal rather than many small ones. This is how big single crystals are grown.

Investigation 8: Recrystallisation yield A level

What to look for: At 20 °C only 20.7 g stays dissolved: 50 − 20.7 = 29.3 g crystallises (ignoring water of crystallisation). Some solid always stays dissolved in the cold solvent, so use the minimum of hot solvent and cool it well.

Batch experiment 1: Cooling rate and crystal size

A straight line on log–log axes, gradient about −0.26 (theory −0.30): cool ten times faster and the crystals are about half the size. The grid gives crystals somewhat bigger than the JMAK formula, because each new crystal takes up a whole cell at once and releases its latent heat a little early; the trend is the same.

Result from a test run: -2: 0.374 ± 0.0074 · -1.5: 0.299 ± 0.00651 · -1: 0.2 ± 8.67e-4 · -0.5: 0.057 ± 0.00556 · 0: -0.103 ± 0.00309 · 0.5: -0.242 ± 9.93e-5 · 1: -0.383 ± 2.77e-4 · 1.5: -0.532 ± 3.96e-4 (mean ± SE, 2 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Cooling rate and number of crystals

Faster cooling, many more crystals (gradient about +0.5; theory +0.6): the melt gets far below its freezing point before the crystals can release enough latent heat to warm it, and nuclei form readily there. The grid counts up to about half as many as the formula, for the reason given in the previous experiment.

Result from a test run: -2: 1.36 ± 0.0147 · -1.5: 1.51 ± 0.013 · -1: 1.7 ± 0.00165 · -0.5: 1.99 ± 0.0111 · 0: 2.31 ± 0.00614 · 0.5: 2.59 ± 2.15e-4 · 1: 2.87 ± 5.63e-4 · 1.5: 3.17 ± 7.93e-4 (mean ± SE, 2 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Impurities and the number of crystals

More specks, more crystals (and so smaller ones): nucleation is easier on a surface than in the bulk.

Batch experiment 4: Crystals from cooling copper sulfate

The colder you cool it, the more crystallises, following the solubility curve. Results sit a little below theory: some solid always stays on the filter paper or is lost when washing (a systematic error).
Theory (from the solubility curve): 0: 40.7 · 10: 37.5 · 20: 34.3 · 30: 30.4 · 40: 26.5 · 50: 20.8 · 60: 15 · 70: 7.5
Result from a test run: 0: 40.1 ± 0.182 · 10: 37 ± 0.154 · 20: 33.6 ± 0.233 · 30: 29.7 ± 0.116 · 40: 25.9 ± 0.26 · 50: 20.3 ± 0.212 · 60: 14.5 ± 0.195 · 70: 6.94 ± 0.306 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Which salts crystallise on cooling?

Alum and copper sulfate crystallise well on cooling (steep solubility curves); salt hardly at all (a flat curve), so salt is crystallised by evaporating the water.
Result from a test run: 1: 34.3 ± 0.0595 · 2: 2.18 ± 0.113 · 3: 65.1 ± 0.125 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)
