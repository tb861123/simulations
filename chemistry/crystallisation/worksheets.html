1 · Faster cooling, smaller crystals?

Faster cooling, smaller crystals?

Each run cools a fresh melt at one rate. On a log–log graph the crystal size falls in a straight-ish line: about ten times faster cooling makes crystals about 1.6 times smaller.

This is why granite (cooled over thousands of years underground) has crystals you can see, and basalt (a lava flow) does not. Slower than about 0.001 °C/hour the crystals become bigger than the slice, so the grid cannot show them.

…and more crystals?

4 · When does a melt freeze as glass?

When does a melt freeze as glass?

The melt is cooled instantly to a fixed temperature and held for a day, then frozen. Just below Tm almost no crystals start, so it stays liquid and then freezes as glass. Far below Tm the melt is too stiff for atoms to move: glass again. Only in between does it crystallise.

This U-shape is the "nose" of a time–temperature–transformation diagram. Obsidian forms when lava cools through the middle too fast to crystallise. These runs use a smaller 10 × 6.5 mm slice to be quick.

5 · Do impurities make more, smaller crystals?

Do impurities make more, smaller crystals?

Dust specks are places where crystals start easily (heterogeneous nucleation), so they add crystals. More crystals sharing the same space means each is smaller: switch Record to Mean crystal size.

The same idea is used to make ice cream smooth and chocolate glossy: control where crystals start.
