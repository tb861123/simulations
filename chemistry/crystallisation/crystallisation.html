How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Grid: a slice 20 mm × 13 mm, like a rock thin section, made of square cells 0.1, 0.067, 0.05 or 0.033 mm across (the "Picture detail" setting). All rates are per mm² and per second, so the physics is the same at every setting. Batch runs always use 0.1 mm cells so their results can be repeated exactly.
  • Magma freezes at Tm = 1100 °C (one freezing point; real magmas freeze over a range of temperatures as different minerals form). It becomes a glass below Tg = 650 °C, where atoms can no longer move.
  • Mobility M(T) = e−A/(T−T₀) (Vogel–Fulcher), with T₀ = 577 °C. It is 1 at Tm and about 10−6 at Tg: the melt gets far stickier as it cools.
  • Growth: each crystal grows outwards as a circle at G = 2×10−5 mm/s × M × ΔT/100, until it meets its neighbours. Where crystals meet, straight grain boundaries form.
  • Nucleation: new crystals start at random at I = 0.01 per mm² per s × M × (ΔT/100)2.5. A melt can be "supercooled" below Tm for a while before any crystals start.
  • Impurities are specks (a set number per mm²) where a crystal can start more easily: each speck starts a crystal at 0.003 per s × M × ΔT/100.
  • Latent heat: freezing all the melt gives out enough heat to warm it by 350 °C, so while crystals grow the temperature stays close to Tm (the plateau on the cooling curve). Above Tm crystal edges melt again.
  • Cooling: heat is lost at a steady rate (°C per hour). Real magma bodies cool fastest at first and slower later.
  • Theory (JMAK / Avrami): extended area Xext = Σ π r² over all crystals born, fraction crystallised X = 1 − e−Xext, and new crystals only start in melt: dN = I(1 − X)dt. For slow cooling this predicts crystal size ∝ (cooling rate)−0.2.
  • Solutions: each dissolved particle takes one random step to a neighbouring cell: every 2.5 s for 0.1 mm cells, less often for smaller cells (diffusion: time ∝ distance²). The chance of sticking per touch is scaled with the cell size in the same way. Touching a crystal, it sticks with the chance you set. Surface particles break off at a rate set so that the solution settles at the real solubility (copper sulfate 21 g per 100 g water at 20 °C, 55 g at 80 °C; salt about 36 g at any temperature). New crystals start more easily the more supersaturated the solution is.
  • Not modelled: different minerals with different freezing points (granite has quartz, feldspar and mica), crystal faces and shapes, convection in the magma or the solution, heat flowing through the slice (it is the same temperature everywhere), and water of crystallisation.
