How the model works (and what it leaves out)

pH and indicators

  • pH is worked out exactly from a charge balance (positive ions = negative ions), using Kw = 1.0×10⁻¹⁴ (25 °C), ethanoic acid Ka = 1.74×10⁻⁵ (pKa 4.76), ammonia Kb = 1.78×10⁻⁵, and HSO₄⁻ Ka = 1.0×10⁻². Concentrations are used in place of "activities", so very concentrated solutions (above about 0.1 mol/dm³) are a little off; real 1 mol/dm³ HCl has pH just below 0.
  • Everyday substances (lemon juice, sea water…) use typical measured pH values; real samples vary.
  • Indicators change colour over their real pH ranges (methyl orange 3.1–4.4, litmus 4.5–8.3, bromothymol blue 6.0–7.6, phenolphthalein 8.2–10.0). The colour is a smooth mix of the two forms; the end point is taken half-way through the change. Real eyes see the first permanent tint a little earlier or later. Universal indicator is drawn from a standard colour chart.

Titration

  • The pH curve is exact for the chosen acid and alkali. The temperature rise uses the heat of neutralisation (−57.9 kJ/mol for strong + strong, −56.1 with ethanoic acid, −53.4 with ammonia) and assumes no heat is lost and the solution behaves like water (4.18 J/g/°C).
  • In the batch, the burette is read to the nearest 0.05 cm³ after a small parallax error, the pipette delivers its volume ± a small error, and the last additions are drops of a set size, so the student overshoots the end point by part of a drop (on average half a drop). Real students also misjudge the colour change; that is not modelled separately.

Making salts

  • Amounts come from the balanced equation: the acid or the solid runs out first (the limiting reactant). Excess solid is used so all the acid is used up; if the solid is not in excess the salt is contaminated with acid.
  • Crystals: the solution is evaporated until it is saturated at the "crystallisation point" temperature, then cooled; the crystals are what no longer fits in the water (real solubility data, CRC Handbook). Each salt is treated as one hydrate at all temperatures (really zinc and magnesium sulfate change hydrate above about 40–60 °C). A few % of the crystals are lost on the glassware and filter paper. Real crystals hold a little water between them unless dried well.
  • Evaporating to dryness gives all the salt, but drives off the water of crystallisation (blue copper sulfate crystals turn to a white powder) and may decompose some salts, which is why crystallisation is used for hydrated salts.
  • Insoluble salts: a precipitate forms when its ions meet. Each solid has a real solubility (mol/dm³); that much stays dissolved, so "slightly soluble" solids such as calcium sulfate and calcium hydroxide only appear from concentrated solutions. Filtering loses about 2% of the solid. An unwashed solid keeps the dissolved salts in about 0.5 cm³ of liquid; an undried one keeps that water (about 0.5 g).

Tests lab

  • The same chemistry engine runs every tube: neutralisation, acid + carbonate → CO₂, acids dissolving hydroxide and carbonate precipitates, precipitation from the solubility rules, Al(OH)₃ and Zn(OH)₂ dissolving in excess alkali, and ammonia from NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ when warmed. So the order matters: barium chloride added to a carbonate before the acid gives a white precipitate (barium carbonate), and hydrochloric acid added before silver nitrate always gives white silver chloride.
  • Flame colours are drawn from each element's strongest emission lines (real wavelengths, NIST). Sodium is so bright that a trace of it hides other colours (try the contaminated wire); blue cobalt glass absorbs the yellow. Iron, aluminium, zinc, magnesium and ammonium compounds give no distinctive colour here.
  • Iron(II) hydroxide slowly turns brown at the surface as air oxidises it to iron(III) hydroxide ("Leave to stand").
  • Water: boiling point = 100 + 0.512 m and freezing point = −1.86 m (m = moles of dissolved particles per kg); fine for dilute solutions.
  • Left out: safety (silver nitrate stains, barium and lead salts are toxic, chlorine is poisonous), colours of very dilute solutions, and complex ions other than aluminate and zincate (copper with excess ammonia solution is not modelled).

A level: Kw, Ka and buffers

  • Buffer pH is found from the same exact charge balance as everything else, so it includes the dilution by what is added and the H⁺ and OH⁻ from water. The 9CH0 equation [H⁺] = Ka[HA]/[A⁻] (pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA])) is shown alongside; it agrees except for very dilute buffers or when one form is nearly used up. pKa values: ethanoic acid 4.76, ammonium ion 9.25 (data book, 25 °C).
  • The "Neutral at 50 °C" reading uses Kw = 5.47×10⁻¹⁴ (data book); everything else is at 25 °C.
  • CP9: the half-neutralised mixture has exactly [HA] = [A⁻]; the pH meter reading has a random error with SD 0.02 pH units (a calibrated meter). Real activities make measured pKa values a little lower than data-book values; the model ignores that.
