The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Is the titre proportional to the acid's concentration?

What this shows. moles of NaOH = moles of HCl, so titre = 25 cm³ × c(acid) ÷ 0.1 M: a straight line through the origin with gradient 250 cm³ per mol/dm³. The points sit on it to within about ±0.06 cm³: each titre is the difference of two burette readings, each read to the nearest 0.05 cm³, plus a small pipette error and up to one drop of overshoot. That is why titrations are so precise, and why the readings are recorded to 2 decimal places.

2 · How precise is one titre?

What this shows. The titres spread with an SD of about 0.06 cm³, as predicted from the reading, drop and pipette errors (the blue curve), so about 95% are within ±0.12 cm³ of the mean: two titres will usually be concordant. But the mean is about 0.03 cm³ ABOVE the true 25.00 cm³, and with 500 runs that is many standard errors: a systematic error. You always add the drop that changes the colour, so you overshoot by half a drop on average. Repeating does not remove a systematic error; smaller drops do.

3 · Which indicator for ethanoic acid?

What this shows. The true equivalence volume is 25.00 cm³ for every indicator (the yellow line). Methyl orange changes at pH 3.7, after only about 2.2 cm³: completely wrong, because a weak acid's pH starts high and rises gradually. Litmus (24.4), bromothymol blue (24.8) and universal indicator (24.9) are a little early. Only phenolphthalein, which changes at pH 8–10 on the steep part of the curve (equivalence pH 8.7), gives 25.0 cm³. The grey dashed line is where each indicator really changes.

4 · pH at equivalence vs acid strength

What this shows. At equivalence the flask holds only the salt NaA. The weaker the acid (bigger pKa), the more A⁻ ions take H⁺ back from water (A⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HA + OH⁻), so the more alkaline it is: from pH 7.4 (pKa 2) to 11.3 (pKa 10), rising by 0.5 per pKa unit. The textbook formula 7 + ½pKa + ½log(0.05) (dashed) matches the exact charge balance almost exactly. The scatter is the pH meter (±0.05). That is why a weak acid needs phenolphthalein: its equivalence point is alkaline.

5 · Weighing the sulfate test: mass of BaSO₄

What this shows. Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄. Up to 25 cm³ the barium chloride is the limiting reactant, so the mass rises in a straight line (0.0233 g per cm³); after that all 2.5 mmol of sulfate is used up and the mass stays at 2.5 mmol × 233.4 = 0.583 g. The weighed masses are about 2% below the theory because some fine solid passes through the filter or stays in the beaker (the dashed line), and scatter because the balance reads to 0.01 g. 6 · Copper sulfate crystals: heat for longer?

What this shows. 50 cm³ of 1 M sulfuric acid makes 0.05 mol of copper sulfate, at most 12.5 g of CuSO₄·5H₂O. Stopping at 40 °C leaves so much water that only about 30% crystallises on cooling to 20 °C; stopping at 100 °C (much less water left) gives about 80%. Some salt always stays in the cold saturated solution (20.7 g per 100 g of water), so the yield is never 100% by crystallising. Scatter: judging the crystallisation point (±3 °C) and losses on the glassware (3 ± 1.5%).
