Key ideas

atomic number Z = protons
mass number A = protons + neutrons
charge = protons − electrons
Ar = Σ(mass × % abundance) ÷ 100
Shells fill 2, 8, 8 for the first 20 elements. Losing electrons makes a positive ion; gaining electrons makes a negative ion. Isotopes have the same electrons, so the same chemistry, but different masses.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Shell (Bohr) model. Electrons are drawn on circular shells. Shells fill from the inside: 2, then 8, then 8, then the 4th shell (the iGCSE rule for elements 1–20). For copper and bromine the third shell holds up to 18, giving 2,8,18,1 and 2,8,18,7.
  • Real electrons do not orbit like planets. Quantum theory describes them as clouds (orbitals) where an electron is likely to be found. The circling is only a picture.
  • Not to scale. A real nucleus is about 10⁻¹⁴ m across and the atom about 10⁻¹⁰ m. In the picture the nucleus is a fifth of the atom's width, so it is drawn thousands of times too big. Nearly all of an atom is empty space, but nearly all its mass is in the nucleus.
  • The nucleus is drawn as a flat cluster of balls. Protons and neutrons are held together by the strong nuclear force (not shown). Whether a nucleus is stable is looked up from real data, not calculated.
  • Stable or not. A combination is "stable" if it is a natural isotope in the data; some famous radioactive isotopes (carbon-14, tritium, sodium-24…) are listed with their half-lives; other near misses are labelled radioactive; far-off combinations are marked as not existing.
  • Ions. You can add or remove up to 3 electrons. Only some ions turn up in real compounds (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Cl⁻, O²⁻…); the model warns when an ion is unusual. Electrons are lost from, or added to, the outer shell.
  • Isotope sample. Atoms are picked at random using the natural abundances (or your own mix of two isotopes). Weighing them one at a time builds up a mass spectrum; the average mass gets closer to Ar as more atoms are counted.
  • Relative atomic mass. Ar = Σ(m × abundance)/100. With mass numbers this is the exam method; with exact isotope masses it matches the data book to 2–3 decimal places. Isotope masses are a little less than the mass number (except ¹H) because energy (and so mass) is released when the nucleus forms.
  • Batch runs. Each run picks N atoms at random and works out their average mass. Theory: mean = Ar, SD = √(Σp(m − Ar)²/N), so the error shrinks as 1/√N.
  • A level box (Advanced). Electron configurations fill 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p, with copper's (and chromium's) 4s¹ exception, and positive ions losing 4s before 3d; orbitals fill singly before pairing (Hund's rule). Ionisation energies are NIST / data-book values (kJ mol⁻¹, rounded to 4 figures; values beyond IE₃ for Cu and Br are approximate). The mass spectrometer gives every ion the same kinetic energy (4.0×10⁻¹⁶ J) over a 1.50 m drift region, so t = d√(m/2KE); each run detects a random sample of 2000 (or 4000) ions, which is why the measured Ar scatters a little. Peaks use mass numbers, as in exams.
  • Left out: sub-shells (s, p, d) at Standard level, elements beyond calcium (apart from Cu and Br), the variation of natural abundances from place to place (so data books give ranges, e.g. H 1.00784–1.00811), and how a real mass spectrometer ionises and separates atoms.
