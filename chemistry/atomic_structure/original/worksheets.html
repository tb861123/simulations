Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Is chlorine's Ar really 35.5?

What this shows. Each run weighs 100 chlorine atoms picked at random. The averages form a bell-shaped spread around the theory value (35 × 75.76 + 37 × 24.24) ÷ 100 = 35.48. A sample of 100 atoms has, by chance, a few more or fewer chlorine-37 atoms than 24, so its average misses by about ±0.09. A real sample (a few grams) has about 10²² atoms, so its Ar is always 35.5 to many decimal places.

2 · Bigger samples, better answers?

What this shows. The error in the measured Ar shrinks as the sample gets bigger: the log–log graph is a straight line of gradient −½, so 100 times more atoms gives an error 10 times smaller (error ∝ 1/√N). This is why a real mass spectrometer, which counts millions of ions, gives very precise relative atomic masses.

3 · How does the mix set Ar?

What this shows. With no chlorine-37 every atom has mass 35, and with 100% it would be 37. In between, Ar rises in a straight line: Ar = 35 + 2 × (% chlorine-37)/100. Natural chlorine (24.24%) sits a quarter of the way along, at 35.5. Ar is a weighted average: it is nearer the mass of the more common isotope.

4 · Why is copper 63.5, not 63.6?

What this shows. With exact isotope masses (62.930 and 64.928) the average comes out at 63.55, the data-book value. With mass numbers (untick exact masses in the table) it is 63.62. Isotope masses are slightly less than their mass numbers because some mass is turned into the energy that holds the nucleus together.

5 · Magnesium: three isotopes

What this shows. Magnesium is 78.99% Mg-24, 10.00% Mg-25 and 11.01% Mg-26. The formula works for any number of isotopes: (24 × 78.99 + 25 × 10.00 + 26 × 11.01) ÷ 100 = 24.32. The runs scatter around it with an SD of about 0.02, as the theory curve predicts.

6 · How common is carbon-13?

What this shows. On average 1.07% of carbon atoms are carbon-13, so a sample of 1000 has about 11. But the number varies a lot from sample to sample (SD ≈ 3, so 0.33%): counting rare things gives a big relative error. The spread follows the binomial theory curve, SD = √(p(1 − p)/N).
