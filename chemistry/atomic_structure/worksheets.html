Atomic Structure and Isotopes · Teacher notesatomic-structure.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Build sodium

What to look for: 2,8,1: two electrons in the first shell, eight in the second, one in the third. Mass number = 11 + 12 = 23. Its symbol is ²³₁₁Na.

Investigation 2: Make an ion

What to look for: Na⁺: 11 protons (+11) and 10 electrons (−10): charge +1, configuration 2,8 (like neon). Cl⁻: 17 protons and 18 electrons: charge −1, configuration 2,8,8 (like argon). Ions have full outer shells.

Investigation 3: Isotopes of chlorine

What to look for: Both have 17 protons and 17 electrons (same chemistry); chlorine-37 has 2 more neutrons. Natural chlorine is about 75% Cl-35 and 25% Cl-37: Ar = (35 × 75 + 37 × 25) ÷ 100 = 35.5. No single atom has a mass of 35.5.

Investigation 4: Carbon-14

What to look for: Still carbon (6 protons), but carbon-14 is radioactive with a half-life of 5730 years; it is used for radiocarbon dating. Too many neutrons for its protons makes a nucleus unstable.

Investigation 5: Full shells

What to look for: Argon is 2,8,8: a full outer shell, so it does not need to gain, lose or share electrons. Potassium is 2,8,8,1: one electron in a new shell, easily lost, so it is very reactive.

Investigation 6: Copper's Ar

What to look for: Ar = (63 × 69.15 + 65 × 30.85) ÷ 100 = 63.6 with mass numbers; with the exact isotope masses it is 63.55, the data-book value. A bigger sample gives an average closer to Ar.

Investigation 7: s, p and d A level

What to look for: K: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s¹ (4s fills before 3d). Cu: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹, not 3d⁹ 4s²: a full 3d sub-shell is more stable (chromium does the same: 3d⁵ 4s¹).

Investigation 8: Transition metal ions A level

What to look for: 4s is emptied first, then 3d: Cu²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁹. Once the 3d orbitals are filled, 4s is higher in energy.

Investigation 9: Successive ionisation energies A level

What to look for: Three electrons come off fairly easily, then a big jump (the 4th comes from the second shell, much closer to the nucleus and less shielded), and another big jump before the last two. So aluminium is 2,8,3, in Group 3.

Investigation 10: First ionisation energy across a period A level

What to look for: Generally it rises (more protons, same shielding). Al dips: its outer electron is in 3p, higher in energy than 3s. S dips: its fourth 3p electron is paired, and the repulsion between the pair makes it easier to remove.

Batch experiment 1: Is chlorine's Ar really 35.5?

The averages scatter around 35.45 with a standard deviation of about 0.09: chance decides how many of each isotope are in a sample of 100.
Theory (Ar): 35.5
Result from a test run: mean 35.5, SD 0.0885, SE 0.00396 from 500 runs

Batch experiment 2: Bigger samples, better answers?

The error falls as 1 ÷ √N: 100 times more atoms, 10 times more precise. A real mass spectrometer counts millions of ions.
Theory (∝ 1 ÷ √N): 10: 0.216 · 30: 0.125 · 100: 0.0683 · 300: 0.0394 · 1000: 0.0216 · 3000: 0.0125
Result from a test run: 10: 0.297 ± 0.0539 · 30: 0.123 ± 0.0183 · 100: 0.0843 ± 0.0155 · 300: 0.0498 ± 0.00687 · 1000: 0.0196 ± 0.00305 · 3000: 0.0128 ± 0.00244 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: How does the mix set Ar?

A straight line from 34.97 (all ³⁵Cl) to 36.97 (all ³⁷Cl): Ar is a weighted average. Natural chlorine (24% ³⁷Cl) sits a quarter of the way along.
Theory (weighted average): 0: 35 · 20: 35.4 · 40: 35.8 · 60: 36.2 · 80: 36.6 · 100: 37
Result from a test run: 0: 35 ± 0 · 20: 35.4 ± 0.0096 · 40: 35.8 ± 0.0107 · 60: 36.1 ± 0.00756 · 80: 36.6 ± 0.0183 · 100: 37 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Why is copper 63.5, not 63.6?

Exact masses give 63.55 (the data-book value); mass numbers give 63.62. Real isotope masses are not whole numbers (except carbon-12, by definition), because of the mass defect.
Result from a test run: 1: 63.5 ± 0.00389 · 2: 63.6 ± 0.00271 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Magnesium: three isotopes

Yes: Ar = Σ(mass × abundance) ÷ 100 for any number of isotopes.
Theory (Ar): 24.3
Result from a test run: mean 24.3, SD 0.0224, SE 0.00129 from 300 runs

Batch experiment 6: How common is carbon-13?

Rare isotopes scatter a lot (about ±0.3% around 1.07%): with only about 11 carbon-13 atoms per sample, chance matters. To measure a rare isotope precisely you need a very large sample.
Theory (natural abundance): 1.07
Result from a test run: mean 1.08, SD 0.314, SE 0.0181 from 300 runs
