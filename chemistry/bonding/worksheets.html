In a lattice of particles held by bonds, how does the melting temperature depend on the strength of each bond?
Each run: 144 particles on a grid, each held by bonds to its four neighbours. The temperature is raised steadily; a particle breaks free with a chance that falls steeply with the energy holding it. The result is the temperature at which half are free.

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Giving electrons away

What to look for: Sodium (2,8,1) gives its single outer electron to chlorine (2,8,7). Both end up with a full outer shell, like a noble gas: Na⁺ is 2,8 and Cl⁻ is 2,8,8. The ions have opposite charges and attract strongly in all directions, building a giant lattice. For magnesium oxide, two electrons move, giving Mg²⁺ and O²⁻.

Investigation 2: Why salt shatters

What to look for: Solid salt does not conduct: its ions are locked in place. The hammer pushes one layer along by one ion, so positive meets positive and negative meets negative: the layers repel and the crystal splits. Ionic compounds are hard but brittle.

Investigation 3: Molten salt conducts

What to look for: When molten, the ions are free to move: positive Na⁺ ions drift to the negative electrode and negative Cl⁻ ions to the positive one, carrying the current. The bulb lights. Dissolving salt in water frees the ions too. This is why electrolysis needs molten or dissolved compounds.

Investigation 4: Sharing electrons

What to look for: Each covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons: one from each atom. Oxygen shares two pairs and keeps two lone pairs, so it has 8 in its outer shell; each hydrogen has 2. Carbon dioxide has two double bonds (two shared pairs each), and nitrogen a triple bond.

Investigation 5: Why water boils at 100 °C

What to look for: No: the molecules stay whole (H₂O in the gas is still H₂O). Boiling only separates molecules, which are held together by weak forces between them, not by the strong covalent bonds inside them. Small molecules have weaker intermolecular forces, so methane boils far below room temperature. Water's forces are unusually strong (hydrogen bonds), which is why it is liquid at room temperature.

Investigation 6: Diamond and graphite

What to look for: Both are giant covalent structures with very high melting points: every atom is held by strong covalent bonds. In diamond each carbon bonds to four others in a rigid 3-D network: the hardest natural substance, and no free electrons, so no conduction. In graphite each carbon bonds to three others in flat layers, leaving one electron per atom free to move along the layers: it conducts, and the layers slide (pencil 'lead').

Investigation 7: A sea of electrons

What to look for: Metals are positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons. The electrons drift through the lattice and carry the current, even in the solid. Hit it and the layers of ions slide over each other but the electron sea keeps holding them together, so the metal bends instead of shattering: metals are malleable and ductile.

Investigation 8: Melting points compared

What to look for: Diamond (3550 °C: millions of strong covalent bonds must break) > copper (1085 °C: strong metallic bonding) > sodium chloride (801 °C: strong ionic bonds in a giant lattice) > water (0 °C: only weak intermolecular forces between molecules). Giant structures melt high; simple molecules melt low.

Investigation 9: Stronger forces, higher boiling points A level

What to look for: London (instantaneous dipole–induced dipole) forces grow with the number of electrons, because a larger electron cloud is more easily distorted. Longer molecules also have more surface in contact. So boiling points rise steadily with relative molecular mass, from methane (−162 °C) to octane (126 °C).

Batch experiment 1: Bond strength and melting

A straight line through the origin: double the bond strength and the melting point doubles. This is why giant structures, where melting breaks strong bonds, melt so much higher than simple molecules, where it only overcomes weak forces between molecules.
Result from a test run: 1: 2.08 ± 0.0514 · 2: 4.24 ± 0.0383 · 3: 6.68 ± 0.208 · 4: 8.41 ± 0.264 · 5: 11 ± 0.367 · 6: 13.1 ± 0.38 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Ionic charge and melting point

Larger charges attract more strongly, so more energy is needed to separate the ions. The spread within each group comes from the different sizes of the ions: small ions get closer and attract more strongly.
Result from a test run: 1: 784 ± 36.9 · 2: 923 ± 92.1 · 4: 2560 ± 51.3 · 6: 2290 ± 71.5 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Boiling points of alkanes

Bigger molecules have more electrons and more surface in contact, so the forces between molecules are stronger and more energy is needed to separate them.
Result from a test run: 16: -162 ± 0.356 · 30: -89.3 ± 0.496 · 44: -40.9 ± 1.1 · 58: 0.356 ± 0.88 · 72: 35.7 ± 1.47 · 86: 68.4 ± 1.15 · 100: 98.7 ± 0.876 · 114: 126 ± 1.31 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Noble gases

Noble gases are single atoms, so the only forces between them are London forces, which grow with the number of electrons.
