Key ideas

Ionic bond: strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
Covalent bond: a shared pair of electrons, attracted to both nuclei.
Metallic bond: attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons.
To conduct, a substance needs charged particles that can move: ions (molten or dissolved) or delocalised electrons.
Melting a giant structure breaks strong bonds; melting a simple molecular substance only overcomes weak forces between molecules.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Melting and boiling points are data, not predicted. The model uses data-book values at 1 atm and holds the temperature still while the substance melts or boils until the enthalpy of fusion (or vaporisation) has been supplied.
  • Heat capacities are typical values, the same for solid, liquid and gas, and no heat is lost to the surroundings. Real heating curves are less sharp because heat leaks away and thermometers lag.
  • Conductivity test: 6 V 0.5 A bulb (12 Ω) in series with the sample, electrodes 1 cm apart. Conductivities: metals from σ₂₀ and their temperature coefficient α (a straight-line rule that is good to about ±5% up to 500 °C); molten metals conduct about half as well as just below their melting point; molten salts use measured values for NaCl, KBr and CaCl₂ and a typical 3 S/cm for the others; solutions use Λ = Λ₀ − K√c/(1 + √c), which matches real NaCl data within about 2% up to 1 mol/dm³ (solutions are at 25 °C).
  • Non-conductors are set to exactly zero. Really, solid salt and pure water conduct a tiny amount (about a millionth of a solution), far too little to light a bulb.
  • The particle picture is a 2-D sketch. Real lattices are 3-D (the 3-D view shows the true NaCl, CaF₂-type, diamond, graphite and SiO₂ arrangements). Formulas such as CaCl₂ and Al₂O₃ are drawn with the right ratio of ions, not the real arrangement. Particles are drawn far apart in a gas to show it fills the box; really a gas would spread much further.
  • Ions that reach an electrode go back into the liquid at the other side, to keep the picture going. Really they are discharged there (electrolysis: see the Electrolysis simulation).
  • Pushing: metal layers and graphite layers slide (malleable / soft); in an ionic solid a shift of half an ion lines up like charges, which repel, and the crystal cracks (brittle); diamond and silicon dioxide barely move, then shatter. This is a picture of the explanation, not a calculation of forces.
  • Dot-and-cross diagrams show electrons as dots and crosses only to show where they came from: all electrons are identical. Shells are circles; real electrons are in orbitals.
  • Batch runs: a heating-curve experiment (a thermometer read every few seconds with a random error; the "student" finds the flat part of the curve), and a conductivity test whose meter reading flickers because the charges move about randomly (the flicker shrinks with a longer measuring time).
  • Left out: polar bonds and hydrogen bonding (why water boils so high for its size), metals' different crystal structures, impurities (which lower and spread a melting point), pressure (CO₂ only melts above 5.1 atm), and diamond turning into graphite when strongly heated.
