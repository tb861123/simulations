Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Does solid salt conduct?

What this shows. The current is zero all the way up to 800 °C: in solid salt the ions are locked in the lattice and cannot move. Above the melting point (801 °C) the ions are free to move and the bulb lights (about 0.49 A, nearly the full 0.5 A, because molten salt conducts far better than the bulb's filament). The theory line jumps between the 800 °C and 900 °C points because the melting point lies between them.

2 · Find the melting point of salt

What this shows. Each run heats 0.1 mol of salt at 20 W and reads the thermometer every 2 s with a random error (SD 1 °C). The temperature stays flat for about 140 s while the salt melts, giving about 70 readings, so their average is much more precise than one reading: the results scatter by only about 0.12 °C around 801 °C. A flat region on a heating curve is how you spot a melting point.

3 · Bigger molecules, higher boiling point?

What this shows. Each extra CH₂ group makes the molecule bigger, with more electrons, so the forces between molecules get stronger and more energy is needed to separate them. The boiling point rises from −162 °C (methane, a gas) to 174 °C (decane, a liquid). The rise slows down for longer chains. This is why crude oil can be separated by fractional distillation.

4 · Do metals conduct worse when hot?

What this shows. Copper's conductivity falls from about 6.5 × 10⁷ S/m at 0 °C to about 2.1 × 10⁷ S/m at 500 °C: the hotter ions vibrate more and scatter the delocalised electrons. The theory line is σ = σ₂₀/(1 + α(T − 20)) with α = 0.0039 per °C. Even red-hot copper conducts millions of times better than molten salt.

5 · More ions, more conduction?

What this shows. On log–log axes the points lie close to a line of gradient 1: ten times more salt, about ten times more ions, about ten times the conductivity. The dashed line assumes each ion carries charge with no interference. In stronger solutions the ions get in each other's way (each ion is surrounded by oppositely charged ions), so real solutions fall a little below it: 1 mol/dm³ conducts about 30% less than the ideal.

6 · How long does ice take to melt?

What this shows. Melting 0.1 mol of ice needs 0.1 × 6.01 kJ = 601 J whatever the heater, so the time at 0 °C is t = 601 J ÷ P. Double the power, half the time: a log–log gradient of −1. Results are rounded to whole readings (1 s apart), which is why the fastest melt (about 7.5 s) scatters most.
