How the model works (and what it leaves out)

The data

  • Real data (CRC Handbook and standard data tables): melting and boiling points, densities at 20 °C, metallic radii (Group 1), covalent radii (Group 7), first ionisation energies, electronegativities and standard electrode potentials E°. Francium and astatine are so radioactive that only tiny amounts have ever existed; their values marked "estimated" are predictions (their first ionisation energies have been measured).

Group 1 with water

  • The piece is a ball of the chosen mass whose radius shrinks at a steady speed v while it reacts, so it vanishes after t = r₀ / v. Real pieces are cut by hand, are not round, and sodium and potassium melt into balls; the batch adds random variation to the mass and the rate to stand for this.
  • The speeds v (lithium 0.05, sodium 0.15, potassium 0.6, rubidium 2, caesium 5 mm/s at 20 °C) are chosen so that a 50 mg piece takes about as long as in a school demonstration (lithium about a minute, sodium about 15 s, potassium about 4 s). They are not measured values. Warmer water speeds them up: v ∝ e^(−Ea/RT) with Ea = 30 kJ/mol, using the starting temperature (the small warming during the reaction is left out of the rate).
  • Why the trend? The model does not calculate the speed from the atom. Real reaction speed depends on how easily the outer electron is lost (ionisation energy, which falls down the group), on melting (a liquid ball has a fresh surface) and on the heat released. Surprisingly, lithium releases the most energy per mole (222 kJ) but reacts the slowest: how fast a reaction goes is not set by how much energy it releases.
  • Hydrogen: 2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂, so every 2 moles of metal make 1 mole of H₂, which takes up 24 000 cm³ at room temperature and pressure. "Gas lost" stands for hydrogen that escapes before it is collected.
  • Temperature rise: ΔT = n|ΔH| × (heat kept) ÷ (mass of water × 4.18 J/g/°C). ΔH per mole of metal (kJ/mol) from enthalpies of formation: Li −222, Na −184, K −197, Rb −195, Cs −203. Heat escaping to the air and the container is set by "heat kept in the water". Some of the energy really goes into burning the hydrogen in the air (potassium's flame), which this model leaves out.
  • pH = 14 + log₁₀[OH⁻] at 25 °C, with [OH⁻] = moles of metal ÷ volume of water (each M makes one OH⁻). The indicator colour follows the universal indicator chart.
  • Floating and sinking: lithium, sodium and potassium are less dense than water and float; rubidium and caesium are denser and sink (the old picture of rubidium floating was wrong). Caesium reacts so fast that it explodes. Flames: potassium's hydrogen catches fire with a lilac flame (potassium's flame-test colour).

Group 7

  • States come from comparing the temperature with the real melting and boiling points at 1 atmosphere. The particles in the jars are drawn molecules: real ones are far smaller and faster. The colours are the real colours; bromine and iodine give off some vapour even below their boiling points.
  • Displacement: X₂ + 2Y⁻ ⇌ 2X⁻ + Y₂. How far it goes is worked out from the equilibrium constant K = e^(2FΔE°/RT), with E° = +1.36 (Cl₂), +1.09 (Br₂), +0.54 V (I₂). When the added halogen is more reactive, K is about 10⁹ or more, so it goes to completion; when it is less reactive, K is tiny, so less than 1% reacts: "no reaction". The batch colorimeter and pipettes add random measuring errors. Iodine joining iodide ions (I₃⁻, which makes the brown colour) is left out of the sums.
  • The particle picture: halogen molecules and halide ions wander at random; when a halogen molecule meets a halide ion of a less reactive halogen it takes electrons from two of them. Potassium ions are spectator ions and do nothing. Water molecules are not drawn.
  • A level box (Advanced). Group 2 data: ionisation energies and metallic radii from data tables; solubilities are approximate values at about 20 °C (they differ between sources; the trends are what matter). Thermal stability: the times are illustrative, following the order of approximate decomposition temperatures (MgCO₃ 540, CaCO₃ 900, SrCO₃ 1290, BaCO₃ 1360 °C; Group 2 nitrates from about 330 to 630 °C) with ±12% variation between runs; over 10 minutes counts as "no change". Charge ÷ radius uses Shannon ionic radii and is only a guide to polarising power. Flame tests: the main visible emission lines (nm) from line tables; calcium, strontium and barium colours come partly from molecular bands. The unknown uses soluble salts only, and the tests give the textbook results (no contamination by sodium, no faint precipitates).
  • Left out: fluorine reacts with water (so is not used in the test tubes); reactions of halogens with metals (sodium burns in chlorine to make sodium chloride) and with hydrogen; halogen hydrides; the silver nitrate test for halide ions.
