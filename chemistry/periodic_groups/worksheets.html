Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · More reactive down Group 1?

What this shows. The same mass of each metal disappears faster and faster down the group: lithium about 56 s, sodium 15 s, potassium 4 s, rubidium 1 s and caesium about a third of a second (note the log scale: each step down is several times faster). The outer electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded each time, so it is lost more easily. The points scatter by about 10% because each hand-cut piece has a slightly different mass, shape and surface. The theory line is the model's own prediction for a perfect round piece; the rates were chosen to match classroom demonstrations, not measured.

2 · Which metal makes most hydrogen per gram?

What this shows. Every mole of alkali metal makes half a mole of hydrogen (2M → H₂), so the same MASS of a lighter atom makes more gas: 50 mg of lithium (Ar 6.94) gives about 86 cm³, sodium 26 cm³, potassium 15 cm³, rubidium 7 cm³ and caesium only 4.5 cm³. The theory is ½ × (m ÷ Ar) × 24 000 cm³. The scatter comes from the mass of each piece (±10%) and reading the syringe (±1 cm³), not from the chemistry: the amount of gas does not depend on how reactive the metal is.

3 · More sodium, bigger temperature rise?

What this shows. The temperature rise is proportional to the mass of sodium: a straight line through the origin. Each mole of sodium releases 184 kJ, so 200 mg (8.7 mmol) releases 1.6 kJ, enough to warm 100 g of water by 3.8 °C if none escaped (dashed line). With only 70% of the heat kept (some warms the trough and the air, and some burns the hydrogen) the rise is lower, but still proportional. The thermometer's ±0.2 °C errors matter most for small pieces, where the rise is tiny.

4 · How alkaline does the water get?

What this shows. Every sodium atom makes one hydroxide ion, so the hydroxide concentration is proportional to the mass: 50 mg in 100 cm³ gives 0.022 mol/dm³ and pH 12.3. pH is a log scale, so each TEN times more sodium raises the pH by only 1: a straight line on this log axis, from pH 11.6 (10 mg) to 12.9 (200 mg). It never passes 14 for sensible amounts. The scatter is the pH meter (±0.05) and the mass of each piece.

5 · How much bromine can chlorine make?

What this shows. Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂: each chlorine molecule makes one bromine molecule, so the bromine rises in a straight line as chlorine water is added (0.05 mmol per cm³) until all 0.5 mmol of bromide ions are used up at 5 cm³ (0.25 mmol of Br₂). After that the chlorine is in excess, the bromide is the limiting reactant, and adding more chlorine makes no more bromine. The equilibrium constant (about 10⁹) is so large that the reaction goes to completion. Scatter: pipettes ±1%, colorimeter ±3%.

6 · Which halides can bromine displace?

What this shows. Bromine reacts completely with iodide ions (about 100%: K ≈ 10¹⁸) but less than 0.1% reacts with chloride ions (K ≈ 10⁻⁹): bromine is more reactive than iodine but less reactive than chlorine. With bromide ions nothing changes. Real reactions are not all-or-nothing: the equilibrium constants from the electrode potentials just happen to be enormous or tiny for these pairs, which is why the test tubes give such clear results. Try halogen 1 (chlorine) or 3 (iodine) in the table.
