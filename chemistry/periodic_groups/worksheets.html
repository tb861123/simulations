Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Down Group 1

What to look for: Lithium fizzes steadily and floats; sodium melts into a ball and whizzes about; potassium melts, fizzes violently and the hydrogen catches fire with a lilac flame. Reactivity increases down the group. All make hydrogen and an alkaline solution (the indicator turns purple).

Investigation 2: Float or sink?

What to look for: Lithium, sodium and potassium are less dense than water (under 1 g/cm³), so they float. Rubidium (1.53) and caesium (1.93) are denser, so they sink, and react even more violently: caesium explodes.

Investigation 3: How much hydrogen?

What to look for: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂. Moles of Na = 0.050 ÷ 23 = 0.00217; moles of H₂ = half = 0.00109; volume = 0.00109 × 24 000 = 26 cm³.

Investigation 4: Why more reactive?

What to look for: Potassium (2,8,8,1) has one more shell than sodium (2,8,1). Its outer electron is further from the nucleus and shielded by more inner electrons, so it is attracted less strongly and lost more easily.

Investigation 5: Halogens at room temperature

What to look for: Fluorine: pale yellow gas. Chlorine: yellow-green gas. Bromine: red-brown liquid (with orange vapour). Iodine: grey-black solid (purple vapour when heated). Melting and boiling points rise down the group: bigger molecules, stronger forces between them.

Investigation 6: Displacement

What to look for: Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂: the solution turns orange. Br₂ + 2KI → 2KBr + I₂: it turns brown. Iodine cannot displace chlorine (no change): a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive one. Order: Cl > Br > I.

Investigation 7: Predict francium and astatine

What to look for: Francium: even more reactive than caesium (it would explode), dense, low melting point. Astatine: a black solid at room temperature (melting point above iodine's), the least reactive halogen. Both are so radioactive that hardly any has ever been seen.

Investigation 8: Redox in displacement A level

What to look for: Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻ (reduced: oxidation number 0 → −1). 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ (oxidised: −1 → 0). Chlorine is the oxidising agent. E°(Cl₂) = +1.36 V > E°(Br₂) = +1.09 V, so the reaction is feasible.

Investigation 9: Ionisation energies down a group A level

What to look for: They all fall down the group: more shells, more shielding and a larger radius outweigh the extra protons, so the outer electron is held less tightly.

Batch experiment 1: More reactive down Group 1?

Each metal reacts several times faster than the one above it: reactivity increases down the group.
Result from a test run: 1: 57.1 ± 2.48 · 2: 15.1 ± 0.811 · 3: 4.04 ± 0.2 · 4: 1.03 ± 0.0329 · 5: 0.406 ± 0.0292 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Which metal makes most hydrogen per gram?

Lithium makes the most per gram (86 cm³ per 50 mg): its atoms are lightest, so 50 mg contains the most atoms. The most reactive metal (caesium) makes the least gas per gram.
Result from a test run: 1: 86.5 ± 3.44 · 2: 26.1 ± 1.23 · 3: 16.2 ± 0.514 · 4: 7.15 ± 0.287 · 5: 4.1 ± 0.313 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: More sodium, bigger temperature rise?

A straight line through the origin: the energy released is proportional to the amount of sodium.
Theory (theory): 20: 0.268 · 50: 0.67 · 80: 1.07 · 110: 1.47 · 140: 1.88 · 170: 2.28 · 200: 2.68
Result from a test run: 20: 0.273 ± 0.0488 · 50: 0.716 ± 0.0383 · 80: 1.08 ± 0.0208 · 110: 1.48 ± 0.0716 · 140: 1.81 ± 0.0993 · 170: 2.25 ± 0.0363 · 200: 2.71 ± 0.0154 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: How alkaline does the water get?

Strongly alkaline (pH about 12 to 13). Ten times the sodium raises the pH by 1: pH is a log scale.
Theory (14 + log₁₀[OH⁻]): 10: 11.6 · 20: 11.9 · 50: 12.3 · 100: 12.6 · 150: 12.8 · 200: 12.9
Result from a test run: 10: 11.6 ± 0.0113 · 20: 11.9 ± 0.00914 · 50: 12.3 ± 0.00612 · 100: 12.6 ± 0.0083 · 150: 12.8 ± 0.0104 · 200: 13 ± 0.0108 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: How much bromine can chlorine make?

The amount rises in proportion to the chlorine added, then levels off at 0.25 mmol once all the bromide has reacted (2Br⁻ → Br₂).
Theory (limiting reactant): 1: 0.05 · 2: 0.1 · 3: 0.15 · 4: 0.2 · 5: 0.25 · 6: 0.25 · 8: 0.25 · 10: 0.25
Result from a test run: 1: 0.0499 ± 1.57e-4 · 2: 0.0995 ± 5.84e-4 · 3: 0.151 ± 0.00227 · 4: 0.204 ± 0.00205 · 5: 0.252 ± 0.00334 · 6: 0.25 ± 0.00375 · 8: 0.251 ± 0.00285 · 10: 0.252 ± 0.0043 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: Which halides can bromine displace?

Bromine displaces iodine completely (100%), cannot displace chlorine (almost 0%), and of course does nothing with bromide.
Result from a test run: 1: 0.128 ± 0.102 · 2: 0 ± 0 · 3: 99.8 ± 0.149 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)
