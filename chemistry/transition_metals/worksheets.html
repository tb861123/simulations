Transition Metals and Colour · Teacher notestransition-metals.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Test for Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺

What to look for: Cu²⁺: pale blue precipitate (copper(II) hydroxide). Fe²⁺: green precipitate (iron(II) hydroxide). Fe³⁺: orange-brown precipitate (iron(III) hydroxide). The colour identifies the ion.

Investigation 2: Iron(II) goes rusty

What to look for: It turns orange-brown at the surface: oxygen oxidises iron(II) to iron(III). Test iron(II) quickly, or the result looks like iron(III).

Investigation 3: Coloured or colourless?

What to look for: Zn²⁺ is d¹⁰: every d orbital is full, so no electron can jump to a higher one and no visible light is absorbed. Cu²⁺ (d⁹) has a gap in the upper set, so it absorbs red-orange light and looks blue.

Investigation 4: Ammonia: base, then ligand A level

What to look for: First NH₃ acts as a base, removing H⁺ from water ligands: Cu(OH)₂(H₂O)₄ precipitates. In excess it replaces four water ligands (ligand exchange): deep blue [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ dissolves. Stronger-field NH₃ raises ΔE, so the absorbed light moves to shorter wavelength (600 nm).

Investigation 5: Chloride changes the shape A level

What to look for: Cl⁻ ions are larger than H₂O molecules, so only four fit: tetrahedral [CuCl₄]²⁻, yellow-green. It is an equilibrium: adding water moves it back to the blue aqua ion.

Investigation 6: Amphoteric hydroxides A level

What to look for: Cr(OH)₃ and Zn(OH)₂ are amphoteric: they react with excess OH⁻ to form soluble [Cr(OH)₆]³⁻ (dark green) and [Zn(OH)₄]²⁻ (colourless). Cu, Fe, Ni, Co and Mn hydroxides do not.

Investigation 7: Vanadium's rainbow A level

What to look for: VO₂⁺ yellow (+5) → VO²⁺ blue (+4) (passing through green, a mixture) → V³⁺ green (+3) → V²⁺ violet (+2). Zinc is a strong enough reducing agent to reach +2. Left in air, V²⁺ is oxidised back to green V³⁺.

Investigation 8: Why colour? A level

What to look for: NH₃ splits the d orbitals more than H₂O: bigger ΔE, shorter λ absorbed (800 → 600 nm), deeper blue. Tetrahedral complexes split less (about 4/9 as much), so [CuCl₄]²⁻ absorbs in the infrared; its yellow comes from a charge-transfer band in the violet.

Investigation 9: The colorimeter A level

What to look for: Choose the filter of the colour the solution ABSORBS (complementary to the colour you see): for deep blue copper–ammonia, orange or yellow. The steeper the calibration line, the more precisely you can read the unknown.

Investigation 10: A homogeneous catalyst A level

What to look for: S₂O₈²⁻ and I⁻ are both negative, so they repel: high Ea. Fe²⁺ is oxidised by S₂O₈²⁻ to Fe³⁺, which oxidises I⁻ back to I₂ and is reduced to Fe²⁺: two steps between oppositely charged ions. Iron can do this because it has variable oxidation states.

Investigation 11: Autocatalysis A level

What to look for: Mn²⁺ is a product and also the catalyst, so the reaction speeds itself up (autocatalysis): an S-shaped curve. Adding Mn²⁺ at the start removes the slow beginning.

Batch experiment 1: Beer–Lambert: absorbance against concentration

A straight line through the origin: A = εcl. (Bands are wide, so a filter sees almost a single ε.)

Batch experiment 2: Which filter?

Violet and blue light mostly pass through a blue solution, so the calibration line is shallow and the same meter error becomes a concentration error several times larger. Use the complementary colour (orange or yellow).
