Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Simple cells: reactivity and voltage

What this shows. Magnesium, zinc, aluminium, iron and lead, each paired with copper. The further the metal is from copper in the reactivity series, the bigger the voltage, with one exception: aluminium gives less than zinc, because its oxide layer stops it behaving as reactively as its place suggests. Every reading is well below the difference in E° (grey dashed line): simple cells are not standard cells; each metal sits at its own corrosion potential in salt water. Readings scatter by about 0.04 V because surfaces differ.

3 · CP10: electrode potentials via a copper reference

What this shows. Each metal's half-cell is measured against a copper half-cell, then E(metal) = E(Cu) + reading. Most land on the data-book line. Magnesium is about 0.7 V too positive: it reacts with the water, so the electrode never reaches equilibrium with its ions (a mixed potential). E° values for very reactive metals are worked out indirectly (from thermochemical data), not measured in water.

4 · Concentration and EMF

What this shows. Diluting the copper(II) ions pulls Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu to the left, so the copper electrode's potential becomes less positive and the cell EMF falls. On a log scale it is a straight line: 0.030 V less for each tenfold dilution (0.059 ÷ 2 at 25 °C, the Nernst equation, which is beyond 9CH0). This is why E° values only predict reactions under standard conditions.

5 · Why a high-resistance voltmeter?

What this shows. The cell has an internal resistance of about 2 kΩ (mostly the salt bridge). A meter with low resistance lets current flow, so some of the EMF is 'lost' inside the cell and the reading is low: with 1 kΩ it reads only a third. Above about 1 MΩ the current is negligible and the meter reads the true EMF. That is why CP10 asks for a high-resistance voltmeter.

6 · Fuel cell: voltage falls as current rises

What this shows. Even at tiny currents the voltage is below 1.23 V (slow electrode reactions, especially at the oxygen electrode). It then falls gently as resistance losses grow in proportion to the current, and steeply near the top when the gases cannot reach the electrodes fast enough. Power = V × I peaks before the steep drop; cells are run below that, where the efficiency is higher.

8 · Displacement: how hot does it get?

What this shows. The further the metal is above copper, the more energy is released per mole of copper displaced (magnesium about 530 kJ, zinc 220 kJ, iron 150 kJ), so the bigger the temperature rise. Measured rises are below the yellow 'no heat lost' line: the cup loses heat while the reaction runs. Iron reacts slowly, so it loses the most: its rise is much smaller than its ΔH suggests.
