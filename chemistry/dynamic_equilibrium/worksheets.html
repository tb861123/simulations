Dynamic Equilibrium · Teacher notesdynamic-equilibrium.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: What does 'dynamic' mean?

What to look for: The flashes never stop. At equilibrium the forward and reverse rates are equal (and not zero), so the amounts stay constant even though particles keep reacting.

Investigation 2: Start from the other side

What to look for: Yes, with the same total atoms it reaches the same equilibrium from either direction. Compare the Q tile with the first investigation: Q settles at the same K.

Investigation 3: Add more A

What to look for: Extra A means more A–B collisions, so the forward rate jumps above the reverse rate. B falls and AB rises until the rates match again. Q dips below K, then climbs back to it.

Investigation 4: Squeeze it

What to look for: A + B (2 particles) ⇌ AB (1 particle). Compressing raises every concentration, which speeds up the forward (two-particle) step more than the reverse (one-particle) step, so more AB forms: the side with fewer particles.

Investigation 5: Heat an exothermic reaction

What to look for: Both rates rise, so equilibrium is reached sooner. The reverse step has the bigger activation energy, so it gains more from heating: the yield of AB falls. This is the Haber-process compromise.

Investigation 6: Make it endothermic

What to look for: Now the forward step is endothermic and gains more from heating, so the yield of AB rises with temperature.

Investigation 7: Kc and its units A level

What to look for: Kc = [AB] ÷ ([A][B]). Units: concentration ÷ (concentration × concentration) = concentration⁻¹ (here 'per particle per 10 000 square units'; for a real reaction, dm³ mol⁻¹). Q fluctuates around Kc because the box is small.

Investigation 8: Kp: squeeze and see A level

What to look for: The mole fraction of AB rises (equilibrium moves to fewer particles), but Kp = p(AB) ÷ (p(A) × p(B)) stays the same (within the noise): changing pressure moves the position, not the constant. Only temperature changes K.

Investigation 9: Only temperature changes K A level

What to look for: Concentration, pressure and catalyst changes all leave Kc unchanged once equilibrium returns. Raising the temperature lowers Kc for this exothermic reaction: ln K = −ΔH/RT + ΔS/R.

Investigation 10: A catalyst

What to look for: Only the time. K depends on the difference Ea(f) − Ea(r) = ΔH, which a catalyst does not change.

Batch experiment 1: Is K really constant?

Only a hundred or so particles are in the box, so the numbers of A, B and AB fluctuate all the time. Real samples contain ~10²³ particles and the fluctuations are far too small to see. A flat best-fit line means K does not depend on the starting mixture.
Result from a test run: 0: 0.329 ± 0.0386 · 14: 0.388 ± 0.021 · 28: 0.332 ± 0.0268 · 42: 0.373 ± 0.0212 · 56: 0.399 ± 0.0422 · 70: 0.374 ± 0.0266 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: K and temperature (exothermic)

The gradient of the best-fit line estimates −ΔH (in the model's units, with k = 1): expect about +1.2 to +1.5. It falls short of 2 because hot particles also collide more often (collision rate ∝ √T), which helps the forward step. A positive gradient here means K falls as T rises: the signature of an exothermic reaction.
Result from a test run: 0.5: -1.78 ± 0.0263 · 0.6: -1.65 ± 0.0488 · 0.7: -1.49 ± 0.0519 · 0.8: -1.28 ± 0.052 · 0.9: -1.2 ± 0.0251 · 1: -1.08 ± 0.0246 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Yield and pressure

The count of AB wanders up and down around its equilibrium value, so each run's time-average is slightly different.
Result from a test run: 30: 60 ± 0.904 · 45: 51 ± 1.13 · 60: 46.7 ± 1.67 · 75: 44.4 ± 1.65 · 90: 40 ± 1.31 · 100: 35.9 ± 1.13 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Kp when the box is squeezed

Flat within the scatter: squeezing raises the mole fraction of AB but leaves Kp alone. (At T = 1, Kp = Kc in these units.)
Result from a test run: 40: 0.387 ± 0.0331 · 55: 0.408 ± 0.0397 · 70: 0.356 ± 0.041 · 85: 0.328 ± 0.0306 · 100: 0.432 ± 0.0233 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Fluctuations at equilibrium

Equilibrium is a balance of random events, so the count is a distribution, not one number. The relative size of the wobble shrinks like 1/√N as the number of particles grows.
Result from a test run: mean 25.8, SD 3.41, SE 0.279 from 150 runs
