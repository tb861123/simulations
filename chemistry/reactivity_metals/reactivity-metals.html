How the model works (and what it leaves out)

1 · Water, steam and acid

  • Hydrogen is made at the metal surface, at a rate k × area × [acid] that speeds up with temperature (e^(−Ea/RT), Ea = 40 kJ/mol). The k values put the metals in the right order and give classroom-like times (magnesium ribbon in 1 M acid about a minute, iron granules many minutes); they are not measured. Real rates also depend on stirring, impurities and the exact surface.
  • Area: ribbon/foil 60, granules 3 and powder 300 cm² per gram, shrinking as the pieces get smaller (∝ mass^(2/3)).
  • Aluminium's oxide layer stops it reacting at first; the acid slowly removes it (time scale 60 s ÷ [acid]). With water and steam the layer protects it almost completely.
  • Amounts: metal + acid makes z/2 mol of H₂ per mole of metal (z = charge on the ion); 1 mol of gas = 24 000 cm³ at room temperature and pressure. The acid is used up as it reacts, so if there is not enough acid, the acid limits the hydrogen. Iron with steam makes Fe₃O₄ (4 H₂ for every 3 Fe); in reality that reaction is reversible.
  • Heat: all of the heat (from enthalpies of formation) warms the liquid; no heat is lost. Steam reactions do not heat a liquid, so no temperature is shown. Magnesium burning in steam glows white.
  • Left out: potassium, sodium, lithium and calcium with acid are far too dangerous to try; the model just shows them as violent. Slow rusting of iron in water (it needs oxygen too) is not modelled.

2 · Displacement

  • Whether it happens comes from the order of the series (standard electrode potentials E°): a metal displaces any metal below it. How fast is k × ΔE° × area × [ion], a made-up rate chosen so stirred zinc powder in copper sulfate finishes in about 20 s and a strip takes many minutes.
  • Heat: ΔH per mole of metal from enthalpies of formation of the ions in water (Mg²⁺ −466.9, Al³⁺ −531, Zn²⁺ −153.9, Fe²⁺ −89.1, Cu²⁺ +64.8, Ag⁺ +105.6 kJ/mol). The solution has the heat capacity of water (4.18 J/g/°C, 1 g/cm³); the cup, the thermometer and the solid metal are left out. Heat is lost at a steady fraction of the extra temperature per minute. Real results are usually a few degrees lower, partly because the reaction is never quite instant and partly because some metals also react with the water or acid in the solution (magnesium makes hydrogen too).
  • Aluminium is slow to start in copper sulfate because of its oxide layer (time scale 2 minutes in the model).

3 · Extraction

  • The method follows the position in the series: carbon can only reduce the oxides of metals below it. Real choices also depend on cost, ore purity and the energy price: copper is now mostly extracted by smelting sulfide ores and purified by electrolysis; potassium is made by reducing potassium chloride with sodium.
  • Blast furnace: the iron made is (ore mass) × (Fe₂O₃ fraction) × 0.699, minus a small amount lost in the slag. The temperatures shown are typical (about 200 °C at the top to 2000 °C where the hot air enters). Real pig iron contains about 4% carbon.
  • Electrolysis of aluminium: Faraday's law, mass = efficiency × (current × time ÷ 3F) × 26.98 g. Real cells have a current efficiency of about 90–95% and use about 13–15 kWh of electricity per kg. The carbon anodes burn away (C + O₂ → CO₂) and are replaced regularly. The cryolite lowers the melting point from 2072 °C to about 950 °C.
  • A level box (Advanced). Oxidation numbers use the usual rules (elements 0; O −2; the numbers add up to the charge). Fe₃O₄ is shown as one Fe²⁺ and two Fe³⁺. The extraction steps are the textbook equations (simplified: real furnaces and smelters run many reactions at once).
