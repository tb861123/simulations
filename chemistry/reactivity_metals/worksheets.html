Reactivity and Extracting Metals · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Which metal reacts fastest with acid?

What this shows. The time to collect 20 cm³ of hydrogen gets longer down the series: magnesium about 13 s, aluminium about 110 s (held back at first by its oxide layer), zinc about 4 minutes and iron about 14 minutes (log scale: each is several times slower). Copper never makes any hydrogen because it is below hydrogen in the series, so it has no points. The runs scatter because granules differ in surface area (±15%) and the room temperature varies. The theory line is the model's rate equation; the rates are chosen to give realistic times, not measured.

2 · How much hydrogen from 0.1 g of each metal?

What this shows. The amount of hydrogen depends on the moles of metal and the charge on its ion, not on reactivity: 0.1 g of magnesium makes 99 cm³ (Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂), aluminium 133 cm³ (each Al gives 3 electrons, so 2Al → 3H₂), zinc only 37 cm³ and iron 43 cm³ (heavier atoms, so fewer moles in 0.1 g). The acid is in excess, so the metal is the limiting reactant. Scatter comes from weighing (±2%) and reading the syringe (±1 cm³).

3 · Temperature rise vs mass of zinc

What this shows. The temperature rise goes up in a straight line with the mass of zinc, then levels off at about 26 °C once there is more than 0.82 g. 25 cm³ of 0.5 M copper sulfate holds 0.0125 mol of Cu²⁺; that much zinc (0.0125 × 65.4 = 0.82 g) uses it all up. After that, the copper sulfate is the limiting reactant and extra zinc just stays unreacted. Each mole of zinc releases 219 kJ. The measured rise is a little below the theory because some heat escapes while the powder reacts (the dashed line is the model with that heat loss).

4 · Further apart in the series, bigger temperature rise?

What this shows. The same amount of copper sulfate (0.0125 mol) is displaced each time, but the heat released depends on how far above copper the metal is: magnesium about 64 °C, aluminium about 50 °C (a little lower in the runs: its oxide layer makes it slow to start, so more heat escapes), zinc 26 °C and iron 18 °C. Copper and silver cannot displace copper, so nothing happens. ΔH comes from enthalpies of formation of the ions. In a real lab magnesium also fizzes with the water, and the solution would boil if more concentrated.

5 · How much iron from 1 tonne of ore?

What this shows. Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂: 159.7 g of iron(III) oxide contains 111.7 g of iron, so each tonne of ore gives 699 kg × the fraction of Fe₂O₃. A 60% ore gives about 420 kg; doubling the purity doubles the iron (a straight line through the origin, gradient 6.99 kg per %). The runs come out about 2% below the ideal line (gradient about 6.85) because a little iron is lost in the slag (the dashed line), and scatter because the ore's analysis varies by ±2%.

6 · Aluminium from a 300 kA cell

What this shows. Faraday: the mass of aluminium is proportional to the charge (current × time). Each aluminium ion needs 3 electrons, so 3 × 96 485 C makes 27 g. A 300 kA cell makes about 2250 kg a day at 93% current efficiency: a straight line through the origin, about 94 kg per hour. The grey dashed line is 100%: real cells lose some current (some aluminium re-oxidises), so they always fall a few per cent short. Changing the efficiency from day to day (±1%) causes the small scatter. (Chemistry only.)
