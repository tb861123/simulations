Rate Equations and Kinetics · Teacher notesrate-equations.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Rate is a gradient

What to look for: The graph of [I₂] against time is a straight line, so every tangent has the same gradient: the rate stays the same until the iodine runs out. That is unusual: most reactions slow down as the reactant is used up. Iodine is not involved in the slow step, so its concentration does not matter.

Investigation 2: A reaction that slows down

What to look for: As the bromoalkane is used up there are fewer particles to react each second, so the rate (the gradient) falls. The curve gets flatter and flatter.

Investigation 3: Double the concentration

What to look for: Doubling the concentration doubles the starting rate: more particles in the same volume, so more successful collisions each second.

Investigation 4: Warm it up

What to look for: About 3 times faster for 10 °C here. A small rise in temperature gives many more particles with at least the activation energy.

Investigation 5: The iodine clock

What to look for: 1/t is proportional to the initial rate. Doubling [I⁻] doubles 1/t (halves the time): first order in iodide.

Investigation 6: Constant half-life A level

What to look for: Each half-life is the same (about 87 s at 25 °C) and does not depend on the starting concentration: the sign of a first-order reaction. t½ = ln 2 ÷ k, so k ≈ 0.008 s⁻¹.

Investigation 7: Half-lives that grow A level

What to look for: Each half-life is double the last: the sign of second order (here first order in each of two reactants at the same concentration). t½ = 1 ÷ (k[A]₀).

Investigation 8: Pseudo-first order A level

What to look for: OH⁻ hardly changes, so rate = k[RBr][OH⁻] ≈ k′[RBr] with k′ = k[OH⁻]: the half-lives become constant. Swamping one reactant is how chemists find the order of the other.

Investigation 9: What the rate equation tells you A level

What to look for: [H⁺] and [propanone] change the rate, [I₂] does not. So the rate-determining step involves propanone and H⁺; iodine reacts in a later, fast step (CP13).

Investigation 10: Orders from initial rates A level

What to look for: Both are first order: rate = k[H₂O₂][I⁻]. Each 1/t is proportional to the rate; the true k at 25 °C is 0.0115 dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ (units: rate ÷ two concentrations).

Investigation 11: CP14: the Arrhenius plot A level

What to look for: Gradient = −Ea/R. The clock reaction has Ea ≈ 56 kJ/mol. A stopwatch error of a few tenths of a second matters most at the hottest, fastest runs.

Batch experiment 1: First order: does t½ depend on [A]₀?

The half-life is the same at every starting concentration: first order. The scatter comes from the noise and the bath.
Theory (ln 2 ÷ k): 0.01: 86.6 · 0.02: 86.6 · 0.04: 86.6 · 0.06: 86.6 · 0.08: 86.6 · 0.1: 86.6
Result from a test run: 0.01: 86.3 ± 0.997 · 0.02: 86.3 ± 1.17 · 0.04: 86 ± 0.766 · 0.06: 90.6 ± 1.56 · 0.08: 87.2 ± 1.45 · 0.1: 84.8 ± 1.43 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Second order: t½ against [A]₀

Double the starting concentration and the half-life halves: second order.
Theory (1 ÷ (k[A]₀)): 0.02: 1250 · 0.04: 625 · 0.06: 417 · 0.08: 313 · 0.1: 250
Result from a test run: 0.02: 1210 ± 16.7 · 0.04: 620 ± 10.8 · 0.06: 406 ± 5.65 · 0.08: 305 ± 4.94 · 0.1: 247 ± 3.08 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Zero order in iodine

A flat line: changing [I₂] does not change the rate. Iodine is zero order (CP13).
Theory (k[propanone][H⁺]): 1: 14 · 1.5: 14 · 2: 14 · 2.5: 14 · 3: 14
Result from a test run: 1: 14.1 ± 0.155 · 1.5: 13.9 ± 0.147 · 2: 14.4 ± 0.197 · 2.5: 14.1 ± 0.194 · 3: 13.9 ± 0.203 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Iodine clock: 1/t against [I⁻]

A straight line through the origin: 1/t ∝ [I⁻], first order in iodide.
Theory (exact 1/t): 0.02: 0.00908 · 0.04: 0.0182 · 0.06: 0.0273 · 0.08: 0.0363 · 0.1: 0.0454
Result from a test run: 0.02: 0.00909 ± 8.92e-5 · 0.04: 0.0181 ± 2.37e-4 · 0.06: 0.0275 ± 3.14e-4 · 0.08: 0.0368 ± 4.78e-4 · 0.1: 0.046 ± 5.91e-4 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: CP14: Arrhenius plot

A straight line. Gradient × 1000 = −Ea/R, so Ea = −gradient × 8.314 J/mol ≈ 56 kJ/mol.
Theory (Ea = 56 kJ/mol): 3.1: -2.06 · 3.2: -2.74 · 3.3: -3.41 · 3.4: -4.08 · 3.5: -4.76
Result from a test run: 3.1: -2.06 ± 0.0221 · 3.2: -2.74 ± 0.0176 · 3.3: -3.4 ± 0.0119 · 3.4: -4.07 ± 0.0124 · 3.5: -4.76 ± 0.0065 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: How good is one class's Ea?

Most are within a few kJ/mol. The hottest runs are short, so the same stopwatch error is a bigger fraction of the time there.
Theory (true Ea): 56
Result from a test run: mean 56.1, SD 1.79, SE 0.103 from 300 runs

Batch experiment 7: Reaction time and the error in Ea

With no stopwatch error only the bath wobble remains. A bigger error spoils the result: use more thiosulfate (longer times) to make it a smaller percentage.
Result from a test run: 0: 0.446 ± 0.0392 · 0.2: 0.739 ± 0.0796 · 0.4: 1.39 ± 0.13 · 0.8: 2.61 ± 0.314 · 1.2: 3.29 ± 0.352 · 1.6: 5.55 ± 0.725 (mean ± SE, 60 runs each)
