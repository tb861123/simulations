How the model works (and what it leaves out)

1 · Profiles

  • The ΔH values are data-book values. The activation energies are illustrative (only hydrogen peroxide's, about 75 kJ/mol, is a measured value); you can change them. The catalysed route lowers the part of the barrier above the higher level by 45%, so ΔH stays the same. Real catalysed reactions often go by several smaller steps, not one lower hump.
  • The curve shape is smooth and made up; only the three levels (reactants, peak, products) mean anything. Photosynthesis really happens in many light-driven steps.

2 · Cup

  • The solution has the density and specific heat capacity of water (1 g/cm³, 4.18 J/g/°C). The solid's own mass is left out of m, as students do. The cup and thermometer take some heat (5 J/°C for polystyrene, 60 J/°C for a glass beaker).
  • The reaction or dissolving goes as (n − x) ÷ τ, with τ from about 4 s (neutralisation) to 45 s (magnesium ribbon), four times slower without stirring. Heat is lost at a steady fraction of the extra temperature each minute: 1.2% (polystyrene with a lid), 3% (no lid) and 6% (glass), typical but not measured values.
  • The thermometer is read every 30 s to 0.1 °C; the reagents are mixed at 3 minutes. Extrapolation fits a straight line to the readings from one minute after the peak to the end.
  • ΔH data: neutralisation −57.1, Zn + Cu²⁺ −217, Mg + 2H⁺ −467, dissolving NH₄NO₃ +25.7, KNO₃ +34.9, CaCl₂ −82.8 kJ/mol. Real neutralisations of strong acids give −57 to −58 kJ/mol; dissolving enthalpies change a little with concentration. Magnesium's hydrogen carries a little heat away; this is left out.

3 · Fuels

  • ΔHc data (liquids): methanol −726, ethanol −1367, propan-1-ol −2021, butan-1-ol −2676, pentan-1-ol −3329 kJ/mol. Each extra CH₂ adds about 650 kJ/mol.
  • Only part of the heat reaches the water: 45% with a draught screen and the can 2 cm above the wick, 28% without a screen, falling as the can is raised; a glass beaker passes on 80% as much. These are typical school-lab values, not measured ones. The copper can (60 g, 23 J/°C) takes some heat too; the student's calculation leaves it out. The water cools at 1.5% of its extra temperature per minute.
  • Longer alcohols burn with a more sooty flame; the model takes 0–8% incomplete combustion, which releases less energy. Some fuel evaporates from the wick before and after burning (0.02 g), so the measured mass burnt is a little too big.

4 · Bonds

  • Bond energies are averages over many compounds (C–H 413, O=O 498, C=O 805 in CO₂, O–H 464 kJ/mol …), so ΔH from bond energies is close to, but not the same as, the data-book value. They apply to gases: data-book values with liquid water (methane −890, ethanol −1367) are bigger because condensing water releases more energy.
  • At A level these are called mean bond enthalpies (9CH0 Topic 8).
  • The picture moves atoms in a straight line from where they start to where they end; it is not how the reaction really happens. Real reactions break and make bonds a few at a time.

5 · Hess cycles (A level)

  • ΔfH and ΔcH values are standard data-book values at 298 K. Dissolving enthalpies for copper(II) sulfate are approximate (they depend on how much water is used).
  • CP8: KHCO₃ + HCl is +31.6 and K₂CO₃ + 2HCl is −32.9 kJ/mol (worked out from ΔfH values), so the target is +96.1 kJ/mol. Each experiment loses 4% of the temperature change to the room; the thermometer is read to 0.1 °C with a random error of about 0.05 °C, and the balance to 0.01 g. These are why your answer differs from the data book.

6 · Born–Haber (A level)

  • Atomisation, ionisation and electron-affinity values are data-book values. The second electron affinity of oxygen is taken as +798 kJ/mol (books give +798 to +844, so the MgO lattice energy is approximate). The lattice energy is worked out from the cycle; the ionic-model (theoretical) values are approximate textbook values. Ionic radii are Shannon radii for 6-coordinate ions.

7 · Entropy and Gibbs energy (A level)

  • Standard entropies and ΔH at 298 K. The model assumes ΔH and ΔS do not change with temperature, so the line ΔG = ΔH − TΔS is straight. Real values drift a little at high temperatures, and a state change (e.g. water boiling) would put a kink in the line.
