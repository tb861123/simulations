Energy Changes · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Temperature rise as alkali is added

What this shows. While the acid is in excess, more alkali means more water formed and more heat, so the temperature rise climbs: 2.2 °C with 5 cm³, 5.9 °C with 20 cm³. It peaks at 25 cm³ (about 6.6 °C, theory 6.8 °C), where the moles of acid and alkali are equal: 0.025 mol × 57.1 kJ/mol = 1.43 kJ heats 50 g of solution. After that no more water forms, but the extra alkali still has to be warmed, so the rise falls (4.5 °C at 50 cm³). The peak finds the end point, like a titration (a thermometric titration). The runs sit a little below the no-loss line because the cup takes some heat and a little escapes before the highest reading.

2 · How close does a class get to −57 kJ/mol?

What this shows. The data-book value is −57.1 kJ/mol. The class results cluster tightly around −55.2 kJ/mol (SD about 0.5), about 3% less negative. That difference is systematic, not random: the cup and thermometer take about 2.4% of the heat (5 J/°C out of 214 J/°C) and a little escapes in the half-minute before the highest reading. Repeating the experiment makes the mean more precise (a tiny standard error) but cannot remove a systematic error, so the mean stays dozens of standard errors away from the data book. The small scatter comes from the measuring cylinders (±1%) and reading the thermometer.

3 · Heat loss: how wrong does ΔH get?

What this shows. With no heat lost at all, ΔH is still about −55.8 kJ/mol, because the cup itself takes some heat. Each extra 1% per minute of heat loss makes ΔH about 0.25 kJ/mol less negative, reaching about −53.4 kJ/mol at 10% per minute (a glass beaker is about 6%). Neutralisation is fast, so the highest reading comes within a minute and not much heat escapes first; for slower reactions such as zinc with copper sulfate the loss matters much more, which is why the cooling line is extrapolated back to the time of mixing (try the ΔT setting).

4 · Endothermic: temperature fall vs mass dissolved

What this shows. Dissolving ammonium nitrate is endothermic (+25.7 kJ/mol), so the temperature falls, and twice the mass gives twice the fall: a straight line through the origin with a gradient of about −1.45 °C per gram. With no heat exchanged the gradient would be −1.54 °C per gram (25.7 kJ ÷ 80 g/mol ÷ (50 g × 4.18 J/g/°C)). The measured falls are about 5% smaller because the cold solution takes heat from the room and the cup while the solid dissolves. The mass of the solid itself is left out of m, as students usually do.

5 · Energy from the alcohols

What this shows. The data-book values fall in a straight line, about 650 kJ/mol more negative for each extra CH₂ (each CH₂ adds more bonds to break but even more energy from making extra CO₂ and H₂O). The measured values follow the same pattern but are only about 37% as big: −495 kJ/mol for ethanol against −1367, with a best-fit gradient of about −220 kJ/mol per carbon. Most of the heat from the flame goes into the air, some warms the can, the longer alcohols burn with a sooty flame (incomplete combustion), and a little fuel evaporates. The scatter comes mostly from the flame and draughts (how much heat reaches the can varies by about 8%).

6 · How good are bond energies?

What this shows. There is no random part here: the same bonds give the same answer every time. Bond energies agree with the data-book values (for gases) to within a few per cent for most reactions: HCl −185 (−185), H₂O −486 (−484), NH₃ −93 (−92), ethanol −1276 (−1277), NO +181 (+181). Methane gives −818 against −802 and ethane −125 against −137, because the values are averages: a C–H bond in methane is not exactly the same as one in ethane. Data-book values that use liquid water (methane −890, ethanol −1367) are bigger, because water condensing also gives out energy.
