Collision Theory and Rates · Teacher notescollision-theory.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Not every collision reacts

What to look for: Only a few per cent. Most collisions are too gentle: the molecules just bounce apart unchanged. A collision succeeds only if its energy is at least the activation energy, the minimum needed to start breaking the bonds. On the left graph, only the molecules in the tail to the right of the Ea line carry that much energy.

Investigation 2: Turn up the heat

What to look for: Much faster, far more than the modest rise in collision rate. Hotter molecules do collide a bit more often, but the main effect is on the left graph: the hill flattens and stretches right, so a far bigger share of molecules is beyond the Ea line. A small rise in temperature gives a large rise in successful collisions.

Investigation 3: Concentration

What to look for: With four times as many A₂ molecules, each B₂ meets an A₂ four times as often, so the starting rate is about four times greater. The share of collisions that succeed does not change. The final amount of AB is the same (300), because B₂ runs out: it is the limiting reactant.

Investigation 4: Pressure

What to look for: Halving the volume doubles the concentration of both gases, so each molecule meets a partner twice as often and there are twice as many of them per unit volume: the molecules in the box react roughly twice as fast (and the rate per unit volume is four times as great).

Investigation 5: A catalyst

What to look for: Almost all the flashes (gold ones) are right beside a bead. The catalyst offers a different route with a lower activation energy, shown dashed on the energy diagram, so far more collisions there succeed. The beads are still all present at the end: a catalyst is not used up. It does not change the amount of product, only how quickly you get it.

Investigation 6: Surface area: lump or powder?

What to look for: In the lump most of the solid is locked inside where the acid cannot reach it; only the outside layer can be hit. Breaking it up exposes more particles, so there are more collisions each second and the curve starts steeper. The final amount of product is the same. This is why powdered marble fizzes faster than chips, and why flour dust can explode.

Investigation 7: A higher hill to climb

What to look for: No: the rate falls far faster than that. Each extra 2 to 3 kJ/mol (at 400 K) cuts the successful share by about half again, because the tail of the energy distribution thins out exponentially. The reading 'theory e^(−Ea/RT)' is this share, and it is the heart of the Arrhenius equation.

Investigation 8: Runaway

What to look for: Each reaction releases energy, which heats the gas, which makes more collisions succeed, which releases energy faster still. The curve bends upward and the reaction runs away: an explosion. With the flask in a water bath (walls that conduct heat) the energy leaks away and the same reaction stays slow and steady. Chemical engineers work hard to keep exothermic reactions cool.

Investigation 9: The rate equation A level

What to look for: Doubling A₂ doubles the starting rate; doubling both makes it four times as great. So the reaction is first order in each: rate = k[A₂][B₂], second order overall. That is what you would expect when the reaction happens in one step, a single collision between an A₂ and a B₂. (Use the batch run for a cleaner measurement: single runs are noisy.)

Investigation 10: The right way round A level

What to look for: It drops to about a quarter of what it was: energy is necessary but not sufficient. For big, awkwardly shaped molecules the share that collide the right way round can be tiny. One job of an enzyme is to hold molecules in exactly the right orientation.
