Collision Theory and Rates

Particles must collide, with enough energy and the right way round, to react. See how temperature, concentration, pressure, surface area and catalysts change the rate.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Particles are hard discs in a flat (2D) box 40 nm × 25 nm. A, B, C and D all have mass 30 g/mol and radius 0.3 nm (C is 60 g/mol and 0.4 nm when A + B join up). They move in straight lines and bounce elastically, like snooker balls.
  • Speeds start from the Maxwell–Boltzmann spread for the temperature: each velocity component is random with standard deviation √(RT/m). The average kinetic energy in 2D is RT per mole.
  • Water bath: particles of an unreactive solvent (not drawn) bump into each particle about once every 3 ps and give it a fresh random velocity for the set temperature. This keeps the temperature steady and keeps the Maxwell–Boltzmann spread even though reactions use up the fastest particles. Heat given out by the reaction is taken away at once.
  • Collision energy: E = ½μv², where v is the closing speed along the line joining the centres and μ = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂). Only this part of the motion squashes the particles together; a glancing blow has little of it.
  • Reaction rule: if E ≥ Ea the pair reacts; if not, they just bounce. With "right way round" on, A only reacts when the other particle hits within 60° of A's marked end, so 1 in 3 energetic collisions work (the steric factor P = ⅓).
  • Theory: for hard discs, the fraction of collisions with E ≥ Ea is exactly e−Ea/RT. Collisions per second between A and B = 2d × v̄rel × g × NANB/area, with d = 0.6 nm, v̄rel = √(πRT/2μ) and g a small crowding correction. Rate = collisions × P × e−Ea/RT.
  • Arrhenius: rate = A e−Ea/RT, so ln(rate) against 1/T is a straight line with gradient −Ea/R. In this model collisions also get a little more frequent as particles speed up (v̄ ∝ √T), so the gradient gives Ea + ½RT, slightly more than Ea.
  • Catalyst: gives a different route with half the activation energy. It is not used up and does not change the energy of the products.
  • Solid: 64 fixed particles in square lumps. Only particles on the surface can be hit. A reacts with a solid particle if the energy of the hit (½ m v² along the line of centres) is at least Ea; the solid particle is removed and A becomes C.
  • Initial rate is measured from the time taken for the first 20% of the limiting reactant to react. The theory value allows for the reactants used up during that time.
  • Scaled energies: real activation energies are 40–200 kJ/mol and only about 1 collision in a billion succeeds. Here Ea is up to 30 kJ/mol, so you can watch. The ideas are the same.
  • Not modelled: 3D motion, molecular shapes and spinning, attractions between particles, heat from the reaction warming the gas, reverse reactions, and the many steps of real catalysed reactions.

The reactants must climb the energy hill (Ea) before they can turn into products. A catalyst gives a route with a lower hill. Tip: drag the white Ea line on the collision-energy graph.

Key equations

rate = change in amount ÷ time taken
Ea = the minimum energy a collision needs to react.
fraction of collisions with E ≥ Ea = e^(−Ea/RT)
rate = collision frequency × P × e^(−Ea/RT)
Arrhenius: k = A e^(−Ea/RT), so ln k = ln A − Ea/(RT)
Rule of thumb: a 10 °C rise roughly doubles the rate for a typical reaction near room temperature (Ea ≈ 50 kJ/mol). The +10 °C reading shows the factor for the settings here.
