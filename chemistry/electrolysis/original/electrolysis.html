How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Which product forms follows the rules in the specification. Molten: metal at the cathode, non-metal at the anode. Solutions: the metal only if it is less reactive than hydrogen (copper, silver), otherwise hydrogen; a halogen from bromide or iodide, and from chloride when it is concentrated, otherwise oxygen from OH⁻. Really this depends on electrode potentials, overvoltages, the electrode material and the current, and some mixtures form.
  • Dilute or concentrated chloride: the share of the anode's electrons that make chlorine is c² ÷ (c² + 0.25) for c mol/dm³ of Cl⁻ (about 4% at 0.1 M, 50% at 0.5 M, 80% at 1 M, 99% in brine). This smooth curve is made up to show the trend; real mixtures depend on the electrode and the current.
  • Current: the supply keeps the current you set, unless it would need more than the supply's voltage. The voltage needed is the decomposition voltage (about 0.05–3.4 V, rounded values) plus I × R, with R = gap ÷ (conductivity × area), a 4 cm gap and rounded conductivities. Conductivity is fixed at its starting value; really it changes as ions are used up and as the liquid warms.
  • Faraday's law is exact: every electron does one job. Moles of electrons = I × t ÷ 96 485; metal = ÷ z; gas = ÷ electrons per molecule; 24 000 cm³ per mole at 20 °C (volume ∝ absolute temperature). The current efficiency is 100%.
  • Ions get used up: discharged ions are removed from the solution. When the Cu²⁺ (or Ag⁺) runs out, hydrogen forms at the cathode instead; as chloride runs low, more oxygen forms. With copper electrodes the anode puts Cu²⁺ back as fast as the cathode removes it, so the colour stays the same.
  • Gases dissolve first: before bubbles are collected, 100 cm³ of solution takes in 1.6 cm³ of hydrogen, 3.1 cm³ of oxygen (real values for water at 20 °C) and about 20 cm³ of chlorine (an estimate for brine). The model assumes none was dissolved at the start. Chlorine also reacts a little with water and with the alkali that forms. Each tube holds 50 cm³; after that the gas escapes.
  • Loose deposits: above a current density (current ÷ area) of about 0.04 A/cm² for copper (0.01 A/cm² for silver), the metal grows as a powder and up to 35% falls off. The threshold and the amount are illustrative.
  • Molten salts: lead(II) bromide melts at 373 °C, sodium chloride at 801 °C. The gases are not collected (this is done in a fume cupboard): bromine is a brown vapour, chlorine pale green. Molten lead collects as a bead under the cathode; sodium floats.
  • The pictures are not to scale. Only a few ions are drawn; the H⁺ and OH⁻ ions from water are drawn far more often than they really are (about one water molecule in 500 million is ionised). The thinning of a copper anode is exaggerated so that you can see it.
  • A level box (Advanced). Oxidation numbers follow the usual rules (elements 0; O −2 except in peroxides; H +1 except in metal hydrides; the numbers add up to the charge). The half-equations are written for the conditions shown (acid, alkali or either). The builder combines a reduction and an oxidation by multiplying each so the electrons cancel, then cancels H⁺, OH⁻ and water that appear on both sides. Which ion is discharged is decided here by the iGCSE rules; really it depends on electrode potentials (A level Topic 14, in the Electrochemical cells simulation), concentration and overpotential.
  • Left out: heating of the electrolyte by the current, the resistance of the wires, the anode carbon slowly burning in oxygen, ozone at the anode, and the reactions that happen when products mix.
