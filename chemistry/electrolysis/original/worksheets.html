Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Is the mass of copper proportional to the time?

What this shows. The copper gained by the cathode rises in a straight line through the origin: doubling the time doubles the charge (Q = I t) and so doubles the moles of electrons and of copper. Each Cu²⁺ needs 2 electrons, so 0.5 A for 20 min (600 C, 0.0062 mol of electrons) gives 0.0031 mol = 0.198 g, about 0.0099 g per minute. The runs sit about 1% below Faraday's line because a little copper is lost when the electrode is washed and dried, and they scatter because the balance reads to about ±0.01 g and the current drifts. (Chemistry only.)

2 · Does doubling the current double the copper?

What this shows. Up to about 0.4 A the mass follows Faraday's straight line: twice the current, twice the copper. Above that the current density (current ÷ area, more than 0.04 A/cm² here) is so high that copper ions cannot reach the surface fast enough to build a firm layer: the copper grows as a dark, loose powder and part of it falls off, so the mass on the electrode falls further and further below the line. This is why electroplating uses small currents and large electrodes. The threshold and the amount lost are illustrative values.

3 · How close does a class get to Faraday?

What this shows. Faraday predicts 0.198 g of copper. The results form a bell-shaped spread with an SD of about 0.008 g, from the ammeter (±2%), the stopwatch (±3 s), two weighings (±0.005 g each) and washing losses. The mean is 1–2% below Faraday because some copper is always lost when the electrode is washed and dried: a systematic error, which repeating does not remove. The standard error of the mean is tiny, so this small difference shows up clearly as several standard errors.

4 · Same charge, different metals

What this shows. The same charge (900 C = 0.0093 mol of electrons) passes each time, but the mass of metal is very different: lead 0.97 g (Pb²⁺ needs 2 electrons, but lead atoms are heavy, Ar 207), silver 1.01 g (Ag⁺ needs only 1 electron, so twice as many atoms), copper 0.30 g (2 electrons, Ar 63.5) from both copper salts. Moles of metal = moles of electrons ÷ charge on the ion. The large electrodes keep the current density low so the silver does not flake off.

5 · Hydrogen : oxygen, always 2 : 1?

What this shows. Each O₂ needs 4 electrons and each H₂ only 2, so Faraday says 2 : 1. Early on the measured ratio is much bigger than 2, because the first few cm³ of each gas dissolve in the solution, and oxygen is about twice as soluble as hydrogen (3.1 against 1.6 cm³ per 100 cm³). As more gas is made, the dissolved part matters less and the ratio falls towards 2. Small volumes are also hard to read, so the early ratios scatter most.

6 · Why is there less chlorine than hydrogen?

What this shows. Faraday says the anode makes as much chlorine as the cathode makes hydrogen (both need 2 electrons per molecule): the straight line. The chlorine collected stays well below it, because chlorine is far more soluble than hydrogen and the first 20 cm³ or so dissolve in the solution (and react with it). After that the collected volume rises parallel to the line. 4 M chloride is concentrated, so only about 1.5% of the anode's electrons make oxygen instead. The dissolving amount is an estimate for brine.
