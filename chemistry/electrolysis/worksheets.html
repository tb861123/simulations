Teacher notes

Investigation 1: It has to be melted

What to look for: In the solid the ions are held in a lattice and cannot move, so no current flows and nothing happens. When it melts the ions are free: positive lead ions drift to the negative electrode (cathode) and become beads of molten lead; negative bromide ions drift to the positive electrode (anode) and become brown bromine vapour. An ionic compound conducts only when molten or dissolved.

Investigation 2: Follow the electrons

What to look for: Electrons flow through the wires from the anode, round through the power supply, to the cathode. They never pass through the liquid: there the current is carried by moving ions. At the cathode positive ions gain electrons (reduction); at the anode negative ions lose electrons (oxidation). OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.

Investigation 3: Salt water: where is the sodium?

What to look for: Hydrogen forms at the cathode, not sodium: sodium is far more reactive than hydrogen, so its ions hold on to their charge and hydrogen ions are discharged instead. Chlorine forms at the anode. Sodium ions and hydroxide ions are left, so the solution turns into sodium hydroxide. All three products are valuable: this is the chlor-alkali industry. Notice that less chlorine is collected than hydrogen at first: chlorine is slightly soluble.

Investigation 4: Copper plating

What to look for: Copper is less reactive than hydrogen, so copper ions are discharged and a pink-brown layer of copper builds up on the cathode. At the anode, sulfate ions are not discharged; hydroxide ions from the water are, giving oxygen. The blue colour is due to copper ions, so it fades as they are removed from the solution.

Investigation 5: Purifying copper

What to look for: The copper anode dissolves: its atoms lose electrons and go into solution as Cu²⁺. The same number of copper ions are discharged at the cathode. So the cathode gains exactly what the anode loses, and the solution stays just as blue. This is how copper is purified for electrical wiring: the impure copper is the anode, and pure copper builds up on the cathode while impurities fall to the bottom.

Investigation 6: Splitting water

What to look for: Twice as much gas collects at the cathode (hydrogen) as at the anode (oxygen). Overall the water is being split: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂. The 2 : 1 ratio by volume is the reason water's formula is H₂O. Test the gases: hydrogen burns with a squeaky pop; oxygen relights a glowing splint.

Investigation 7: How much? (Chemistry only)

What to look for: The mass deposited is proportional to the charge passed, and charge = current × time. So doubling either the current or the time doubles the mass. 1 A for 600 s is 600 C, which is 600 ÷ 96 500 = 0.0062 mol of electrons; each copper ion needs two, so 0.0031 mol of copper, or 0.197 g.

Investigation 8: Redox at the electrodes A level

What to look for: Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. Copper goes from +2 to 0: it gains electrons, so it is reduced, and Cu²⁺ is the oxidising agent. Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻. Chlorine goes from −1 to 0: it is oxidised, and Cl⁻ is the reducing agent. The number of electrons lost at the anode always equals the number gained at the cathode, which is how the half-equations are combined: Cu²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → Cu + Cl₂.
