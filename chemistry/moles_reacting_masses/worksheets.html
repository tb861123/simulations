Moles, Reacting Masses and Yield · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Formula of magnesium oxide: 200 students

What this shows. Each run is one student burning about 0.2 g of magnesium. The ratio should be 1.00 (MgO), but the histogram is centred below 1, about 0.9: a systematic error, not just scatter. Some MgO smoke escapes when the lid is lifted, a little magnesium is left unburnt, and some forms magnesium nitride (Mg₃N₂), which adds less mass than MgO. All of these make the mass of oxygen too small. The spread (SD about 0.06) comes mostly from the balance: 0.01 g in about 0.13 g of oxygen is ±8%. Repeating more times shrinks the standard error but does not remove a systematic error.

2 · How often to lift the lid?

What this shows. Too few lifts and the magnesium runs short of oxygen: some is left unburnt after 8 minutes. Too many and white MgO smoke escapes each time. Both make the ratio too low, so the best result is in the middle, about once a minute. The dashed grey line is the model's average student with a perfect balance; the yellow line is the true formula. Even at the best setting the result is a little low, because of magnesium nitride: tick 'Water added and reheated' in the table to remove it.

3 · Bigger sample, better result?

What this shows. The bars (±1 SD) shrink as the mass of magnesium grows. The balance error is the same ±0.01 g whatever the mass, so it is a smaller percentage of a bigger mass: percentage uncertainty = uncertainty ÷ value. The mean does not move towards 1, because the systematic errors (smoke, nitride) are a fixed fraction of the mass. Precision improves; accuracy does not.

4 · Water of crystallisation: heat for longer

What this shows. With a gentle flame the first four water molecules leave quickly but the last one is held more tightly, so x creeps up towards 5 and needs a long time to reach it. A student who stops after a few minutes without checking for constant mass gets x ≈ 3 or 4. That is why you heat, cool, weigh and repeat until two weighings agree.

5 · Too strong a flame?

What this shows. A strong flame drives the water off fast, but the solid spits (losing a little salt) and, after a few minutes, anhydrous copper(II) sulfate starts to decompose: CuSO₄ → CuO + SO₃. Both make the mass lost bigger than the mass of water, so x climbs past 5. Heat gently, and stop when the mass is constant.

6 · Limiting reactant: magnesium and acid

What this shows. The acid holds 0.0050 mol of HCl, enough for 0.0025 mol of magnesium (0.061 g) and 0.0025 mol of hydrogen (about 60 cm³). 7 · CP1: molar volume of hydrogen

What this shows. Molar volume = volume of hydrogen ÷ moles of magnesium. The ideal gas value at 20 °C and 101.3 kPa is 24.1 dm³ mol⁻¹, and the mean lands very close to it, but partly by luck: two systematic errors cancel. The 2% oxide coat means fewer moles of Mg than the mass suggests (Vm about 2% low), while gas collected over water carries water vapour (2.3 kPa of the 101.3, about 2% more volume). Try the syringe, or 0% oxide, in the table and the mean moves. The spread comes from the balance (0.001 g in 0.08 g by difference: about ±1%) and reading the cylinder to 1 cm³.

8 · pV = nRT: molar volume and temperature

What this shows. The volume of a mole of gas is proportional to the absolute temperature: Vm = RT ÷ p rises by about 0.08 dm³ for each degree. At 0 °C it is 22.4 dm³ (STP); 24.0 dm³ corresponds to about 20 °C, and at 25 °C it is 24.5 dm³. With a clean ribbon (no oxide) the points run parallel to the line, about 1% below it, because the syringe sticks a little (about 0.8 cm³ of 80); the scatter is from the balance and reading the syringe.

9 · Percentage yield: how far to evaporate?

What this shows. Copper(II) sulfate is quite soluble (about 21 g in 100 g of water at 20 °C), so the liquid left after the crystals form still holds a lot of it. The more liquid you leave, the more stays dissolved and the lower the yield. Leave about 19 cm³ or more and the solution never becomes saturated: no crystals at all. Evaporate too far and it all sets solid into a damp crust. Cooling in ice (try 0 °C in the table) raises the yield.
