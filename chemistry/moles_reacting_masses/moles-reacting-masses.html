How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Relative atomic masses: the model uses IUPAC standard atomic weights (2021, abridged, e.g. Mg 24.305, O 15.999). You calculate with the values on your periodic table (iGCSE: Mg 24, O 16; A level: Mg 24.3, O 16.0), so a perfect experiment can still be 0.1–1% "out". 1 mol = 6.022 × 10²³ particles (exact since 2019).
  • Reacting masses: moles = mass ÷ Mr; the reaction goes until one reactant runs out (the limiting reactant). Every reaction is taken as complete and one-way: no equilibrium, no side reactions, no losses.
  • Magnesium in a crucible: fraction burned 1 − e^(−t/τ), τ = 1.2 + 2.0 ÷ L^0.7 minutes (L = lid lifts a minute: more air, faster burning). White MgO smoke lost: on average 0.2% + 1% × L² of the oxide, varying a lot from run to run. About 7% ÷ (1 + L) of the magnesium forms magnesium nitride, Mg₃N₂, which adds less mass than MgO; water and reheating turn it into MgO. Each student's ribbon and crucible differ. Illustrative numbers, chosen to give typical school results.
  • Copper(II) oxide: reduced with a time constant of 2 minutes; if the gas is turned off while the tube is hot, 5–15% of the copper turns back into oxide. The flame, the gas flow and safety (light the excess gas) are not modelled.
  • Water of crystallisation: all but one water molecule leave with a time constant of 2.5 min (gentle) or 1.0 min (strong); the last one 12 min or 2.5 min. A strong flame makes the solid spit (about 0.6% lost) and slowly decomposes copper(II) sulfate (to CuO + SO₃) after 6 minutes. Real salts lose water in more steps, at set temperatures.
  • Yield: CuSO₄ from 25 cm³ of acid with excess CuO. Copper sulfate stays dissolved in the cold liquid left over; solubility from data tables (14 g per 100 g water at 0 °C, 21 g at 20 °C, 29 g at 40 °C). Transfer losses: careful 1.5% + 2%, normal 3% + 4%, careless 7% + 9%. Wet crystals hold about 6% water. The liquid volume left is taken as the mass of water.
  • Gases: ideal gas, V = nRT ÷ p. Rate ∝ n^(2/3) × acid concentration, doubling every 10 °C. Magnesium's oxide coat (0–6% of the mass, ±25%) uses acid but makes no gas. Late bung: about 3.5% of the gas lost. Syringe: sticks about 0.8 cm³ low, read to 1 cm³; measuring cylinder 1 cm³; burette 0.1 cm³. Over water, the gas carries water vapour (2.3 kPa at 20 °C). Carbon dioxide dissolves a little (about 0.12 cm³ per cm³ of acid, more over water). Gases are taken as ideal; real H₂ and CO₂ at room conditions are within 1% of ideal.
  • Solutions: weighing by difference (two readings), class B flasks (each one off by up to its tolerance), meniscus ±0.1 cm³ or 2 cm³ if read badly, 2% left behind if the beaker is not rinsed, NaOH pellets about 97% NaOH. The volume of the dissolved solid and temperature changes of the glass are ignored.
  • Balances read to 0.01 g or 0.001 g and wobble by a quarter of the last digit. Live experiments use new random numbers each time; batch runs use fixed seeds, so they repeat exactly.
