Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Separating crude oil

What to look for: Longer chains have stronger forces between molecules (intermolecular forces), so higher boiling points. They condense low down where it is hot. Short chains stay as gas until near the cool top. Each tray collects a fraction: a mixture of chains with similar boiling points, not a single compound.

Investigation 2: A cooler furnace

What to look for: Fewer molecules boil, so more is left at the bottom as residue. Very hot oil starts to crack and char in the furnace; real refineries send the residue to a second column at low pressure, where boiling points are lower.

Investigation 3: Cracking decane

What to look for: Cracking makes a shorter alkane and an alkene: e.g. C₁₀H₂₂ → C₈H₁₈ + C₂H₄. There are not enough hydrogen atoms for both pieces to be alkanes. Alkenes have a C=C double bond, which reacts with bromine: orange bromine water turns colourless. Alkanes leave it orange.

Investigation 4: Why crack?

What to look for: Crude oil has more long chains than people want, and less petrol and diesel. Cracking turns the surplus of long chains into shorter, more useful ones, plus alkenes to make polymers.

Investigation 5: No catalyst

What to look for: Without the catalyst you need about 850 °C instead of 600–700 °C. The catalyst provides a route with a lower activation energy, so more collisions have enough energy to break bonds at a lower temperature.

Investigation 6: Burning: enough oxygen?

What to look for: Complete combustion: carbon dioxide and water, a blue flame. With too little oxygen: incomplete combustion makes carbon monoxide (toxic: it stops blood carrying oxygen, and has no smell) and soot (carbon particles: blackening and breathing problems). Less energy is released too.

Investigation 7: Addition polymers

What to look for: A radical opens the C=C double bond; the chain grows one monomer at a time, with no other product (addition). Chains are hundreds of monomers long, and not all the same length. The repeat unit is drawn with the extension bonds.

Investigation 8: Longer chains

What to look for: Fewer radicals start fewer chains, so each one gets more monomers: mean chain length ≈ 2 × monomers ÷ initiator radicals (each chain ends by two growing ends joining). Longer chains tangle more: stronger, higher melting plastics.

Investigation 9: Free-radical substitution A level

What to look for: Initiation: Cl₂ → 2Cl• (UV breaks the weakest bond). Propagation: Cl• + CH₄ → HCl + •CH₃, then •CH₃ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•: the radical is regenerated, a chain reaction. Termination: two radicals meet (Cl• + •CH₃ → CH₃Cl, •CH₃ + •CH₃ → C₂H₆). Each initiation leads to many propagation cycles.

Investigation 10: Excess chlorine: a mixture A level

What to look for: Chlorine radicals attack CH₃Cl, CH₂Cl₂ and CHCl₃ as well, so further substitution gives a mixture, up to CCl₄. Use excess methane to make CH₃Cl the main product. Free-radical substitution is hard to control.
