Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · How much of crude oil is petrol?

What this shows. Each run takes 1000 molecules of light crude at random and distils them. About 11.5% of the mass comes out as petrol (C₅–C₈), the value expected from the crude's make-up; samples scatter by about ±0.8% because a small sample has, by chance, a few more or fewer short chains. Demand for petrol is about 27% of all oil products, more than twice what distillation gives: so refineries crack longer chains.

2 · Do shorter chains rise higher?

What this shows. The shorter the chain, the lower its boiling point and the higher it rises before it condenses. C₃ and C₄ never condense (they leave the top as gas, 100%), and from C₂₅ the boiling point is above the 400 °C furnace, so they never boil (0%: bitumen). The theory line kinks where the temperature steps between sections change. Points scatter a little because the trays are not perfect.

3 · Hotter furnace, less bitumen?

What this shows. Only molecules with boiling points below the furnace temperature turn to vapour, so the hotter the furnace, the less is left at the bottom: about 61% at 250 °C, falling to 12% at 450 °C. The theory is the share of the crude that boils above the furnace temperature (CRC boiling points). Refineries stop at about 400 °C because hotter oil starts to crack; the residue is distilled again under low pressure instead.

4 · Less oxygen, less energy?

What this shows. With 100% or more of the oxygen needed, octane burns completely and releases 5470 kJ/mol (the dashed data-book line). With less oxygen, carbon monoxide forms instead of CO₂, and below 68% soot too; each carbon atom that ends up as CO or C releases less energy, so the energy falls in straight lines. The scatter (about ±130 kJ/mol) comes from the thermometer readings. A school experiment loses much of its heat to the air: set 'heat reaching the water' to about 40% to see why measured values are so low.

5 · Why heat and a catalyst for cracking?

What this shows. With the silica/alumina catalyst almost nothing cracks below 450 °C, but by 650 °C three-quarters of the decane cracks in 2 s and above 700 °C nearly all of it does: the rate of bond breaking rises steeply with temperature. The dashed line is the same reaction with no catalyst: it needs about 200 °C more. The catalyst lowers the activation energy, which is why catalytic cracking runs at 600–700 °C.

6 · Longer polymer chains, higher melting point?

What this shows. Short chains (a few units) melt far below room temperature, like the small alkanes; with about 20 units (C₄₀) it is a wax that melts near 80 °C, like candle wax; by a few hundred units the melting point levels off near 140 °C, like real poly(ethene) (HDPE melts at 130–137 °C). Longer chains have more contact between them, so more energy is needed to separate them. The points match the theory closely because 500 chains give a precise average length.
