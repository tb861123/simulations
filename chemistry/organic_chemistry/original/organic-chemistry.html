How the model works (and what it leaves out)

1 · Crude oil

  • Crude oil is modelled as straight-chain alkanes only, from C₁ to C₈₀, with a smooth spread of chain lengths: mass share w(n) ∝ n² e^(−3n/μ), where μ is the average chain length by mass. Light, medium and heavy crudes use μ = 18, 22 and 32, which give roughly the yields of real North Sea, Middle Eastern and Venezuelan crudes. Real crude oil also has branched alkanes, rings (cycloalkanes), aromatic compounds and sulfur compounds.
  • Boiling points are real data (CRC Handbook) up to C₃₀; above that they are estimates, because such big molecules break up before they boil.
  • The column has 7 equal sections with edges at 20, 25, 130, 180, 260 and 340 °C and the furnace temperature at the inlet. Anything that boils below the furnace temperature becomes vapour; the rest stays liquid and leaves at the bottom as bitumen. A vapour condenses where the column is cooler than its boiling point, plus a random amount for imperfect trays (SD = "mixing between trays"). Real columns have 30–50 trays and send some liquid back down (reflux) to sharpen the cut.
  • Chain ranges come from the data. With these temperatures the fractions are C₁–C₄, C₅–C₈, C₉–C₁₀, C₁₁–C₁₄, C₁₅–C₁₉, C₂₀–C₂₄ and C₂₅+ (at 400 °C). Textbooks give slightly different ranges, and real fractions overlap because branched molecules boil lower than straight ones. Petrol sold at the pump is blended from the petrol and naphtha fractions.
  • Viscosity and flash point are typical values for each fraction near 20 °C, not calculated. The pour test time grows with viscosity^0.4 so that thick oils still move on screen; bitumen would really take hours.
  • Burning: hydrogen is assumed to burn to water first; the oxygen left over makes CO₂, then CO, then soot. Energies come from enthalpies of formation (NIST), with liquid water as in data books. Real flames make a mixture whatever the air supply, and also make nitrogen oxides (from the air at high temperature) and sulfur dioxide (from sulfur impurities); these are not modelled.
  • The calorimeter (batch only): burning m grams heats water; the share of heat that reaches the water and two thermometer readings have random errors. ΔH = m c ΔT ÷ moles burnt, with c = 4.18 J/g/°C.

2 · Cracking

  • Each C–C bond breaks at random at a rate k = A e^(−Ea/RT). A and Ea are chosen to give realistic behaviour (decane half-cracks in about a second at 650 °C with the catalyst, or at about 850 °C without it); they are not measured values for one real catalyst.
  • The products: the smaller piece becomes the alkene (with the C=C at the end) and the bigger piece the alkane, which can crack again. Really the C=C can be anywhere, alkenes crack too, and some molecules form rings or lose hydrogen (ethane → ethene + hydrogen); this model leaves those out.
  • Bromine water: each alkene molecule that bubbles through uses up one bromine molecule. Only the gases (C₁–C₄) bubble through; bigger products condense as a liquid.
  • Supply and demand uses the crude oil from part 1 (perfect column) and approximate world demand. Real figures change from year to year and country to country.

3 · Polymers

  • Chain growth: a growing chain end grabs the nearest monomer; at each step it stops with chance 1/(R + 1). In the picture the box only holds a few hundred monomers, so chains also stop when monomers run out; a real sample has about 10²³. The batch runs use an endless supply.
  • Melting point uses Broadhurst's formula for straight chains (fitted to real alkanes, reaching 141 °C for very long poly(ethene) chains). Real poly(ethene) melts lower (105–137 °C) because the chains are branched and not perfectly lined up. The batch melting point applies to poly(ethene) only.
  • A level box (Advanced). Free radicals: a random model that counts molecules (200 of the scarcer reactant) and picks each initiation, propagation or termination step with a chance proportional to its rate; the rate constants are illustrative, chosen to give long chains. Alkene reactions: textbook products and conditions. Polymer disposal: CO₂ and acidic gas per kg are worked out from the formula of the repeat unit (complete combustion); the energies released are approximate literature values (MJ/kg).
  • Left out: branching (LDPE vs HDPE), catalysts such as Ziegler–Natta, cross-linking, condensation polymers (polyesters, nylon), and the end groups left by the initiator.
