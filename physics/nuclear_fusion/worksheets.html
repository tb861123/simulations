Nuclear Fusion · Teacher notesnuclear-fusion.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Why so hot?

What to look for: Both nuclei are positive, so they repel. At room temperature they collide with about 0.04 eV, a millionth of what is needed to get close enough: the chance per collision is effectively zero. At 150 million K they are moving fast enough that some get through. That is why fusion needs a very high temperature.

Investigation 2: The Sun needs quantum physics

What to look for: Without tunnelling, a proton would need 600 keV to get over the barrier, but kT in the Sun's core is only 1.35 keV: the chance is about 10⁻¹⁹⁰, so the Sun could not shine. Quantum tunnelling lets a tiny fraction (about 1 in 10⁵ near the Gamow peak) through. Nuclei behave as waves and can 'leak' through a barrier they cannot climb.

Investigation 3: Hotter is much faster

What to look for: Doubling the kelvin temperature only doubles the mean kinetic energy, but the chance per collision rises about 12 times, because the few nuclei that fuse are in the far tail of the energy spread, and the tail grows very fast with temperature. (Close to 15 million K the rate rises roughly as T⁴: a core 10% hotter fuses about 45% faster. That rule only holds for small changes.) The power per m³ rises only about 2.6 times: at the same pressure a gas twice as hot has half as many nuclei per m³, and the rate goes as the number per m³ squared.

Investigation 4: Squeeze it

What to look for: At the same temperature, twice the pressure means twice as many nuclei per m³, so each nucleus meets twice as many others, and there are twice as many nuclei: the rate rises 2 × 2 = 4 times (rate ∝ n²). This is why stars need gravity to squeeze their cores, and why fusion reactors must confine the plasma.

Investigation 5: The easiest fuel

What to look for: All three have charges of +1, so the barriers are similar, but D–T has by far the largest chance of fusing once the nuclei are close (its S-factor) and releases the most energy (17.6 MeV). ¹H + ¹H needs the weak force to turn a proton into a neutron, so it is about 10²⁴ times slower: fine for the Sun, which is huge and lasts billions of years, hopeless for a power station.

Investigation 6: Bigger charges, hotter stars

What to look for: Carbon has 6 protons, so the barrier is 36 times larger than for two protons (U ∝ Z₁Z₂). The Sun will never be hot enough. Massive stars, squeezed harder by their own gravity, reach hundreds of millions of kelvin and fuse heavier and heavier elements.

Investigation 7: The classical temperature A level

What to look for: U = (8.99 × 10⁹ × (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)²) ÷ 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁵ = 9.6 × 10⁻¹⁴ J (0.6 MeV). ³⁄₂kT = U gives T = 2U ÷ 3k = 4.6 × 10⁹ K, 300 times hotter than the Sun's core (1.6 × 10⁷ K). The difference is made up by the fast tail of the energy spread and by tunnelling.

Investigation 8: Energy from the mass defect A level

What to look for: Mass before 5.030151 u, after 5.011268 u; Δm = 0.018883 u × 931.5 MeV/u = 17.6 MeV. Most of it (14.1 MeV) is carried by the neutron, which is why reactor walls become radioactive.

Investigation 9: The Gamow peak A level

What to look for: e^(−E/kT) says there are fewer and fewer nuclei at high energy; the tunnelling chance e^(−√(E_G/E)) rises steeply with energy. Their product peaks at E₀ = (E_G(kT)²/4)^⅓: here about 30 to 40 keV, several times kT (13 keV) but far below the 400 keV barrier.

Batch experiment 1: Is the Sun hot enough without tunnelling?

With tunnelling, about 1 colliding proton pair in 30 000 gets through the barrier at the Sun's core. Without it, about 1 in 10¹⁹⁰: the Sun would be dark. (Most pairs that do get through fall apart again: the weak force must turn a proton into a neutron while they touch, which is why ¹H + ¹H is so slow.)
Theory (no tunnelling): 3: -1000 · 5: -602 · 8: -375 · 12: -249 · 15.7: -190 · 20: -149 · 25: -119 · 30: -98.4
Result from a test run: 3: -8.45 ± 0 · 5: -6.97 ± 0 · 8: -5.82 ± 0 · 12: -4.97 ± 0 · 15.7: -4.47 ± 0 · 20: -4.06 ± 0 · 25: -3.72 ± 0 · 30: -3.46 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Do the fusing nuclei sit at the Gamow peak?

The fusing nuclei are far above the average energy (³⁄₂kT = 15 keV) but far below the barrier (about 400 keV): exactly where the Gamow peak says.
Theory (exact mean): 46.7
Result from a test run: mean 46.8, SD 0.507, SE 0.0358 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 3: How rare is fusion in a tokamak?

Even in a tokamak only about 3 collisions in a thousand fuse. The rest bounce off, which is why the plasma must be kept hot and confined for a long time.
Theory (model's exact value): 2710
Result from a test run: mean 2740, SD 163, SE 11.5 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 4: How steeply does the Sun's rate rise with temperature?

A gradient of about 4: the Sun's rate ∝ T⁴. A slightly hotter core burns much faster, which keeps stars stable: if the core heats up, it expands and cools again.
Result from a test run: 7: -49.6 ± 0 · 7.1: -49.2 ± 0 · 7.2: -48.8 ± 0 · 7.3: -48.4 ± 0 · 7.4: -48.1 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Why do heavy stars use the CNO cycle?

A gradient of about 16–18: nitrogen's charge of 7 makes the barrier much higher, so the rate is extremely sensitive to temperature. Above about 17 million K (stars heavier than about 1.3 Suns) the CNO cycle wins.
Result from a test run: 7.2: -47.1 ± 0 · 7.3: -45.2 ± 0 · 7.4: -43.5 ± 0 · 7.5: -41.9 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: Squeeze it: does doubling the pressure double the rate?

Gradient 2.00: the rate ∝ n² ∝ P² at fixed temperature. Double the pressure, four times the rate.
Result from a test run: 14: 9.61 ± 0 · 14.5: 10.6 ± 0 · 15: 11.6 ± 0 · 15.5: 12.6 ± 0 · 16: 13.6 ± 0 · 16.5: 14.6 ± 0 · 17: 15.6 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)

Batch experiment 7: How long does a proton in the Sun wait to fuse?

A typical proton waits billions of years. That is why the Sun is still shining after 4.6 billion years: the slowness of ¹H + ¹H is what makes stars last.
Theory (mean τ): 3.6
Result from a test run: mean 3.11, SD 2.88, SE 0.166 from 300 runs
