The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Is the Sun hot enough without quantum tunnelling?

What this shows. The points are the chance per collision of going over the barrier (no tunnelling); the line is the chance with tunnelling. At the Sun's core (107.2 K) the classical chance is about 1 in 10190: there have been only about 1057 protons in the Sun, so the Sun could never shine without tunnelling. The two only meet at billions of kelvin, where nuclei have enough energy to go straight over.

2 · Do the fusing nuclei sit at the Gamow peak?

What this shows. Each run sends a million collisions with random energies from the Boltzmann factor, and records the mean energy of the ones that tunnel through. They cluster tightly around the theory value (about 39 keV), far above the typical energy kT = 10 keV but far below the 444 keV barrier: the Gamow peak. The simple formula E₀ = (E_G (kT)²/4)⅓ gives 31 keV; the exact mean is higher because the peak is lopsided. Runs scatter because each sees only a few thousand lucky collisions.

3 · How rare is a fusing collision in a tokamak?

What this shows. Only about 1 collision in 1,400 gets through, even at ten times the Sun's core temperature. The random counts scatter around the theory value with a standard deviation close to √(count)/N, as for any rare random event (Poisson statistics). This is why a reactor must hold its plasma for seconds: each nucleus needs hundreds of tries.

4 · How steeply does the Sun's fusion rate rise with temperature?

What this shows. The rate rises roughly as T4 near the Sun's core (gradient ≈ 4 on these log–log axes): doubling the temperature makes it about 16 times faster. The Gamow-peak formula (line) agrees with the full calculation (points) to within a few per cent. A small rise in core temperature makes a star much brighter, which is why the Sun's core acts like a thermostat.

5 · Why do heavy stars use the CNO cycle?

What this shows. Carbon has six protons, so its barrier is much higher and the rate climbs far more steeply: about T16 at 20 million K (compare about T4 for p + p); the gradient fitted over the whole range is about 15. Below about 17 million K the pp chain wins; in hotter, more massive stars the CNO cycle (with carbon as a catalyst) takes over.

6 · Squeeze it: does doubling the pressure double the rate?

What this shows. At a fixed temperature the number of nuclei per m³ is proportional to pressure, and each reaction needs two nuclei, so the rate goes as n² ∝ P²: a gradient of 2. Doubling the pressure makes fusion four times faster. Stars get their huge pressure from gravity; reactors use magnetic fields (tokamaks) or laser-driven implosions (NIF).

7 · How long does a proton in the Sun wait to fuse?

What this shows. Each proton's waiting time is random, with an exponential spread like radioactive decay; the average is a few billion years in this model (real estimates are about 10 billion years, with the Sun's actual mixture of hydrogen and helium). That is why the Sun can shine steadily for 10 billion years: most of its protons are still waiting.
