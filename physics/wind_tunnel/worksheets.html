Wind Tunnel · Teacher noteswind-tunnel.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Where does lift come from?

What to look for: The tilted wing turns the airflow downwards; by Newton's third law the air pushes the wing up. In the pressure view you can see the same thing another way: lower pressure above the wing, higher below. Both descriptions are correct: they are two views of one flow.

Investigation 2: The price of lift

What to look for: Beyond about 12–15° the air can no longer follow the top surface: it separates and the wing starts shedding vortices, so the forces wobble. Drag climbs much faster than lift, so lift ÷ drag peaks at a modest angle and then falls. On a full-size aircraft wing the same separation makes the lift itself collapse suddenly: a stall. This small, slow wing (low Reynolds number) loses efficiency instead of dropping out of the sky, much as an insect's wing does.

Investigation 3: The singing wire

What to look for: Vortices peel off alternately from top and bottom: a Kármán vortex street. Each one tugs the cylinder sideways, so the lift oscillates. The frequency is proportional to wind speed (and inversely to diameter), which is why wires hum in the wind, and why a steady wind destroyed the Tacoma Narrows bridge.

Investigation 4: Streamlining

What to look for: The teardrop has several times less drag than the plate. Most of the drag of a blunt shape comes from the low-pressure wake behind it, so tapering the back to let the flow close up gently matters most. That is why fast cars, fish and cycling helmets taper at the rear.

Investigation 5: Syrup or air?

What to look for: At low Reynolds number viscosity damps every wobble and the flow is smooth and steady (laminar), like honey round a spoon. Above about 45 the wake becomes unstable and vortices shed. The Reynolds number Re = speed × size ÷ viscosity decides which regime you are in: a bacterium and a whale live in different worlds.

Investigation 6: Slipstreaming

What to look for: The rider behind sits in the slow, low-pressure wake of the leader and meets much less oncoming air, so the total drag is far less than double. Racing cyclists save up to 30–40% of their effort by drafting; geese fly in a V for a related reason.

Investigation 7: Design a better wing

What to look for: A little camber raises lift at the same angle for little extra drag. Very thick wings add drag; very thin ones stall early. Lift-to-drag ratio is the figure of merit: it is the distance a glider travels forward for every metre it sinks.

Batch experiment 1: Lift curve

Each run starts from slightly different random swirls and averages over a different stretch of time, which matters once the wing is shedding vortices (at high angles). The measured lift rises with angle but falls further and further below the frictionless theory: at this low Reynolds number the boundary layer is thick and the flow separates from the top of the wing. Full-size wings at high Reynolds number follow the theory line closely up to about 15°, then stall abruptly.
Theory (thin-wing theory with no viscosity): -4: -0.208 · 0: 0.23 · 4: 0.669 · 8: 1.11 · 12: 1.55 · 16: 1.98 · 22: 2.64 · 28: 3.3
Result from a test run: -4: -0.292 ± 6.80e-6 · 0: 0.0327 ± 2.20e-6 · 4: 0.41 ± 8.93e-5 · 8: 0.713 ± 2.97e-4 · 12: 0.89 ± 1.08e-4 · 16: 1.18 ± 2.09e-5 · 22: 1.35 ± 2.55e-4 · 28: 1.8 ± 0.00762 (mean ± SE, 2 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Streamlining and drag

Blunt shapes shed vortices, so their drag fluctuates and the average depends on which stretch of time is measured. At this low Reynolds number skin friction is a large part of the drag, so very long shapes stop improving; at high Reynolds number the gains from streamlining are bigger still.
Result from a test run: 0.3: 2.29 ± 0.0148 · 0.6: 2.14 ± 0.0654 · 1: 1.83 ± 0.00405 · 1.5: 1.6 ± 0.0175 · 2: 1.46 ± 3.52e-4 · 3: 1.44 ± 2.37e-4 · 4: 1.55 ± 9.29e-4 (mean ± SE, 2 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Vortex shedding frequency

A straight line through the origin means frequency ∝ speed: the Strouhal relationship f = St·v/D, with St ≈ 0.2 for a cylinder. The count is of whole oscillations in a fixed time, so it can be out by a fraction of a cycle.
Result from a test run: 0.05: 0.876 ± 0.0128 · 0.07: 1.24 ± 0.00712 · 0.09: 1.6 ± 0.0083 · 0.11: 1.92 ± 0.0083 · 0.13: 2.28 ± 0.0261 (mean ± SE, 2 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Drag and speed

A straight line through the origin means drag ∝ speed²: doubling your cycling speed takes four times the force, and eight times the power.
Result from a test run: 0.0025: 0.0277 ± 9.92e-5 · 0.0049: 0.0559 ± 3.46e-4 · 0.0081: 0.0926 ± 7.14e-4 · 0.0121: 0.133 ± 9.97e-4 · 0.0169: 0.189 ± 0.00455 (mean ± SE, 2 runs each)
