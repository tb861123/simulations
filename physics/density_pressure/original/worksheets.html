Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · One aluminium block, ruler

What this shows. The same block measured 300 times with a ruler. The mean is very close to the data-book value (2700 kg/m³), but single results scatter with an SD of about 4%: each side is read to the nearest millimetre, and a 1 mm error in a 1.8 cm side is over 5%. The blue curve is the spread predicted from the reading errors.

2 · Small blocks are less precise

What this shows. The results scatter far more for small blocks: the same ±1 mm reading error is a much bigger fraction of a 1 cm side than of a 6 cm side. Measure bigger objects, or use a more precise instrument (vernier callipers), to reduce the percentage uncertainty.

4 · Eureka can: a systematic error

What this shows. Some water always stays in the spout and on the sides of the eureka can, so the measured volume is too small and the density too big: the mean is about 3% above the true value however many times you repeat. Repeating reduces random errors, not systematic ones. Change the method to the measuring cylinder and run again: the mean moves onto the true value.

6 · Pressure and depth

What this shows. The sensor reading rises in a straight line with depth: p = hρg. The gradient of the best-fit line is ρg ≈ 9.79 kPa per metre for water, so ρ = gradient ÷ g ≈ 998 kg/m³. Each reading scatters by about 0.1 kPa (the sensor).

7 · Floating: how much is under? (beyond 4PH1)

What this shows. A floating cube sinks until the water it pushes aside weighs as much as it does, so the fraction under water = ρ_cube ÷ ρ_water: a straight line up to 100%. Anything denser than water (998 kg/m³) sinks completely.

8 · Archimedes: upthrust = weight of water displaced

What this shows. Weigh an object on a spring balance in air and then under water: the difference is the upthrust. It equals the weight of the water pushed aside, ρ_water × V × g, about 0.98 N for every 100 cm³, whatever the object is made of. The spring balance reads to ±0.05 N, so small objects give scattered results.

9 · Gas: pressure and number of particles

What this shows. Twice the particles (twice the density) gives twice the pressure: p = NkT/V, a straight line through the origin. Each run averages the force on the wall over 20 time units (thousands of hits), so the averages agree closely with the theory; moment by moment the force fluctuates a lot, especially with few particles (see the Pressure gauge view). In a real gas there are about 10²² molecules in every litre, so the pressure is perfectly steady.

10 · Terminal velocity and radius (beyond the spec)

What this shows. Stokes' law predicts v ∝ r² (gradient 2 on log–log axes). Small balls follow it, but the tube walls slow every ball (more for big ones) and the biggest balls are too fast for Stokes' law, so the measured points fall below the ideal line and the gradient is less than 2. Use a wide tube and small balls in the real practical.
