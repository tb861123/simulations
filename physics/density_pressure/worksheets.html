Density and Pressure · Teacher notesdensity-pressure.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Density of a block

What to look for: Each side is read to the nearest mm, and a 1 mm error in 20 mm is 5%. The volume multiplies three sides, so their errors add up. Take a mean of repeats.

Investigation 2: Small blocks, big errors

What to look for: The 6 cm block: the same ±1 mm reading error is a much smaller percentage of a bigger length. Measure big things where you can.

Investigation 3: Irregular objects

What to look for: The eureka can keeps about 1 cm³ of water in its spout, so the volume is always too small and the density too big: a systematic error that repeating does not remove.

Investigation 4: Snowshoes

What to look for: Same weight (force) over about 7 times the area, so about 1/7 of the pressure: below the 3 kPa that soft snow can hold.

Investigation 5: Pressure and depth

What to look for: Gradient = ρg = 9.79 kPa per metre for water. The reading is the same whichever way the sensor faces: pressure acts in all directions.

Investigation 6: Why are dams thicker at the bottom?

What to look for: Δp = hρg: every 10 m of water adds about 100 kPa (about 1 atmosphere). At the bottom of a dam the pressure is greatest, so the wall must be thickest there.

Investigation 7: Floating and sinking

What to look for: Fraction under = ρcube ÷ ρwater. It sinks when its density is more than the liquid's. In mercury even steel floats!

Investigation 8: Terminal velocity

What to look for: Drag grows with speed. When weight = upthrust + drag, the resultant force is zero, so (Newton's first law) the speed stays constant: terminal velocity.

Investigation 9: Percentage uncertainty A level

What to look for: % uncertainty in V = 3 × % uncertainty in the side; add the mass's. Ruler: 3 × 0.5/15 = 10%; vernier: 3 × 0.05/15 = 1%.

Investigation 10: Viscosity from falling spheres A level

What to look for: Stokes: v = 2r²g(ρs − ρl)/(9η), so the gradient is 2g(ρs − ρl)/(9η). Big balls fall a little slower than Stokes predicts (walls and inertia), so use the small ones.

Batch experiment 1: One aluminium block, measured by many students

Centred on 2700 with an SD of about 135 kg/m³ (predicted from the reading errors): random error, which averaging reduces.
Theory (data book): 2700
Result from a test run: mean 2710, SD 137, SE 7.93 from 300 runs

Batch experiment 2: Small blocks are less precise

The means stay near 2700 but the SD falls roughly as 1 ÷ side: the same 1 mm reading is a smaller fraction of a bigger block.
Theory (2700): 1: 2700 · 2: 2700 · 3: 2700 · 4: 2700 · 5: 2700 · 6: 2700
Result from a test run: 1: 2640 ± 43 · 2: 2740 ± 21 · 3: 2690 ± 14.6 · 4: 2710 ± 16.6 · 5: 2700 ± 9.48 · 6: 2700 ± 6.83 (mean ± SE, 30 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Ruler or vernier callipers? (A level)

SD about 57 kg/m³ (predicted), about ten times smaller than with a ruler (about 570).
Theory (data book): 8500
Result from a test run: mean 8500, SD 57.3, SE 3.31 from 300 runs

Batch experiment 4: Eureka can: a systematic error

The mean is about 3% too high however many times you repeat: a systematic error. Fix the method (wet the spout first), not the number of repeats.
Theory (true): 2700
Result from a test run: mean 2790, SD 43.9, SE 2.53 from 300 runs
