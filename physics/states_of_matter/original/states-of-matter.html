📐 Physics notes

Spec reference. Advanced: WJEC Eduqas A level Physics, thermal physics (specific heat capacity and specific latent heat; the old page cited Component 2, Topic 3). Standard: Edexcel International GCSE Physics 4PH1, Section 5(c) Change of state (particles, heating, temperature–time graphs, specific heat capacity). Chemistry: Edexcel International GCSE Chemistry 4CH1, Topic 1, states of matter.

Key equations:

Q = mcΔT (sensible heat: temperature change)
Q = mL (latent heat: change of state at constant T)
Q = Pt ⇒ t = Q/P (heating at power P)
Q_lost = Q_gained (method of mixtures)

Specific heat capacity c is the energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 K: Q = mcΔT. Different states of matter have different values of c (ice, liquid water and steam all differ).

Specific latent heat L is the energy needed to change the state of 1 kg of a substance without any change in temperature: Q = mL. The latent heat of fusion L_f applies to melting and freezing; the latent heat of vaporisation L_v applies to boiling and condensing.

The heating curve plots temperature against energy supplied. It has sloping sections (where Q = mcΔT raises the temperature) separated by flat plateaus at the melting and boiling points. During a plateau the temperature stays constant even though energy is still being supplied: the energy goes into breaking bonds (increasing potential energy), not into kinetic energy. With a heater of power P, the time for any stage is t = Q/P since Q = Pt.

Why the temperature plateaus. Temperature is a measure of the mean kinetic energy of the particles. During a change of state the supplied energy does work against the intermolecular forces, separating particles and increasing their potential energy, so the mean kinetic energy (and so the temperature) does not change until the change of state is complete.

Latent heat of vaporisation > fusion. For most substances L_v ≫ L_f because boiling must completely separate the particles against the full intermolecular attraction, whereas melting only loosens the rigid lattice. For water L_v ≈ 2.26 MJ/kg against L_f ≈ 0.334 MJ/kg.

Method of mixtures. When a hot body is put in contact with a cold body in an insulated system, energy is conserved: the heat lost by the hot body equals the heat gained by the cold body, Q_lost = Q_gained. Solving m₁c₁(T₁ − T_f) = m₂c₂(T_f − T₂) gives the final temperature T_f. This is the basis of the electrical method and method of mixtures experiments used to measure specific heat capacities.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Energy: the computer keeps track of the energy Q supplied since the start. The heating curve is built from Q = mcΔT for each state (with its own c) and Q = mL for each change of state, so the temperature and the fraction melted or boiled follow from Q.
  • Heater: supplies energy at P watts (P × time). Cooling removes energy at the same rate. Insulation: heat also flows to or from a room at 20 °C at a rate (loss) × (T − 20) watts. Real heat loss also depends on surface area, stirring and radiation.
  • Particles: speed ∝ √T (T in kelvin), because the mean kinetic energy ∝ T. Solid particles vibrate about lattice sites, with bigger vibrations when hotter. Liquid particles stay touching but slide past each other. Gas particles fly freely and bounce off the walls; their hits on the walls cause pressure.
  • Changes of state: the fraction of particles melted (or boiled) equals the fraction of the latent heat supplied. Particles at the surface change first. Real melting starts at the surface too; real boiling makes bubbles all through the liquid (shown as rising bubbles).
  • Simplifications: the particle picture is 2D and has far fewer particles than a real substance (a gram of water has 3 × 10²² molecules), and it is not to scale: real gas particles are about 10 times further apart than drawn. The specific heat capacities are taken as constant in each state, the pressure is 1 atmosphere, the whole substance is at one temperature (perfectly stirred), and the container's own heat capacity is ignored. Gas particles do not collide with each other here. Aluminium is only heated to 60 °C above its melting point (it boils at 2470 °C).
  • Data: typical values (J/kg/K, J/kg, °C). Iron: c 450 / 820 / 370, L_f 247 kJ/kg, L_v 6.09 MJ/kg, m.p. 1538 °C, b.p. 2862 °C.
  • Batch runs: model class experiments (electrical method, time to boil, melting ice, method of mixtures) with random thermometer and timing errors (normal distributions) and heat lost to or gained from the room.
