States of Matter and Heating · Teacher notesstates-of-matter.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Ice to steam

What to look for: Flat part 1: melting at 0 °C. Flat part 2: boiling at 100 °C. While a substance changes state, the energy supplied breaks the bonds between particles instead of making them move faster, so the temperature stays the same until the change is complete.

Investigation 2: Why is boiling so long?

What to look for: The specific latent heat of vaporisation (2 260 000 J/kg) is about 7 times the latent heat of fusion (334 000 J/kg). Melting only loosens the particles; boiling must pull them completely apart, against all their attractions.

Investigation 3: Particles in each state

What to look for: Solid: regular arrangement, touching, vibrating about fixed positions. Liquid: random arrangement, touching, sliding past each other. Gas: random, far apart, moving fast in all directions and hitting the walls (that is gas pressure). Higher temperature: faster particles.

Investigation 4: A cooling curve

What to look for: At 100 °C the steam condenses and at 0 °C the water freezes. While a substance condenses or freezes, it gives out latent heat as bonds form, so the temperature stays constant even though energy is being removed.

Investigation 5: Specific heat capacity practical

What to look for: Q = 50 W × 600 s = 30 000 J; ΔT ≈ 33 °C; c = 30 000 ÷ (1 × 33) ≈ 900 J/(kg K). In a real experiment some energy is lost to the surroundings, so ΔT is smaller and c comes out too large: insulate the block (and check the Batch run).

Investigation 6: Poor insulation

What to look for: Heat lost = 8 W/K × (T − 20). It levels off when the loss equals the 500 W supplied: 8(T − 20) = 500, T ≈ 82 °C. It never boils. Insulation matters.

Investigation 7: Lead heats up fast

What to look for: Lead's specific heat capacity (128 J/(kg K)) is about 33 times smaller than water's (4200), so the same energy raises its temperature 33 times more. Water's high c is why it is used in central heating and why the sea warms and cools slowly.

Investigation 8: Specific latent heat A level

What to look for: Q = mL = 1 × 2.26 × 10⁶ = 2.26 MJ; t = Q ÷ P = 1130 s, about 19 minutes. (Heating it from 20 °C to 100 °C takes only 336 kJ, under 3 minutes.)

Investigation 9: Method of mixtures A level

What to look for: Heat lost by the copper = heat gained by the water: 0.2 × 385 × (100 − T) = 0.2 × 4200 × (T − 20), so T = 26.7 °C. The internal energy of the copper falls and that of the water rises by the same amount (if no energy is lost).

Batch experiment 1: Measuring c of water: what do heat losses do?

With no losses the answer scatters around 4200. As losses grow the water warms less, so ΔT is too small and c comes out too big. Insulating the beaker reduces this systematic error.
Theory (true c): 0: 4200 · 0.25: 4200 · 0.5: 4200 · 1: 4200 · 1.5: 4200 · 2: 4200
Result from a test run: 0: 4230 ± 37.2 · 0.25: 4540 ± 31 · 0.5: 4840 ± 20.2 · 1: 5460 ± 24.5 · 1.5: 6350 ± 57.9 · 2: 7080 ± 55.2 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: How precise is one measurement of c?

Random errors spread the answers by about ±1.5% around the true value. Averaging repeats (or using a bigger temperature rise) reduces the spread.
Theory (true c): 4200
Result from a test run: mean 4200, SD 64.8, SE 4.58 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 3: Does doubling the mass double the time to boil?

A straight line through the origin: time ∝ mass. The gradient (168 s per kg) is c × ΔT ÷ P.
Theory (mcΔT ÷ P): 0.2: 33.6 · 0.5: 84 · 0.8: 134 · 1.1: 185 · 1.4: 235 · 1.7: 286 · 2: 336
Result from a test run: 0.2: 34 ± 0.226 · 0.5: 83.9 ± 0.246 · 0.8: 134 ± 0.262 · 1.1: 185 ± 0.164 · 1.4: 236 ± 0.246 · 1.7: 286 ± 0.226 · 2: 336 ± 0.243 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Time to boil against heater power

Doubling the power halves the time (t ∝ 1/P) for powerful heaters. Weak heaters take longer than the theory, because a bigger share of their power leaks away while they work.
Theory (no heat loss): 300: 1120 · 500: 672 · 750: 448 · 1000: 336 · 1500: 224 · 2000: 168 · 3000: 112
Result from a test run: 300: 1300 ± 0.145 · 500: 732 ± 0.211 · 750: 474 ± 0.0912 · 1000: 351 ± 0.222 · 1500: 231 ± 0.34 · 2000: 172 ± 0.314 · 3000: 114 ± 0.342 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Measuring the latent heat of fusion of ice

Energy leaking in from the room helps melt the ice, so it melts sooner and L comes out too small. The usual fix: run a control funnel with no heater and subtract the ice it melts.
Theory (true L): 0: 3.34e+5 · 0.5: 3.34e+5 · 1: 3.34e+5 · 1.5: 3.34e+5 · 2: 3.34e+5
Result from a test run: 0: 3.34e+5 ± 246 · 0.5: 3.04e+5 ± 283 · 1: 2.79e+5 ± 222 · 1.5: 2.57e+5 ± 135 · 2: 2.39e+5 ± 99.3 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: Method of mixtures (copper into water)

The mean is a little below the ideal value: a systematic error, because the block always loses some heat on the way. Random errors alone would scatter evenly around the ideal.
Theory (ideal): 26.7
Result from a test run: mean 26.5, SD 0.319, SE 0.0226 from 200 runs
