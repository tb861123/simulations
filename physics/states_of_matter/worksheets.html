Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Measuring c of water: what do heat losses do?

What this shows. 0.5 kg of water heated by a 50 W heater for 10 minutes; the student calculates c = Pt/(mΔT). With perfect insulation the results scatter around the true 4200 J/kg/K (the scatter comes from reading the thermometer). Heat lost to the room makes ΔT smaller, so the calculated c comes out too big, and the more heat is lost the bigger the error. Insulating (lagging) the beaker reduces this systematic error; repeating does not.

2 · How precise is one measurement of c?

What this shows. The same experiment with no heat loss, done by 200 students. Each reads the thermometer twice with a random error (SD 0.5 °C) on a temperature rise of about 14 °C, so each answer is uncertain by about √2 × 0.5/14 ≈ 5%. The histogram matches the predicted normal curve and its centre matches 4200 J/kg/K: random errors average out over many repeats.

3 · Does doubling the mass double the time to boil?

What this shows. With no heat loss, the time to reach 100 °C is t = mcΔT/P = m × 4200 × 80 / 2000 = 168 s for every kilogram: a straight line through the origin. Doubling the mass doubles the energy needed, so it doubles the time.

4 · Time to boil against heater power

What this shows. Theory (no loss): t = Q/P, a straight line of gradient −1 on log–log axes. Here the water also loses 1 W per kelvin above room temperature. A powerful heater finishes before much heat escapes, so it matches t = Q/P closely; a weak heater takes so long that the losses add up, and the time is noticeably longer than Q/P. That is why a kettle has a powerful element.

5 · Measuring the latent heat of fusion of ice

What this shows. Ice at 0 °C melted by a 100 W heater; the student times how long it takes and works out L_f = Pt/m. Here the room is warmer than the ice, so the room also supplies heat and the ice melts faster than the heater alone would melt it. The calculated L_f comes out too small: the opposite of the heat-capacity experiment. A control beaker of ice without a heater can measure the room's contribution.

6 · Method of mixtures: where does the energy go?

What this shows. 0.2 kg of copper at 100 °C dropped into 0.25 kg of water at 20 °C. The ideal final temperature is 25.5 °C. Each block cools by a random 0–10 °C while it is carried across, so the measured final temperatures are on average a little lower, and the thermometer adds scatter. Copper's small c (385 J/kg/K) means even a big drop in the block's temperature shifts the water's by only a fraction of a degree.
