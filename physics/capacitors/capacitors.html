How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Ideal components. The capacitor has a fixed capacitance and (unless you add leakage) perfect insulation. The resistor obeys Ohm's law. Wires and the switch have no resistance; the cell has no internal resistance (it would add to R).
  • Exact formulas. V, Q and I come straight from the exponential equations; nothing is stepped numerically.
  • Animation time is scaled. A full charge or discharge (5τ) always takes about 3 s at 1×, whatever τ is; the clock shows the real time in the circuit. So a camera flash (τ ≈ 0.5 s) and a 100 s lab capacitor look equally fast.
  • Moving charges show the direction of the current and roughly how big it is (they slow down and fade as the current dies away). No charge crosses the gap between the plates: charge piles up on one plate and leaves the other. Tick "Show electrons" to see the electrons, which move the other way.
  • "Fully" charged is taken as 5τ (99.3%); in theory an exponential never quite gets there.
  • Leaky capacitor: leakage resistance Rleak in parallel with C, applied to discharge only (as in the old app). It is drawn as a dotted curve on the V graph.
  • Energy: charging from a fixed supply always wastes half the energy taken from the supply (QV₀) as heat in the resistor, whatever the value of R.
  • Real capacitors have a tolerance (often ±10–20%), a maximum working voltage and some internal resistance; electrolytic capacitors also leak noticeably and must be connected the right way round.
  • Batch runs model a class doing the experiment: a data logger with random noise on each voltage reading, a stopwatch with random reaction times and, if set, capacitors that differ from their labelled value. Each run is worked out from the exact formulas.
