Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Measure τ from one discharge (data logger)

What this shows. 100 μF and 100 kΩ, so RC = 10 s. Each run a data logger takes 20 readings over 2τ, each with a little random noise (0.5% of V₀), and τ comes from the gradient of ln V against t (gradient = −1/RC). The results cluster tightly round 10 s: fitting a line to many readings averages out the noise.

2 · Stopwatch at 37%: how precise?

What this shows. Now each student starts a stopwatch when the switch moves and stops it when the voltmeter reads 37% of V₀. Each press is early or late by a random reaction time (SD 0.2 s), so each result is off by the difference of two errors: the predicted spread is √2 × 0.2 = 0.28 s. The mean is still close to RC = 10 s: reaction times make results less precise but not less accurate.

What this shows. R from 1 kΩ to about 1 MΩ with C = 100 μF. On log–log axes τ ∝ R is a straight line of gradient 1: doubling R doubles the time constant. This is why a big resistor makes a slow timer circuit.

5 · Half-life: is t½ = RC ln 2?

What this shows. The time to fall to half of V₀, timed by hand. It rises in a straight line with R; the gradient is C ln 2 = 100×10⁻⁶ × 0.693 = 6.93×10⁻⁵ s per Ω. Any exponential decay halves in the same time whatever it starts from, just like radioactive decay.

6 · How much does leakage shorten τ?

What this shows. C = 10 μF discharging through R = 1 MΩ (RC = 10 s), with a leakage resistance in parallel. When R_leak ≪ R the charge escapes mostly through the leak and τ_eff ≈ R_leak·C. When R_leak ≫ R the leak hardly matters and τ_eff → RC = 10 s. In between, τ_eff = R·R_leak·C/(R + R_leak), the same as R and R_leak in parallel.
