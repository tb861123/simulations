Atomic Spectra and Energy Levels · Teacher notesatomic-spectra.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The visible spectrum

What to look for: Violet (about 400 nm) to red (about 700 nm). Shorter wavelength means higher frequency (v = fλ, the same speed for all) and more energetic photons: ultraviolet beyond violet, infrared beyond red.

Investigation 2: Hydrogen's lines

What to look for: Drops to n = 2 (the Balmer series) give visible lines: H-α red 656 nm (3 → 2), H-β blue-green 486 nm, H-γ and H-δ violet. Drops to n = 1 are ultraviolet (Lyman); to n = 3 infrared (Paschen).

Investigation 3: Calculate a line

What to look for: E₄ = −0.85 eV, E₂ = −3.40 eV, ΔE = 2.55 eV = 4.09 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; λ = hc ÷ ΔE = 1240 ÷ 2.55 = 486 nm (H-β, blue-green).

Investigation 4: Absorption lines

What to look for: Only photons whose energy exactly matches a jump up from the lowest level(s) can be absorbed. The atom re-emits in a random direction, so those colours are dark in the beam: the same wavelengths as the emission lines.

Investigation 5: Fingerprints of elements

What to look for: Each element has its own energy levels, so its own pattern of lines, like a fingerprint. Sodium's strong yellow pair (589 nm) dominates street lamps; helium was discovered in the Sun's spectrum before it was found on Earth.

Investigation 6: Ionisation

What to look for: 13.6 eV (from n = 1 to 0 eV). The Lyman lines crowd towards 1240 ÷ 13.6 = 91 nm: the series limit.

Investigation 7: Diffraction grating A level

What to look for: d = 1/600 mm = 1667 nm; sin θ = 656 ÷ 1667 → θ = 23.2°. More lines per mm spread the lines further apart, so the same angle error gives a smaller wavelength error.

Investigation 8: Laser: population inversion A level

What to look for: 3-level: the lower laser level is the ground state, so more than half of all atoms must be pumped up (pump × lifetime > 1). 4-level: the lower laser level empties quickly, so almost any pumping gives an inversion: much more efficient.

Batch experiment 1: Do the Balmer lines fit Rydberg's formula?

The lines crowd together towards 365 nm (the series limit) as n rises.
Theory (Rydberg formula): 3: 656 · 4: 486 · 5: 434 · 6: 410 · 7: 397 · 8: 389 · 9: 384
Result from a test run: 3: 657 ± 0.867 · 4: 486 ± 0.984 · 5: 434 ± 0.838 · 6: 411 ± 0.817 · 7: 397 ± 0.962 · 8: 389 ± 0.954 · 9: 384 ± 0.75 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Measure the Rydberg constant

The class average is very close to 1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹; individual values scatter by about 1% because of the angle readings.
Theory (R_H): 1.1
Result from a test run: mean 1.1, SD 0.00341, SE 2.41e-4 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 3: The Lyman series converges to 13.6 eV

The energies approach 13.6 eV, the ionisation energy: above it the electron is free and any energy can be absorbed (a continuum).
Theory (13.6(1 − 1/n²)): 2: 10.2 · 3: 12.1 · 4: 12.8 · 5: 13.1 · 6: 13.2 · 8: 13.4 · 10: 13.5 · 15: 13.5 · 20: 13.6
Result from a test run: 2: 10.2 ± 0 · 3: 12.1 ± 0 · 4: 12.8 ± 0 · 5: 13.1 ± 0 · 6: 13.2 ± 0 · 8: 13.4 ± 0 · 10: 13.5 ± 0 · 15: 13.5 ± 0 · 20: 13.6 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 1 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: How fine a grating?

A finer grating spreads the spectrum over bigger angles, so the same reading error matters less.
Theory (d cos θ × Δθ × √(2/π)): 100: 13.9 · 300: 4.55 · 600: 2.13 · 900: 1.25 · 1200: 0.715
Result from a test run: 100: 12.7 ± 1.6 · 300: 4.93 ± 0.594 · 600: 1.88 ± 0.22 · 900: 1.45 ± 0.165 · 1200: 0.641 ± 0.0634 (mean ± SE, 40 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: How many photons can we see?

Most photons from hydrogen are ultraviolet or infrared: only drops that end on n = 2 are visible.
Theory (exact average): 21.9
Result from a test run: mean 22.1, SD 2.74, SE 0.194 from 200 runs
