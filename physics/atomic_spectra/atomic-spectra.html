The discrete spectral lines of hydrogen are fingerprints: astronomers use them to find the composition, temperature and motion of distant stars.

Ionisation energy

From the ground state (n = 1), the energy needed to remove the electron completely is 13.6 eV (the ionisation energy of hydrogen).

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Hydrogen levels: the Bohr model, En = −13.6/n² eV. Photon wavelength λ = hc/ΔE using hc ≈ 1240 eV nm, as on the old WJEC page. This gives H-α at 656.5 nm; the measured value is 656.3 nm in air (light is slightly slower in air than in a vacuum).
  • Series shown: Lyman, Balmer and Paschen (lower levels 1, 2 and 3), up to nmax. "All" shows these three together.
  • Other elements: helium, sodium and neon use measured line wavelengths with rough relative brightness. Their energy levels are more complicated (several electrons repel each other), so the picture of their atom is only a sketch; each line is still one jump between two levels, with ΔE = hc/λ.
  • The atom picture is not to scale: real Bohr orbits have radius rn = n²a₀ (a₀ = 0.0529 nm), so the n = 8 orbit is 64 times the n = 1 orbit. Here the spacing is squashed so that all the orbits fit. Electrons do not really move in neat circles: quantum mechanics only gives the probability of finding them in a region.
  • Random jumps: in "discharge tube" mode a collision lifts the electron to a random higher level; it then drops to any lower level with equal chance, one photon per drop. Real transition probabilities are not equal (they come from quantum mechanics), so real line brightnesses differ.
  • Absorption: white light (all wavelengths) passes through cool gas. Only photons with exactly the right energy for a jump up from the resting level are absorbed. The excited atom soon re-emits the energy in a random direction, so less light of that wavelength continues forwards: a dark line.
  • Batch runs: a diffraction-grating spectrometer, d sin θ = λ (first order), with a random error in each angle reading (normal distribution).
  • Left out: fine structure (lines split by electron spin), the width of lines (Doppler broadening and the uncertainty principle), the recoil of the atom, the reduced mass of the electron, and how bright each line really is.
