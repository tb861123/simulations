Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · How precise is one cooling-can result?

What this shows. The same experiment 300 times. The mean fall is very close to the model's true fall (tiny standard error), but single cans scatter with an SD of about half a degree: two thermometer readings, each read to the nearest 0.5 °C, and water that starts a little hotter or cooler. A difference of 1 °C between two single cans means very little: repeat, or use a digital thermometer.

2 · Layers of cotton wool

What this shows. Each extra layer adds thermal resistance, so the can cools more slowly; but each layer helps less than the one before (diminishing returns), because heat still escapes through the top and base and through the outer surface. Scatter comes from the wrapping (±15%), the starting temperature and the thermometer.

3 · Which insulator is best?

What this shows. One layer of each material. Cotton wool (fibres trapping lots of air) is best, then felt and bubble wrap; one sheet of newspaper helps a little. Shiny foil cuts radiation but conducts, so it helps less than the fluffy materials. With a liquid-in-glass thermometer the differences between felt and bubble wrap are close to the measuring error: repeats are needed to tell them apart.

4 · Starting temperature and cooling

What this shows. The hotter the water, the faster it cools: the rate of heat loss depends on the temperature difference from the room. With no lid the very hot cans lose much more (evaporation rises steeply with temperature), so the line curves upwards. This is why a fair test needs the same starting temperature.

5 · Leslie cube: the four faces

What this shows. Matt black emits most, shiny silver least. Matt white is nearly as good an emitter as black in the infrared (surprising, but true of real white paint): it is the shininess, not the colour, that matters most for infrared. Readings scatter by about 3% from air currents and pointing the detector.

7 · Loft insulation: how thick?

What this shows. The first 100 mm saves most; going from 200 to 300 mm saves only a little more (diminishing returns), because U = 1/(R_ceiling + thickness/k) and the rest of the house still loses heat. Each run is a different winter (UK average ±0.8 °C), which changes the bill by about ±£80: compare with the theory line for an average year. That is why one year's bill is a poor test of whether insulation worked.

9 · Colder days use more gas

What this shows. Gas used in a day falls in a straight line as the outside temperature rises: energy = H × (T_inside − T_outside) × time ÷ efficiency, minus the free heat from people and appliances. The gradient is −24 H ÷ (1000 × efficiency) kWh per °C. This is the 'degree-day' method energy managers use. Points scatter because wind changes the draughts and people come and go.

10 · Newton's cooling: τ from an ln graph (A level)

What this shows. Each run reads a can every minute for 30 minutes and fits a straight line to ln(T − 20) against t: τ = −1/gradient. The mean agrees with τ fitted to perfect readings (yellow), but both are a little different from C/(UA) at the start (grey in the table) because the cooling is not exactly exponential: radiation and evaporation grow faster than (T − 20). Newton's law is beyond the spec; the method is the same as capacitor discharge.
