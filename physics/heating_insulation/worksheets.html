Heating and Insulation · Teacher notesheating-insulation.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Which insulator is best?

What to look for: Cotton wool (about 4 °C fall against 9 °C bare): it traps the most air, a very poor conductor that cannot convect when trapped in small pockets.

Investigation 2: More layers

What to look for: No: 9.1, 4.3, 2.8, 2.0 … °C. Each layer adds the same resistance, but the saving gets smaller: diminishing returns (like loft insulation).

Investigation 3: Take the lid off

What to look for: Without a lid, energy is lost by evaporation and convection from the water surface. Evaporation grows quickly with temperature, so hot drinks cool fast at first.

Investigation 4: The Leslie cube

What to look for: Matt black emits most and shiny silver least (about 1.35 mV against 0.10 mV), even though all four faces are at the same temperature.

Investigation 5: Black or shiny: the coin drop

What to look for: Matt black absorbs infrared best and drops first (about 3 minutes); shiny silver reflects most and may never get hot enough. Good emitters are good absorbers.

Investigation 6: Loft insulation

What to look for: About £500 a year, most of it in the first 100 mm. With a well-insulated loft the walls and air leaks lose the most: cavity wall insulation and draught-proofing are next.

Investigation 7: Turn the thermostat down

What to look for: About 9% (roughly £130) for each degree: heat loss is proportional to the temperature difference with outside.

Investigation 8: Newton's law of cooling (beyond the spec) A level

What to look for: Yes (with a lid): gradient −k, so τ = 1/k ≈ 3700 s for the bare can. The same skill as capacitor discharge and radioactive decay.

Batch experiment 1: How precise is one cooling-can result?

Mean about 9.1 °C, SD about 0.5 °C: two cans must differ by more than about 1 °C before you can be confident one insulator is better.
Theory (model): 9.07
Result from a test run: mean 9.05, SD 0.377, SE 0.0217 from 300 runs

Batch experiment 2: Layers of cotton wool

Diminishing returns: the first layer halves the loss; later layers save less and less.
Theory (model): 0: 9.07 · 1: 4.3 · 2: 2.82 · 3: 2.1 · 4: 1.67 · 5: 1.39
Result from a test run: 0: 9.18 ± 0.153 · 1: 4.46 ± 0.125 · 2: 2.86 ± 0.109 · 3: 1.93 ± 0.118 · 4: 1.67 ± 0.213 · 5: 1.55 ± 0.192 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Which insulator is best?

Cotton wool best, then foil (it reflects infrared back), felt and bubble wrap; one sheet of newspaper hardly helps.
Theory (model): 0: 9.07 · 1: 7.89 · 2: 5.49 · 3: 4.3 · 4: 5.1 · 5: 4.9
Result from a test run: 0: 9.18 ± 0.153 · 1: 8.05 ± 0.125 · 2: 5.53 ± 0.109 · 3: 4.13 ± 0.118 · 4: 5.1 ± 0.213 · 5: 5.06 ± 0.192 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Starting temperature (no lid)

Roughly in proportion to how far above room temperature it starts (Newton's law of cooling), and a little more at high temperatures because evaporation from an open can grows quickly with temperature.
Theory (model): 40: 3.04 · 50: 4.58 · 60: 6.13 · 70: 7.72 · 80: 9.36 · 90: 11.1
Result from a test run: 40: 3.13 ± 0.278 · 50: 4.76 ± 0.225 · 60: 6.1 ± 0.152 · 70: 7.58 ± 0.184 · 80: 9.46 ± 0.245 · 90: 11.3 ± 0.245 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Leslie cube: the four faces

Matt black emits most, shiny silver least (about 14 times less). Colour matters less than shininess for infrared.
Theory (emissivity × σ(T⁴ − T₀⁴)): 0: 1.35 · 1: 1.2 · 2: 1.13 · 3: 0.0994
Result from a test run: 0: 1.35 ± 0.00797 · 1: 1.2 ± 0.00748 · 2: 1.13 ± 0.01 · 3: 0.109 ± 0.0107 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: Black or shiny plate: coin drop

Matt black first (about 170 s), then shiny black and matt white. Shiny silver never gets hot enough: it reflects most of the infrared.
Theory (model): 0: 169 · 1: 207 · 2: 233
Result from a test run: 0: 170 ± 1.37 · 1: 209 ± 1.45 · 2: 234 ± 1.47 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 7: Loft insulation: how thick?

Most of the saving comes from the first 100 mm; beyond 270 mm (the UK recommendation) each extra centimetre saves little.
Theory (average year): 0: 2080 · 25: 1720 · 50: 1630 · 100: 1570 · 150: 1540 · 200: 1530 · 270: 1520 · 300: 1510
Result from a test run: 0: 2090 ± 64.4 · 25: 1740 ± 53.4 · 50: 1650 ± 50.6 · 100: 1580 ± 48.6 · 150: 1550 ± 47.8 · 200: 1540 ± 47.3 · 270: 1530 ± 47 · 300: 1520 ± 46.9 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 8: Turning the thermostat down

A straight line: about £130 (9%) per °C. Heat loss is proportional to the temperature difference with outside.
Theory (∝ (T − 8.6 °C)): 16: 985 · 17: 1120 · 18: 1250 · 19: 1380 · 20: 1520 · 21: 1650 · 22: 1780 · 23: 1920 · 24: 2050
Result from a test run: 16: 991 ± 30.5 · 17: 1130 ± 34.6 · 18: 1260 ± 38.7 · 19: 1390 ± 42.9 · 20: 1530 ± 47 · 21: 1660 ± 51.1 · 22: 1790 ± 55.2 · 23: 1930 ± 59.3 · 24: 2060 ± 63.5 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)
