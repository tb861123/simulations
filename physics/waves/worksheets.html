Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Young's slits: how good is λ = aw/D?

Young's slits: how good is λ = aw/D? 500 students each measure across 10 fringes (±0.5 mm), D (±5 mm) and a (±0.01 mm), then use λ = aw/D. The answers scatter around the true 550 nm with an SD of about 20 nm (≈ 3.6 %). Two errors matter most: the ruler (0.5 mm in 16.5 mm across 10 fringes is 3 %) and the slit separation (0.01 mm in 0.5 mm is 2 %); they combine as √(3² + 2²) ≈ 3.6 %. Measuring across 10 fringes instead of 1 makes the ruler's share 10 times smaller. The mean lands within about one standard error of 550 nm: random errors average out, systematic ones would not.

2 · Is fringe spacing proportional to D?

Is fringe spacing proportional to D? Red laser light (633 nm) through slits 0.5 mm apart. w = λD/a predicts a straight line through the origin with gradient λ/a = 1.27 mm per metre. The best-fit gradient comes out within about 1 % of that. The error bars (±1 SD, about 0.05 mm) stay the same size at every D, because the ruler error does not depend on D — so the PERCENTAGE error is smallest at large D. That is why you put the screen far away.

3 · Closer slits, wider fringes?

Closer slits, wider fringes? w = λD/a, so w ∝ 1/a: on log–log axes this is a straight line of gradient −1. The fitted gradient is −1.00 within about 0.01. With a = 2 mm the fringes are only 0.55 mm apart, so the 0.05 mm reading error is about 10 % of each one: small fringes are hard to measure.

4 · Grating: is a higher order more precise?

Grating: is a higher order more precise? Each student measures the angle of one order (±0.1°) and uses λ = d sin θ / n. Every order gives 589 nm on average, but the spread shrinks for higher orders: the same 0.1° error is divided by n (and helped by the larger angle). Theory says SD = d cos θ × (0.1° in radians) / n: about 5.7 nm for order 1, 2.7 nm for order 2, 1.6 nm for order 3, 1.0 nm for order 4 and 0.55 nm for order 5 (cos θ shrinks as θ grows). The batch gives the same within the scatter expected from 40 runs. So measure the highest order you can see clearly — another reason gratings beat double slits.

5 · Melde's experiment: does f ∝ √T?

Melde's experiment: does f ∝ √T? v = √(T/μ) and f₁ = v/2L, so f ∝ T^0.5: the log–log graph is a straight line of gradient 0.5. The fitted gradient comes out 0.50 (±0.01). Each resonance is found to ±1 %, so the error bars grow in proportion to f. Quadrupling the tension only doubles the pitch, which is why tuning pegs need big turns at first.

6 · Harmonics are whole-number multiples

Harmonics are whole-number multiples fₙ = nv/2L: a straight line through the origin with gradient f₁ = 343.0/(2 × 0.5) = 343 Hz per harmonic. The fitted gradient comes out within a few Hz of that (about 1 %, the size of the error in finding each resonance). Change the experiment to a closed pipe in the main controls and sweep n again: the even harmonics give no result at all, because a closed pipe cannot resonate at them.

7 · Speed of a passing car from its siren

Speed of a passing car from its siren Record the pitch as the ambulance approaches (f_a ≈ 767 Hz) and as it moves away (f_r ≈ 644 Hz) with a phone app (±2.4 Hz each). Dividing the Doppler formulas gives v_s = v (f_a − f_r)/(f_a + f_r), with no need to know f_s. The answers scatter around the true 30 m/s with an SD of about 0.8 m/s (≈ 3 %), because a 2–3 Hz error is noticeable compared with a 123 Hz drop. A faster vehicle or a higher-pitched siren would give a more precise speed.
