Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Why does the top go black?

What to look for: Black. When the film is much thinner than a wavelength (under about 30 nm), the light reflected from the back is almost exactly half a wave out of step with the light from the front (the front reflection flips the wave, the back one does not), so they cancel for every colour. Bubbles often show a black cap just before they burst.

Investigation 2: Newton's sequence of colours

What to look for: Black, grey-white, yellow, red-purple ('the sensitive tint'), blue, green, yellow, red, then repeating pinks and greens. Thicker films have reflection peaks closer together in wavelength, so several colours are reflected at once and they mix towards white. Newton described this sequence in Opticks (1704).

Investigation 3: One colour of light

What to look for: Bright and dark stripes (fringes), each a step of λ/(2n) in thickness: about 221 nm of soap film for sodium light. With blue light the fringes are closer together, because λ is smaller. With white light, the fringes of every colour overlap, which is where the colours come from. The batch run measures fringe spacing against wavelength.

Investigation 4: Tilt it

What to look for: The colours shift towards blue (shorter wavelengths) as you tilt. Inside the film the light travels at an angle, and the extra path is 2nd·cos θ, which gets smaller as θ grows, so the film acts as if it were thinner. That is why a bubble's colours change across its curved surface, and why a peacock's feathers shimmer.

Investigation 5: Oil on a wet road

What to look for: With oil on water, both reflections flip (each is off a denser material), so a very thin oil film looks bright (white-grey), not black. With n = 1.2 only the back reflection flips and thin parts go dark again. Which reflections flip decides whether a vanishing film is bright or dark.

Investigation 6: A non-reflective coating

What to look for: A film a quarter of a wavelength thick (in the film), with an index between air and glass, makes the two reflections cancel near the middle of the spectrum (green). Reflection drops from about 4% to about 1%. Red and blue are cancelled less well, so the little light that is reflected looks purple.

Batch experiment 1: Reflection against thickness (green light)

The scatter comes from counting a finite number of photons (an SE of about 0.004 when 2000 photons give 7%) and the ±3 nm uncertainty in thickness. Bright peaks at d = λ/(4n), 3λ/(4n), … = 103, 310 nm; dark at 0, 207, 414 nm: every λ/(2n) = 207 nm the pattern repeats. A soap film in air never reflects more than about 7.7% (when the two reflections add up exactly).

Batch experiment 2: The spectrum of a 300 nm film

This is the film's reflection spectrum. For a soap film in air the front reflection flips the wave and the back one does not, so the bright peaks sit where 2nd = (m + ½)λ. For d = 300 nm, 2nd = 798 nm, so the peak in the visible is at 798/1.5 = 532 nm (green), with dips where 2nd = mλ: 399 nm (violet) and 798 nm (deep red). The film looks green.

Batch experiment 3: Fringe spacing and wavelength

Each dark fringe is where the film is a whole number of half-wavelengths (in the film) thicker than at the last one: Δd = λ/(2n). With a slope of 0.5 nm per μm that is λ/(1.33) μm: about 391 μm for 520 nm light. Longer wavelengths give wider fringes, in proportion. Noise in the photograph makes the measured spacings scatter a little.

Batch experiment 4: Tilting shifts the colour

Straight on, a 400 nm film reflects most strongly at 2nd/1.5 = 709 nm (deep red) and again at 2nd/2.5 = 426 nm (violet). Tilting moves the red peak to shorter wavelengths, to about 488 nm at 75°, because the extra path 2nd·cos θ (θ inside the film) shrinks: the colour changes as you tilt. Counting noise moves single measurements by a few nanometres, more where the peak is broad and flat.
