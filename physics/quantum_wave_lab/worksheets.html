Quantum Wave Lab · Teacher notesquantum-wave-lab.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: A particle that is a wave

What to look for: The packet spreads out as it goes: a blend of slightly different wavelengths, which travel at different speeds. A narrower starting packet spreads faster. Nothing is 'smeared out' physically: the wave tells you the odds of where a detector would find the particle.

Investigation 2: Walking through walls

What to look for: Part of the wave leaks through: the particle has a real chance of appearing on the far side. Inside the barrier the wave dies away exponentially, so each extra bit of thickness cuts the chance by the same factor. This is how the Sun fuses hydrogen, how flash memory is erased and how a scanning tunnelling microscope sees atoms.

Investigation 3: Over the top, yet it bounces

What to look for: No: some is reflected even though there is energy to spare. Waves reflect wherever their speed changes abruptly, like light at a window. At certain thicknesses the reflections from the two faces cancel and almost everything gets through (a resonance): the same idea as anti-reflection coatings on lenses.

Investigation 4: One particle, two slits

What to look for: Many stripes: an interference pattern, built up dot by dot. Each particle's wave goes through both slits and interferes with itself; the dot appears at one place chosen at random with probability |ψ|². Block one slit and the stripes vanish. No single dot can show you the pattern, and no theory can say where the next dot will land.

Investigation 5: The uncertainty principle

What to look for: The narrower the slit, the wider the wave fans out. Squeezing position (small Δy) forces a larger spread of sideways momentum (large Δp): Δy·Δp ≥ ħ/2. It is a property of waves, not a fault in our instruments.

Investigation 6: Looking changes things

What to look for: A measurement finds the particle at one place (chosen at random according to |ψ|²), and the wave collapses to a tight packet there. Because it is now very localised, its momentum is very uncertain and it spreads rapidly in all directions: measuring position has disturbed the motion.

Investigation 7: A quantum orbit

What to look for: In the bowl the packet circles like a planet without spreading: the closest quantum mechanics comes to a classical orbit. In the corral it bounces, overlaps itself and fills the space with standing-wave ripples, like the electron ripples seen inside real corrals of atoms built with a tunnelling microscope.

Batch experiment 1: Tunnelling and thickness

The wave gives only a probability. Each particle either gets through or does not, so out of 200 the count varies from run to run, as in any real tunnelling experiment. This randomness is not a lack of knowledge: it is how nature works.
Theory (plane-wave formula): 1: 86.1 · 2: 57.5 · 3: 32.7 · 4: 17 · 6: 4.11 · 8: 0.947 · 10: 0.216
Result from a test run: 1: 85.8 ± 0.803 · 2: 55.8 ± 1.14 · 3: 36.4 ± 2.02 · 4: 19.1 ± 1.35 · 6: 3.83 ± 0.279 · 8: 0.667 ± 0.211 · 10: 0.0833 ± 0.0833 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Tunnelling and energy

Classically the graph would be a step: 0% below 1, 100% above. The quantum curve is smooth: some tunnelling below, some reflection above. The packet is a blend of energies, so it smooths the theory curve slightly.

Batch experiment 3: Double slit: fringe spacing

The pattern is built from individual random hits, so the stripe positions are only known as well as the dots allow: more particles, sharper answer. Wavelength λ = h/p: slower particles have longer waves and wider fringes.
Theory (path difference = λ): 7: 31.9 · 8: 36.7 · 9: 41.8 · 10: 46.9 · 11: 52.3 · 12: 57.9
Result from a test run: 7: 35 ± 0.311 · 8: 39.7 ± 0.668 · 9: 42.7 ± 0.235 · 10: 46.6 ± 0.998 · 11: 50.7 ± 1.85 · 12: 55.2 ± 0.0468 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Uncertainty at a slit

Halve the slit and the central band roughly doubles: pinning down the particle's sideways position (Δy ≈ a) makes its sideways momentum less certain (Δp ≈ h/a), so Δy·Δp stays about the same. The measured bands are about 30% wider than the simple formula, because this slit has hard walls: the wave must be zero at both edges, so it is squeezed into less than the full width (a wave that is zero at both edges has its first dark gaps at sin θ ≈ 1.5λ/a). Slits narrower than about 16 cells spread the wave over the whole screen.
Theory (open slit: sin θ = λ/a): 16: 86.2 · 20: 67.6 · 24: 55.8 · 32: 41.4 · 40: 33
Result from a test run: 16: 103 ± 7.42 · 20: 88.3 ± 0.333 · 24: 74 ± 1 · 32: 55 ± 1 · 40: 44.7 ± 2.33 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)
