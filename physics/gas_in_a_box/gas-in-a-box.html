How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • It is flat (2D). Real gases are 3D. In 2D a molecule has two ways to move (x and y), so its average kinetic energy is kT, not 3/2 kT. We define temperature from the molecules' energy: T = (average ½mv²) ÷ k.
  • Pressure is measured, not calculated: every time a molecule bounces off a wall it gives the wall a push of 2mv (its change of momentum). Adding up the pushes over time gives force; dividing by the length of wall gives 2D pressure in N/m (we show mN/m). It is averaged over the last 100 ps because single bounces are random.
  • Ideal gas law in 2D: pA = NkT, where A is the area. Real molecules take up a little space, so the measured pA ÷ NkT comes out a few per cent above 1. The "molecule size" prediction uses the hard-disc formula Z = 1 + 2φ(1 − 7φ/16)/(1 − φ)², where φ is the fraction of the area covered by molecules.
  • 3D-equivalent pressure: a real (3D) gas whose molecules are the same distance apart as in the box would have pressure p = n3/2kT, where n is the number per square metre in the box. This is shown for comparison with everyday pressures (1 atm = 101 kPa).
  • Speeds in 2D follow the Rayleigh (2D Maxwell–Boltzmann) distribution f(v) = (mv/kT)·e−mv²/2kT. Most likely speed √(kT/m), mean √(πkT/2m), r.m.s. √(2kT/m). In 3D the curve has v² instead of v at the start, so it is a little more lopsided.
  • Collisions are perfectly elastic (no energy lost), between smooth hard discs with the real "kinetic diameter" of each molecule. Momentum is swapped along the line joining the centres. Time moves in small steps (a molecule moves at most about ¼ of its diameter per step); a cell grid means each molecule only checks its close neighbours.
  • Mean free path (2D) λ = 1 ÷ (2√2 n d), where n is molecules per unit area and d the diameter (a moving disc hits any molecule whose centre is within d of its path, a strip 2d wide). The collision rate is (mean speed) ÷ λ. Both are measured by counting collisions and the distance travelled.
  • Heated walls act like a hot plate: a molecule that hits one leaves with a new random speed typical of the wall's temperature. Without them, walls are perfect mirrors and no heat gets in or out.
  • The piston is the right-hand wall. A molecule bouncing off a piston that is moving in gains speed (the gas heats up); off a piston moving out it loses speed (the gas cools). Without heated walls this is adiabatic: in 2D, T × A stays constant if you move slowly. Fast pistons heat the gas even more. A "free" piston has a mass of 2000 u and a little friction; the outside pressure pushes it in until the gas pushes back equally hard.
  • Brownian motion: the big particle is 6 nm across and 2000 u. Real particles that size are much heavier; a lighter one settles into a random walk sooner, which lets us see it in a short run. Its spreading rate D does not depend on its mass. Theory (hard disc in a 2D gas): D = √(kT/2πm) ÷ (2nR), where R is the distance between centres when touching and m the gas molecule mass. Mean square displacement ⟨r²⟩ = 4D(t − τ(1 − e−t/τ)) with τ = MD/kT; for long times this is 4Dt.
  • Diffusion: the theory line uses the 2D self-diffusion coefficient of hard discs D = (1 ÷ 2nd)·√(kT/πm), slowed slightly by crowding, and the diffusion equation across the box. It assumes the mean free path is much less than the box, which is only roughly true here.
  • Left out: molecules attracting each other (so no condensation into liquid), spinning and vibrating molecules (in a real 3D gas, N₂ and CO₂ store extra energy in rotation), quantum effects, gravity. The box is tiny (tens of nanometres), so readings jump about more than in a real gas with 10²³ molecules.
