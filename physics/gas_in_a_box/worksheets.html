Gas in a Box · Teacher notesgas-in-a-box.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: What is pressure?

What to look for: Pressure is the total force of all those kicks spread over the area of the walls. More molecules means more kicks each second, so more pressure. With only 20 the kicks come in ones and twos, so the reading is jumpy; with 1000 they blur into a steady push. A real gas has so many molecules that the pressure is perfectly steady.

Investigation 2: Squeeze it: Boyle's law

What to look for: Halving the volume doubles the pressure: p × V stays the same (Boyle's law). The molecules are no faster, but they are packed twice as densely, so twice as many hit each bit of wall every second. The dots follow the dashed curve p = NkT/V. At very small volumes they sit a little above it, because the molecules themselves take up room.

Investigation 3: Heat it: the pressure law and absolute zero

What to look for: A straight line through the origin: pressure is proportional to the temperature in kelvin. Hotter molecules move faster, so they hit the walls harder and more often. Follow the line back and the pressure reaches zero at 0 K (−273 °C), where the molecules would have no kinetic energy left. That is absolute zero.

Investigation 4: Let it expand: Charles's law

What to look for: The gas expands until its pressure matches the outside again, so the pressure stays the same and the volume is proportional to the kelvin temperature (Charles's law). The piston jiggles because it is light enough to feel individual molecules. This is how a hot-air balloon and a liquid-in-glass thermometer work.

Investigation 5: Are they all going the same speed?

What to look for: Within a few collisions each, the speeds spread out into the same lopsided hill every time: the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution (the dashed curve). Collisions share the energy out at random, so a few molecules are almost still, a few are very fast, and most are in between. Nobody has to arrange it. Heat the gas and the hill flattens and moves right.

Investigation 6: Light and heavy molecules

What to look for: Helium is far faster, about √33 ≈ 5.7 times. But the average kinetic energy ½mv² is the same for both, because collisions share energy until it is. That is what 'the same temperature' means: the same average kinetic energy per molecule, whatever the molecule.

Investigation 7: The bicycle pump

What to look for: Pushing the piston in heats the gas: a molecule bouncing off an advancing piston comes back faster, like a ball off a moving bat. The work you do becomes kinetic energy (ΔU = W, with Q = 0). Pulling it out cools the gas, because molecules bounce back slower from a retreating piston. This is why a bicycle pump gets hot, a diesel engine needs no spark, and rising air cools to make clouds. Push in and pull back out: the gas does not quite return to 300 K, because a fast push is not perfectly reversible.

Investigation 8: Brownian motion

What to look for: At any instant slightly more molecules hit one side than the other, by chance, so the particle is nudged about at random. In 1827 Robert Brown saw pollen grains do this under a microscope; in 1905 Einstein explained it, which was the first strong evidence that atoms are real. Heat the gas and the jiggling gets more violent.

Investigation 9: Diffusion: fast molecules, slow mixing

What to look for: Mixing takes far longer than a molecule's speed suggests. Each molecule travels only a few nanometres before a collision sends it off in a new direction, so it wanders in a zig-zag (a random walk) instead of crossing in a straight line. The light helium still spreads faster than the heavy xenon. This is why a smell takes many seconds to cross a room.

Investigation 10: An atmosphere in a box

What to look for: The gas is densest at the bottom and thins out with height, halving every fixed distance: an exponential fall, like the Earth's atmosphere. Gravity pulls the molecules down; their thermal motion spreads them back up. Hotter or lighter gas reaches higher, which is why hydrogen and helium escape from the Earth but oxygen and nitrogen stay.

Investigation 11: The ammonia and hydrogen chloride tube

What to look for: It forms nearer the hydrogen chloride end. Both gases are at the same temperature, so their molecules have the same average kinetic energy, but ammonia molecules (17) are lighter than hydrogen chloride (36.5) and so move faster and diffuse further in the same time. Neither crosses quickly, because each molecule is constantly knocked off course by air molecules. The graph marks where the ratio of the masses predicts the ring.

Investigation 12: A heat engine made of molecules A level

What to look for: The gas is expanded while hot (high pressure, so it does a lot of work on the piston) and compressed while cold (low pressure, so less work is needed). The difference is the net work out, equal to the area inside the loop. Heat flows in from the hot walls and a smaller amount flows out to the cold walls: an engine turns only part of the heat into work. The measured efficiency is below the ideal for this cycle because the piston moves at a finite speed and the gas is never quite at the wall temperature.

Investigation 13: The petrol engine: the Otto cycle A level

What to look for: The compression and power strokes are adiabats, steeper than isotherms, because the gas heats as it is squeezed and cools as it expands. The ideal efficiency depends only on the compression ratio r: 1 − 1/r^(γ−1), here with r = 3 and γ = 5/3. Real petrol engines use r of about 10; higher still and the mixture ignites too early (knocking).

Investigation 14: Carnot's limit A level

What to look for: No engine working between two temperatures can beat 1 − Tc/Th, however cleverly it is built: this is one form of the second law of thermodynamics. The molecular engine here falls a little short, because its piston moves at a finite speed and the gas is never quite at the wall temperature. Make Th and Tc closer and the limit itself shrinks: a power station needs very hot steam and cold cooling water.

With only 150 molecules the number of wall hits in 200 ps varies by chance from run to run. A real gas sample has about 10²⁰ molecules, so its pressure does not flicker at all.

Batch experiment 6: How far between collisions?

Double the number of molecules and each one meets a neighbour after half the distance. At low numbers the path becomes as long as the box, so walls interrupt flights and the simple formula overestimates what is measured.

Batch experiment 7: The ammonia and hydrogen chloride tube

Graham's law says rates of diffusion go as 1/√(mass), which puts the ring 59% of the way along. The model lands close to that but not exactly on it: how far a molecule gets also depends on its size and on the first molecules to arrive, which are the fastest few.
Theory (from the masses): 59.4
Result from a test run: mean 57.9, SD 3.15, SE 0.406 from 60 runs

Batch experiment 8: The spread of speeds

This is not measurement error: it is the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution itself. Even at one fixed temperature, molecules have a wide range of speeds, with a long tail of fast ones.
