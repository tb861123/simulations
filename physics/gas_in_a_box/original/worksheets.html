Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Does pV = NkT hold?

What this shows. Each run measures pressure from wall hits for 300 ps. The average is close to 1 but a few per cent higher, because molecules take up a little room and get in each other's way (the yellow line includes this). Single runs scatter by about 1% because only a few thousand wall hits are counted. The model lands about 1% below the yellow line: collisions are found one small time step late, after the discs have overlapped slightly, so they act a little smaller than their real size.

5 · Brownian motion: does ⟨r²⟩ = 4Dt?

What this shows. Each run follows the big particle for 800 ps and finds D from the gradient of mean square displacement against time (÷ 4 in 2D). One run gives a rough value, because the walk is random; the average of many runs is close to theory. The theory ignores the gas swirling along with the particle, which in 2D changes D by around 10%.

6 · Mixing: do more molecules mix slower?

What this shows. More molecules means a shorter mean free path, so each molecule is knocked back more often and diffuses more slowly: D ∝ 1/n, so mixing time ∝ N. Expect agreement only to within about 20%: the box is just a few mean free paths wide (the theory assumes many), and "80% mixed" is judged from a noisy count, so with few molecules random fluctuations can make it early or late.

8 · Adiabatic squeeze: does T × A stay constant?

What this shows. Each run starts 100 nm wide at 300 K, with walls that let no heat through, and pushes the piston in slowly (20 m/s) to the chosen width. Molecules bounce off the incoming piston faster than they hit it, so the gas warms up. For a slow squeeze kinetic theory predicts T × A = constant in 2D (γ = 2; in 3D it would be TV^0.4 = constant for air). The model comes out slightly hotter than the line because the piston is not infinitely slow.

9 · Where does the white ring first appear?

What this shows. Ammonia (Mr 17) and hydrogen chloride (Mr 36.5) random-walk from opposite ends of a 1 m tube. Lighter molecules move faster, so ammonia diffuses √(36.5/17) = 1.47 times faster. The ring first appears where the two leading edges meet; each edge has spread a distance ∝ √(Dt), so that is 55% of the way from the ammonia end (yellow line): off-centre, towards the HCl end, but not yet at the Graham's law position (grey dashed, 59%). Runs differ slightly because molecules move at random. Batch runs use 35 molecules per cm (the High quality setting).

10 · Where does the ring settle? (Graham's law)

What this shows. Each run lasts 3.5 hours of tube time. Once the gases have been flowing for a while, the reaction happens where ammonia and HCl arrive at the same rate. That is where distance ratio = diffusion rate ratio = √(36.5/17) = 1.47: Graham's law, 59% of the way from the ammonia end. Real tubes often show the ring at about 60–67%; the extra shift comes from things this model leaves out (the heavier HCl pushing through air, the ring itself blocking the tube).

11 · Heavier acid gas: does the ring move? (Graham's law)

What this shows. The heavier the acid gas, the slower it diffuses, so the ring settles closer to its end. Graham's law (rate ∝ 1/√Mr) predicts the fraction √M ÷ (√17 + √M): HCl (36.5) 0.59, HBr (81) 0.69, HI (128) 0.73. The heaviest gases take longest to settle, so after 3.5 h they are still slightly short of the line. Real tubes give similar trends.

12 · Does warming speed up diffusion?

What this shows. Hotter molecules move faster, so the gases meet sooner. In kinetic theory D ∝ T^1.5 (in kelvin) at constant pressure, so the time scales as T^−1.5: from 0 °C to 100 °C (273 K to 373 K) the ring appears about 1.6 times sooner. The theory line is drawn through the model's own 25 °C time, so it tests the scaling, not the absolute time.
