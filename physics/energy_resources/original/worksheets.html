Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Why transmit at high voltage?

What this shows. The percentage lost in the cables falls as 1/V²: on log–log axes the points lie on a line of gradient −2. At 100 kV about 45% is lost; at 400 kV only about 3%. (Try sweeping down to 25 kV: the formula then says more than all the power would be lost, so the results are capped at 100% and the line bends: the cables would melt long before.) Points scatter a little because the cables' resistance rises when they are warm (±4%) and the power sent varies.

2 · Longer lines lose more

What this shows. Resistance is proportional to length, so the I²R loss rises in a straight line through the origin with the length of the line. This is one reason power stations are built near where the electricity is needed, and why long-distance links (such as Scotland to England) use the highest voltages, or d.c.

3 · Model turbine: is P ∝ v³?

What this shows. The log–log graph is a straight line with a gradient close to 3, so P ∝ v³ (from KE per second = ½ρAv³). Each point scatters by about 6–10% because the fan's air is gusty and the anemometer reads to 0.1 m/s: at low speeds the rounding of the meters matters more. Repeats and a best-fit line on the log–log graph give n with an uncertainty.

4 · How precise is one turbine reading?

What this shows. The same fan setting read 500 times. The mean is very close to the true value, but single readings scatter with an SD of about 11%: 6% from gusts, plus 3 × 3% because a small error in the wind speed is cubed (a 3% error in v gives about 9% in v³). This is why the v³ law makes wind measurements so sensitive.

5 · A real turbine's year: capacity factor

What this shows. Each run is a different year of hourly winds with the same average. The capacity factor (energy produced ÷ rated power × 8760 h) varies by a few per cent from year to year: some years are windier. The mean agrees with the theory (the power curve averaged over the wind-speed distribution). UK onshore fleets average about 26%, offshore about 39%.

6 · Windier sites: capacity factor

What this shows. The capacity factor climbs steeply with the average wind speed (because P ∝ v³ below the rated speed), then levels off as more of the time is spent at full power. This is why wind farms are built offshore and on hilltops, where the average wind is higher.

7 · A winter week: how much does the weather matter?

What this shows. The same power stations, 200 different January weeks. The carbon intensity ranges from about 50 to over 200 g/kWh: windy weeks need little gas, calm weeks a lot. The theory (yellow) is one week of steady, average weather; the mean of real weeks differs from it because the merit order is not linear (in a very windy hour the extra wind is exported or switched off, and cannot cancel a calm hour).

8 · More wind: diminishing returns

What this shows. Adding wind cuts the CO₂ quickly at first, almost along the grey dashed line (every MWh of wind replacing a MWh of gas). Then the real weeks (dots) level off well above both lines: the yellow line, a week of steady average wind, reaches zero at about 75 GW, but real wind comes in gusts and lulls. On windy days there is already more wind than demand, so extra turbines are switched off, while calm days still need gas. Storage, interconnectors and flexible demand are needed to use the extra wind. Points scatter because each run is a different week of weather.

9 · No gas: how much storage? (beyond the spec)

What this shows. With no gas or coal, a winter week has hours of blackout whenever the wind drops. More battery storage fills more of the gaps, but a long calm spell needs an enormous store, and the batteries can only refill when there is spare wind. With average steady weather (the theory line) a little storage would be enough; real weather is lumpy. (Energy storage is Eduqas option D, not taught.)
