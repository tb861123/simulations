How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Power stations: each is a chain of stages (for coal: boiler 88%, steam turbine 43%, generator 98%); the overall efficiency is the product. Values are typical of modern UK plant, rounded. Fuel input = electrical output ÷ efficiency. The comparison data (capacity factor, life-cycle CO₂, cost, land) are approximate published or estimated values: see the sources below each chart and in the README. Costs especially change with fuel prices, interest rates and the year, and the sources use different bases, so use them only to rank.
  • Run Britain: one-hour steps. Demand follows a typical GB daily shape (winter: about 24 GW at night to 42 GW at the evening peak; weekends 10% lower). The wind fleet's capacity factor wanders randomly with a memory of about 30 hours (winter average 40%, summer 22%); solar follows the Sun's height at 52.5° N and a random cloudiness each day. Supply is matched by fixed rules (merit order): nuclear, wind, solar and hydro first; then storage, biomass, imports, gas and coal. Storage charges whenever there is spare power and empties whenever there is a shortfall (a real operator plans ahead). Round trip: batteries 88%, pumped storage 75%. Imports are limited when Britain is calm (neighbours are often calm too). There are no limits on how fast gas plant can change output, no grid bottlenecks (Scotland to England), no inertia or frequency control, and no reserve margin, all of which a real grid needs. CO₂ uses operational (chimney) factors: gas 394, coal 937, biomass 120 (its CO₂ is counted as reabsorbed by regrowth, which is debated), imports 150 g/kWh. Costs are illustrative.
  • The National Grid: the whole current is treated as flowing through one pair of cables of resistance 0.03 Ω/km (real lines are three-phase a.c. with several circuits, and losses also happen in transformers and in the local distribution network: in Britain about 2% is lost in transmission and 5–6% in distribution). P_loss = I²R with I = P/V; the voltage drop along the line is ignored when working out I. Transformers are taken as 100% efficient (real large ones are about 99.5%).
  • Classroom power line: a 12 V supply, a 6 Ω lamp (12 V, 24 W) and the resistance of the long wires; ideal transformers with a turns ratio 1 : n and n : 1, then each taken as 90% efficient (small school transformers waste more than big ones). The lamp's resistance is taken as constant (a real filament's falls when it is dim).
  • Wind turbine: power in the wind = ½ρAv³; the turbine captures Cp = 0.45 between the cut-in speed (3 m/s) and the rated speed, then holds its rated power, and shuts down above 25 m/s. Rated power is set from the blade length (about 4.4 MW for 60 m blades). Real turbines have a smoother power curve. Site winds follow a Weibull (Rayleigh, k = 2) distribution with hour-to-hour persistence. Wind speed grows with height, which is left out.
  • Model turbine practical: blades 15 cm long, Cp 0.20, 60% electrical efficiency, a 10 Ω load. Errors: the fan's air is gusty (±3% on the anemometer, ±6% on the power), anemometer read to 0.1 m/s, voltmeter to 0.01 V, ammeter to 1 mA.
  • Quality only changes the picture (moving dots, air streaks). The model and the batch runs are the same at every setting.
