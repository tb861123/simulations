Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · What is the critical mass of U-235?

What this shows. Each run follows 2000 neutrons born at random places in the sphere and counts the new neutrons from the fissions they cause. k rises with mass because a bigger sphere has less surface for its volume, so a smaller fraction of neutrons escapes. The points cross k = 1 at about 52 kg: the critical mass. The scatter (about ±0.03) comes from following only 2000 neutrons.

3 · Do control rods work?

What this shows. 80 kg of U-235 on its own is supercritical (k ≈ 1.10). Every neutron the rods absorb is one fewer to cause a fission, so k falls in a straight line, k = k₀(1 − r). About 9% absorption brings it back to exactly 1: that is how a reactor is held critical.

4 · How long is a chain when k < 1?

What this shows. One neutron starts each chain. Below k = 1 every chain dies out, but the average number of fissions is (k/ν)/(1 − k), which shoots up as k approaches 1 (from 0.05 at k = 0.1 to 3.7 at k = 0.9). The spread is huge: most chains end at once, a few go on for a long time.

5 · Will one neutron start a runaway?

What this shows. Even above k = 1 a chain started by one neutron often dies in the first few generations, simply because its neutron escapes. The fraction that run away (reach 300 neutrons) follows the branching-process theory 1 − s, where s solves s = (1 − k/ν) + (k/ν)(w₂s² + w₃s³). At k = 1.5 only about half (49%) of chains run away. That is why weapon designs add a neutron source to start many chains at once.

6 · How much energy does one fission release?

What this shows. Each run picks a random pair of fragments (a light one near A = 96 and a heavy one near A = 138, the real 'double hump') and works out the energy from the semi-empirical mass formula. Different splits release different energies (about 150–172 MeV). The mean, about 162 MeV, is roughly 7% below the 173.3 MeV of the Ba-141 + Kr-92 split from measured masses, because the formula leaves out shell effects that make some fragments extra stable. Adding the energy of the later beta decays brings the total to about 200 MeV.
