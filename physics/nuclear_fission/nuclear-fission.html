📐 Physics notes

Spec reference. Advanced: WJEC Eduqas A level Physics, nuclear energy (the old WJEC page's Component 3, Topic 6, Nuclear Physics). Standard: Edexcel International GCSE Physics 4PH1 Section 7(c) Fission and fusion.

Key equations:

²³⁵₉₂U + n → ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3n
Δm = m(reactants) − m(products)
E = Δm c² = Δm (u) × 931.5 MeV
B/A = [a_v A − a_s A^(2/3) − a_c Z²/A^(1/3) − a_a (A − 2Z)²/A] / A

What this shows: when a heavy nucleus such as U-235 absorbs a slow (thermal) neutron it becomes unstable and splits into two lighter daughter nuclei plus typically 2–3 neutrons and about 200 MeV of energy. The energy is released because the binding energy per nucleon B/A is larger for medium-mass nuclei than for heavy ones: the products are more tightly bound, so mass is converted to energy (E = Δmc²). The released neutrons can trigger further fissions: a chain reaction. The multiplication factor k decides whether the chain reaction dies out (k < 1), carries on at a steady rate (k = 1, a reactor) or grows explosively (k > 1, a bomb).

Advanced context. The binding energy per nucleon peaks near Fe-56 (about 8.8 MeV per nucleon): both fission of heavy nuclei and fusion of light nuclei release energy by moving towards this peak. The semi-empirical mass formula (Bethe–Weizsäcker) splits B into volume, surface, Coulomb and asymmetry terms. Reactor control needs k held at exactly 1.0, using control rods (cadmium or boron) that absorb the extra neutrons. Delayed neutrons (from the decay of fission products, about 0.65% of the total) give enough time for the rods to be moved mechanically.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • The core is a bare sphere of pure U-235 (19.1 g/cm³) or Pu-239 (19.8 g/cm³). The picture is a slice through its middle.
  • Each neutron starts in a random direction and travels a random distance (exponential, mean free path ℓ) before it is absorbed by a fuel nucleus and causes a fission. If that distance takes it outside the sphere, it escapes. ℓ is chosen so that k = 1 at the measured bare-sphere critical masses (52 kg for U-235, 10 kg for Pu-239).
  • Escape probability for a neutron born at a random place in a sphere of radius R: P = 3/(8a³)[2a² − 1 + (1 + 2a)e^(−2a)], with a = R/ℓ. So k = ν(1 − P)(1 − r), where ν = 2.43 (U-235) or 2.88 (Pu-239) is the average number of neutrons per fission (2 or 3 at random here) and r is the fraction absorbed by control rods.
  • "Choose k" mode works backwards: it finds the sphere size that gives the k you chose (without rods), so the animation is always the same physical model.
  • Left out: neutron scattering (neutrons bounce around rather than flying straight), slowing down by a moderator, capture without fission, U-238 in real fuel, a reflector round the core, delayed neutrons, the core heating up and expanding, and the fuel being used up. Later generations start from where fissions happened, which is nearer the middle, so in the animation k is slightly higher than the formula (which assumes neutrons start anywhere in the sphere).
  • Time: a real neutron generation takes about 10 nanoseconds in a bare sphere (about 0.1 ms in a reactor with a moderator). The animation is slowed down about 100 million times.
  • Energy: the 173.3 MeV for the Ba-141 + Kr-92 channel comes from measured atomic masses. Pu-239 uses the usual approximate 200 MeV. The binding-energy curve is the semi-empirical formula with no pairing or shell terms, so it is smooth and slightly low for some nuclei.
