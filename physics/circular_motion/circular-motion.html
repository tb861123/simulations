📐 Physics notes

Spec reference: Component 1, Topic 3 (Circular Motion), sections (a)–(k).

Key equations:

v = ωr
a = v²/r = ω²r
F = mv²/r = mω²r
T = 2π/ω, f = 1/T
tan θ = v²/(rg) (ideal banking)
vmin, top = √(gR) (loop the loop)
vmin, bottom = √(5gR) (loop the loop)

What this shows: circular motion needs a centripetal force towards the centre of the circle. This is not a new type of force: it is the resultant of the real forces (tension, the normal force, gravity, friction) that happens to point inwards.

Centripetal acceleration a = v²/r = ω²r always points towards the centre. By Newton's second law, the resultant inward force must equal ma.

Banked tracks: when a road is banked at angle θ, the horizontal component of the normal force (N sin θ) provides centripetal force without needing friction. The ideal angle satisfies tan θ = v²/(rg). With friction μ there is a range of safe speeds between vmin and vmax.

Vertical circles: as a ball goes round a vertical loop its speed falls going up (KE → GPE) and rises coming down (GPE → KE). At the top, both gravity and tension point towards the centre. The string goes slack (T = 0) when v² = gr at the top, giving the minimum safe speed vmin, bottom = √(5gR) from conservation of energy.

Advanced context. Non-inertial reference frames and pseudo-forces. In a rotating frame of reference (sitting in a car going round a bend), Newton's laws seem to fail: objects seem to be pushed outwards by a "centrifugal force". This is a pseudo-force (fictitious force) that appears because the frame is accelerating. It has size mω²r outwards, equal and opposite to the centripetal force in the inertial frame.

Coriolis effect: a second pseudo-force in rotating frames. Anything moving within a rotating frame is deflected sideways: FCor = −2m ω × v. On Earth this deflects winds and ocean currents (to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, to the left in the Southern). The Coriolis acceleration at mid-latitudes is about 10⁻⁴ × v m/s² (it is zero for horizontal motion at the equator): negligible for everyday motion but important at planetary scales.

Geosynchronous orbit: set the gravitational force equal to the centripetal force with T = 24 h: GMm/r² = mω²r and ω = 2π/T, so r³ = GMT²/(4π²), r = (GMT²/4π²)1/3 ≈ 42 164 km (altitude ≈ 35 786 km above Earth's surface). This is the same derivation as Kepler's third law applied to Earth. (Strictly, the sidereal day of 23 h 56 min gives exactly this radius.)

📐 Physics notes

Spec reference: Component 1, Topic 3 (Circular Motion).

Key equations:

ω² = g / (L cos θ)
Tperiod = 2π √(L cos θ / g)
Tstring = mg / cos θ
r = L sin θ, v = ωL sin θ

Conical pendulum: a mass on a string moves in a horizontal circle while the string makes an angle θ with the vertical.

Vertically: T cos θ = mg, so the tension is T = mg / cos θ. Horizontally: T sin θ = mω²r = mω²L sin θ, giving T = mω²L.

Combining: ω² = g / (L cos θ). ω (and so the period) does not depend on mass, just as a simple pendulum's period does not. The period T = 2π√(L cos θ / g) gets shorter as θ increases (faster spin, smaller effective length L cos θ).

Key idea: you cannot set θ on its own: it adjusts itself to balance the forces for the speed you spin it at. This self-adjusting property made it so useful as a mechanical speed governor.

Advanced context. Stability and the governor principle. If the engine speeds up, ω increases, θ increases and the throttle closes, so the engine slows down. If the engine slows, θ decreases, the throttle opens and the engine speeds up. The system settles at the angle for the target speed.

Non-linear ω–θ relationship: ω = √(g / L cos θ) grows without limit as θ → 90°. Real governors work at practical angles (typically 40–60°). Beyond about 60° the governor becomes very sensitive to small speed changes: useful for fine regulation but mechanically unstable.

Comparison with the simple pendulum: as θ → 0, Tconical → 2π√(L/g), the same as a simple pendulum's period.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Ball on a string: uniform circular motion at a steady ω, seen from above, so gravity is not shown (it would be balanced by the table or a vertical force). The string pulls only inwards. When it snaps the ball carries straight on along the tangent (Newton's first law): it does not fly outwards. The picture always draws the circle the same size; the scale bar gives the real size. Very fast motion (the electron, the spin dryer) is slowed down on screen and the label says by how much.
  • Electron preset: treated with ordinary (non-relativistic) mechanics, although 1.0 × 10⁸ m/s is a third of the speed of light; a full treatment would need relativity.
  • Banked bend: the car is a point on a plane tilted at θ, moving in a horizontal circle. Forces: weight, the normal reaction N = m(g cos θ + (v²/r) sin θ) and friction F = m((v²/r) cos θ − g sin θ) along the slope (positive = down the slope). It skids when the friction needed is more than μN. (The old WJEC page used N = mg / cos θ, which is only true at the ideal speed.) No air resistance, no tyre deformation, no rolling resistance. The top view is speeded up so you can see the car go round.
  • Loop the loop: a ball on a light, inextensible string (or a car on the inside of a circular track: the same maths with the normal reaction instead of tension). No friction or air resistance, so energy is conserved: v² = vbottom² − 2gr(1 − cos θ). Solved step by step (RK4, 1 ms steps). If the tension would go negative the string goes slack and the ball falls freely (a projectile) until the string pulls tight again; the jerk at that moment is not modelled.
  • Conical pendulum: steady motion only (the bob has settled into its circle). Light string, point mass, no air resistance.
  • Real roller coasters are not circular: they use clothoid (Euler spiral) loops, taller than they are wide, to cut the g-force at the bottom.
  • Batch runs: model class practicals. The whirling-bung and conical-pendulum periods are timed with a stopwatch over several turns; each start and stop has a random human reaction error (normal, SD 0.15 s). Friction in the glass tube and the string not being quite horizontal are left out. The banked-bend runs vary the tyre grip μ from car to car (normal distribution).
