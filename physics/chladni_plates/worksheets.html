Sound Made Visible · Teacher noteschladni-plates.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Find the resonances

What to look for: They are the plate's own natural frequencies. At each one, a single mode of vibration grows to perhaps a hundred times the size it has in between (see the peaks on the left-hand chart). Between peaks the plate barely moves and the sand sits still.

Investigation 2: Why does sand collect on lines?

What to look for: Between them. Along the boundary between a part moving up and a part moving down, the plate does not move at all: a nodal line. Sand is kicked away from everywhere else and stays where it is not kicked.

Investigation 3: More lines, higher note

What to look for: It roughly quadruples. For a plate, frequency goes as (number of lines)², because a stiff plate resists tight bending much more than gentle bending. A string or a drum skin only doubles. The batch run measures this.

Investigation 4: Move the driver

What to look for: A mode cannot be driven from a point where it does not move. The centre is a node for every pattern that is not symmetrical, so only symmetrical ones appear there. Off-centre, many more resonances appear on the chart. Put the driver on a nodal line and that mode dies away.

Investigation 5: Thicker, bigger, different metal

What to look for: Double the thickness and every resonance doubles in frequency (the plate is stiffer faster than it is heavier). Double the side and every resonance drops to a quarter. The patterns themselves do not change: they depend only on the shape.

Investigation 6: Between two resonances

What to look for: Between resonances several modes respond by similar small amounts, and the plate's motion is their sum. Sliding the frequency changes the mixture smoothly, so the nodal lines bend, join and split. You need more loudness because nothing is resonating.

Investigation 7: A drum

What to look for: Circles and diameters. The overtones are at 1.59, 2.14, 2.30, 2.65… times the lowest: not whole-number multiples. That is why a drum gives a thump rather than a clear musical note, while a string (whose overtones are exact multiples) sings.

Batch experiment 1: Lines against frequency

The scan steps in frequency and each reading has 4% noise, so the peak is found slightly off each time. The scatter is small beside the trend: a curve, not a straight line. Plot f against n² and it becomes straight.
Theory (f ∝ n²): 2: 166 · 4: 664 · 6: 1490 · 8: 2660 · 10: 4150 · 12: 5980
Result from a test run: 2: 166 ± 0.103 · 4: 665 ± 0.201 · 6: 1490 ± 0.963 · 8: 2660 ± 1.74 · 10: 4150 ± 2.22 · 12: 5980 ± 1.63 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Thickness

A straight line through the origin: frequency ∝ thickness. Bending stiffness rises as thickness³ but mass only as thickness, and frequency goes as √(stiffness ÷ mass).
Theory (f ∝ thickness): 0.5: 83 · 0.75: 125 · 1: 166 · 1.5: 249 · 2: 332 · 2.5: 415 · 3: 498
Result from a test run: 0.5: 83.1 ± 0.0356 · 0.75: 125 ± 0.029 · 1: 166 ± 0.145 · 1.5: 249 ± 0.178 · 2: 332 ± 0.123 · 2.5: 415 ± 0.163 · 3: 498 ± 0.274 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Size

Halving the side quadruples the frequency: an inverse-square law. (A string or air column would only double: f ∝ 1/L.)
Theory (f ∝ 1/L²): 15: 425 · 18: 295 · 21: 217 · 24: 166 · 28: 122 · 32: 93.4 · 36: 73.8 · 40: 59.8
Result from a test run: 15: 425 ± 0.241 · 18: 295 ± 0.104 · 21: 217 ± 0.153 · 24: 166 ± 0.109 · 28: 122 ± 0.0371 · 32: 93.4 ± 0.0466 · 36: 73.8 ± 0.0432 · 40: 59.8 ± 0.0176 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Drum overtones

A string's overtones would be 1, 2, 3, 4… (a straight line of gradient 1). The drum's bunch up irregularly: 1, 1.59, 2.14, 2.30, 2.65, 2.92… This is why drums sound unpitched.
Theory (ideal drum (Bessel zeros)): 1: 1 · 2: 1.59 · 3: 2.14 · 4: 2.3 · 5: 2.65 · 6: 2.92 · 7: 3.16 · 8: 3.5 · 9: 3.6 · 10: 3.65
Result from a test run: 1: 1 ± 4.77e-4 · 2: 1.59 ± 6.96e-4 · 3: 2.13 ± 0.00149 · 4: 2.3 ± 0.00137 · 5: 2.65 ± 0.00135 · 6: 2.92 ± 9.19e-4 · 7: 3.16 ± 0.00194 · 8: 3.5 ± 0.00265 · 9: 3.6 ± 0.0014 · 10: 3.65 ± 0.00165 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: How fast does the pattern form?

Each grain takes a random walk, so every run differs a little. Louder means bigger hops and a quicker pattern, but with diminishing returns: the last grains to arrive are those that started in the quietest regions, where hops are small whatever the loudness.
Result from a test run: 0.5: 16.5 ± 0.234 · 0.75: 6.32 ± 0.114 · 1: 3.2 ± 0.0266 · 1.5: 1.37 ± 0.0236 · 2: 0.8 ± 0.0105 · 3: 0.483 ± 0.00913 · 4: 0.393 ± 0.0085 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)
