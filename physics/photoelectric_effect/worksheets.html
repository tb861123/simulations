The Photoelectric Effect · Teacher notesphotoelectric-effect.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Red light does nothing

What to look for: Red light frees no electrons however bright it is, and waiting does not help. Below about 290 nm (ultraviolet) electrons appear at once, even in dim light. For zinc the threshold is 1240 ÷ 4.30 = 288 nm. A wave would deliver energy steadily and should eventually free electrons with any colour; the experiment says otherwise.

Investigation 2: Brighter or bluer?

What to look for: Brighter light means more photons each second, so more electrons (more current), but each photon carries the same energy, so the fastest electrons are no faster. Shorter wavelength means higher frequency and more energetic photons, so faster electrons, but not more of them. Intensity sets the number; frequency sets the energy.

Investigation 3: Different metals

What to look for: Green photons carry 1240 ÷ 530 = 2.34 eV. That is enough for caesium (2.14 eV) and potassium (2.30 eV) but not sodium, calcium, zinc, copper or platinum. Each metal holds its electrons with a different 'work function'. Light meters and old TV cameras used caesium because visible light works on it.

Investigation 4: The gold-leaf electroscope

What to look for: With ultraviolet the negative electroscope slowly discharges and the leaf falls: electrons are knocked out of the zinc. Red light does nothing. A positively charged electroscope does not discharge: any electrons that do escape are pulled straight back by the positive charge. This was how Hertz and Hallwachs first saw the effect in 1887–88.

Investigation 5: Ultraviolet and you

What to look for: Below 400 nm is ultraviolet. Each ultraviolet photon carries more than 3 eV, enough to break chemical bonds and damage DNA in skin cells, causing sunburn and skin cancer, and to damage the eyes. Visible photons carry less, so they cannot do this damage one at a time. This is why sunscreen and sunglasses block UV.

Investigation 6: Stopping the electrons A level

What to look for: The current falls to zero at the stopping potential V_s, about −0.66 V here: the fastest electrons (KE_max = hf − φ = 4.96 − 4.30 = 0.66 eV) only just fail to reach the anode, so e V_s = KE_max. Measuring V_s is how KE_max is found in practice. Press 'Sweep the voltage' to trace the whole I–V curve.

Investigation 7: Millikan's measurement of h A level

What to look for: A straight line: V_s = (h/e) f − φ/e. Its gradient is h/e = 4.14 × 10⁻¹⁵ V s, the same for every metal, and it crosses the frequency axis at the threshold frequency f₀ = φ/h. Millikan set out in 1914–16 to prove Einstein wrong, and instead measured h to within 0.5%.

Investigation 8: No waiting A level

What to look for: Spread evenly as a wave, very dim light would deliver energy to each electron so slowly that it would take minutes or hours to collect the few eV needed. Yet photoelectrons appear within 10⁻⁹ s. With photons, the first electron appears as soon as the first suitable photon is absorbed: dim light just means photons are rare. The batch run 'Waiting for the first electron' compares the two.

Batch experiment 1: Millikan: stopping potential against frequency

The gradient is h/e: multiply by e (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) to get h. Below the threshold (5.7 × 10¹⁴ Hz for sodium) the stopping potential is zero, so those points are left off the fit line in a real analysis.

Almost at once: a few billionths of a second. If the same energy were spread evenly as a wave over the 10¹⁵ or so atoms on the lit surface, each electron would take years to gather 2.3 eV.

Batch experiment 6: Energies of the electrons

There is a spread, up to a sharp maximum. Electrons from below the surface lose energy on the way out; only those from the very surface leave with hf − φ. That is why the equation is for the MAXIMUM kinetic energy.
Theory (KE_max = hf − φ): 1.77
Result from a test run: mean 1.17, SD 0.415, SE 0.0169 from 600 runs
