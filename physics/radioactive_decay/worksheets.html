Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Does N follow N₀e^(−λt)?

What this shows. Each dot is one run; the pink points are the mean ± 1 SD at each time. The means sit on the theory curve N₀e^(−λt) (here N₀(0.9)^t, half-life 6.6 s). Individual runs scatter because each nucleus decays at random: the scatter is a binomial spread, largest when about half the nuclei are left.

2 · Do small samples scatter more?

What this shows. The outcome is how far each run's N is from the expected value, as a percentage. On log–log axes the points fall on a line of gradient −0.5: the relative scatter is proportional to 1/√N₀. 100 times more nuclei gives 10 times less scatter. That is why decay looks perfectly smooth for a real sample of 10²⁰ atoms.

3 · Is the half-life the same every time?

What this shows. Each run records the moment exactly half the nuclei have gone. The results form a bell-shaped spread centred on ln2/λ = 6.58 s, with an SD of about 1/(λ√N₀) ≈ 0.30 s. The half-life is a property of the nuclide; each measurement of it from a finite sample has random error, which shrinks as the sample gets bigger.

4 · Are Geiger counts random (Poisson)?

What this shows. A long-lived source (T½ ≈ 11.5 minutes) counted for 10 s, 500 times. The counts average about 15 but vary from count to count, with an SD close to √15 ≈ 3.9: the signature of a Poisson process. So a single count of N has an uncertainty of about √N, which is why you count for longer to get a more precise rate.

5 · Why subtract the background count?

What this shows. Each run fits a straight line to ln(count rate) against time for 20 s, as in a school experiment, without subtracting the background. With no background the fit gives the true 6.6 s. As the background grows, the late count rates are propped up, the line is less steep and the 'half-life' comes out too long: a systematic error, not a random one. Subtracting a separately measured background fixes it.

6 · Secular equilibrium: is A_D = A_P?

What this shows. 100 000 parent atoms with a 1000-day half-life and a 2-day daughter, looked at after 20 days (10 daughter half-lives). The ratio of the activities clusters round the Bateman value, 1.001: equal activities (it is a tiny bit above 1 because the parent is slowly decaying too). The scatter comes from the small number of daughter atoms (about 200): the relative scatter is about 1/√200 ≈ 7%.

7 · Transient equilibrium: how does the daughter grow?

What this shows. 5000 parent atoms (T½ = 10 d) and a daughter with T½ = 1 d. The daughter count rises fast, peaks after about 3.7 days (t = ln(λ_D/λ_P)/(λ_D − λ_P)), then falls with the parent's 10-day half-life. The random runs follow the Bateman curve, with a scatter of about √N_D.
