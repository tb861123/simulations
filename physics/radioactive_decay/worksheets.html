Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Which one is next?

What to look for: No. Every nucleus has the same chance of decaying in the next second, and it does not 'age': one that has survived a long time is no more likely to go next. Decay is random: you cannot predict which nucleus decays next, or when. Yet the sample as a whole behaves very predictably.

Investigation 2: Half-life

What to look for: The same time every time: 70 seconds for protactinium-234m. In each half-life, half of the nuclei left decay, so the curve falls to ½, ¼, ⅛ … of the start. The activity (decays per second) halves in the same time, because it is proportional to the number left.

Investigation 3: Dice

What to look for: About 3.8 throws, whether you start with 100 or 10 000: each dice has a 1 in 6 chance each throw, so the number removed is always the same fraction of those left. Radioactive nuclei behave exactly like this. The dice model is how half-life experiments were first explained in schools.

Investigation 4: Small samples are noisy

What to look for: With 100 nuclei the number decaying in each step is a small, very random number, so the curve is jagged and the half-life you measure could be well off. With 10 000 the random ups and downs average out. The relative scatter goes as 1 ÷ √N: a hundred times more nuclei, ten times smoother.

Investigation 5: What stops alpha, beta and gamma?

What to look for: Alpha particles are stopped by a sheet of paper (or a few centimetres of air). Beta particles pass through paper but are stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium. Gamma rays pass through both and are only reduced by thick lead or concrete: every 12 mm of lead halves them. Alpha is the most ionising, gamma the least.

Investigation 6: Background radiation

What to look for: Even with no source the tube counts about 24 a minute: background radiation from radon gas, rocks and buildings, cosmic rays, food and medical sources. Measure it first and subtract it from every reading. When a source is far away or heavily shielded, its counts can be smaller than the background, and the corrected value is very uncertain.

Investigation 7: Further away

What to look for: It falls to about a quarter: the inverse-square law. The radiation spreads out over a sphere whose area grows as distance squared, so the tube catches a smaller share. Keeping your distance is one of the three simple ways to reduce your dose, with less time and more shielding. Handle sources with tongs.

Investigation 8: The right isotope for the job

What to look for: A medical tracer must give out gamma rays (alpha and beta would not get out of the body, and would do harm inside it), and must have a short half-life, so it is active long enough for the scan but almost gone in a day. Dating needs a long half-life, comparable with the age being measured: carbon-14 suits remains from a few hundred to about 50 000 years old.

Investigation 9: The decay constant A level

What to look for: ln N against t is a straight line, because N = N₀e^(−λt) gives ln N = ln N₀ − λt. Its gradient is −λ. For protactinium-234m, λ = 0.693 ÷ 70 s = 0.0099 per second. Near the end, when few nuclei are left, the points scatter: random fluctuations become large compared with N.

Investigation 10: Carbon dating A level

What to look for: 25% left means two half-lives: 11 460 years. After 20 000 years the fraction is e^(−λt) = 0.089. Beyond about 50 000 years (nearly nine half-lives) less than 0.2% remains and its count rate is swamped by the background, so the uncertainty becomes enormous.

Batch experiment 1: Measuring a half-life

Four times as many nuclei halves the relative scatter. With the 10²⁰ or so nuclei in a real sample the decay curve is perfectly smooth.

Batch experiment 3: The ln N graph

For small p, λ ≈ p. Larger p (a big chance each step) makes the steps coarse, and λ = −ln(1 − p) is a little larger than p.

Batch experiment 4: Random counts

Counts follow a Poisson distribution: the standard deviation is the square root of the mean. Counting for longer gives more counts and a smaller percentage uncertainty.
Theory (expected): 58.8
Result from a test run: mean 58.6, SD 7.9, SE 0.395 from 400 runs

Batch experiment 5: Inverse-square law

At large distances the corrected rate is small compared with the background, so its percentage uncertainty grows.
Theory (∝ 1/d²): 3: 714 · 4: 399 · 5: 254 · 7: 128 · 10: 61.5 · 14: 30.6 · 20: 14.4
Result from a test run: 3: 713 ± 1.84 · 4: 398 ± 1.03 · 5: 254 ± 0.925 · 7: 129 ± 0.546 · 10: 62.2 ± 0.374 · 14: 30.9 ± 0.279 · 20: 14.5 ± 0.222 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Exponential: the same thickness always removes the same fraction. There is no thickness that stops gamma rays completely. (The cobalt-60 source also gives out weak beta particles, which the first 1 mm of lead stops, so the experiment starts at 2 mm.)
