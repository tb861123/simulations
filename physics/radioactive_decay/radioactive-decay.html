Its short half-life means it falls to safe levels within a day of the scan, keeping the patient's dose low. Over 40 million Tc-99m procedures are done each year worldwide.

📐 Physics notes

Spec reference: Component 3, Topic 6 (Nuclear Decay), sections (a)–(h).

Key equations:

N = N₀ e^(−λt)
T½ = ln 2 / λ
A = λN
A = A₀ e^(−λt)

What this shows: radioactive decay is a random process: each nucleus independently has the same probability of decaying in a given time, whatever its history. As N increases the average behaviour gets smoother, approaching the exponential curve. The dice analogy shows the statistical nature: like rolling many dice and removing those showing a 1, the fraction removed each round stays constant, which gives an exponential decline.

Advanced context. T½ = ln 2/λ comes from N₀/2 = N₀ e^(−λT½). Decay is memoryless (a Poisson process): a nucleus has no "memory" of how long it has existed. The probability of decaying in the next short interval Δt is always λΔt, whatever its past.

Carbon dating uses the known atmospheric ¹⁴C/¹²C ratio as a starting point. At death the organism stops taking in ¹⁴C; measuring the remaining ratio gives t = −ln(N/N₀)/λ. Calibration curves (e.g. IntCal) correct for past changes in atmospheric ¹⁴C.

Spec reference: Component 3, Topic 6 (Nuclear Decay) — extension; links to Topic 7.

Key equations:

dN_A/dt = −λ_A N_A
dN_B/dt = λ_A N_A − λ_B N_B
Secular equilibrium: N_B / N_A = λ_A / λ_B when λ_A ≪ λ_B

What this shows: in a decay chain each daughter nuclide is itself radioactive. Its population grows because the parent decays and falls because it decays itself. The Bateman equations describe this linked system. The stacked area chart shows each nuclide's share; the total height always equals N₀ (no atoms are lost, they only change type).

Secular equilibrium: when the parent's half-life is much longer than the daughter's, the daughter reaches a steady state where its production rate (λ_A N_A) equals its decay rate (λ_B N_B): the two activities are equal. This is the U-238 / Th-234 case.

Transient equilibrium occurs when the parent lives longer than the daughter, but only by a modest factor (typically about 10×). After several daughter half-lives the daughter's activity settles at a fixed ratio to the parent's, A_B/A_A = λ_B/(λ_B − λ_A) (a little above 1), and both then fall together with the parent's half-life. Mo-99 / Tc-99m in hospital generators is an example.

No equilibrium arises when the daughter lives longer than the parent: the parent decays away and the daughter simply grows, then decays on its own.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Each nucleus is independent. It decays at a random time drawn from an exponential distribution with rate λ = −ln(1 − p) per second. So in every 1 s step each surviving nucleus decays with probability exactly p: the dice (binomial) model of the old WJEC page, but you can also watch between the steps.
  • The graph shows N at each whole second (the bars) and the theory N₀e^(−λt). The simulated half-life is where the bars first cross N₀/2 (straight-line interpolation between steps, as on the old page).
  • Geiger counter: each decay is detected with probability ε (efficiency and geometry together). Background counts arrive at random (a Poisson process) at B counts per second. Dead time, the different radiations (α, β, γ) and absorption on the way to the tube are left out.
  • Decay chains: the graph uses the exact Bateman solution. The stable end product is N₀ minus all the radioactive atoms (conservation). The animated sample is a separate random simulation of a few thousand atoms, so it follows the curves only roughly.
  • Simplified chains: the real U-238 chain has 14 steps to Pb-206 and the Th-232 chain 10 steps to Pb-208; here the short-lived steps are collapsed into one daughter. In the Rn-222 chain, Po-214 (T½ = 164 μs) is skipped between Bi-214 and Pb-210, and Pb-210 (T½ = 22.3 yr) is the end of the chain shown.
  • Tc-99m generator: milking removes all the technetium (Tc-99m and Tc-99) from the column at once and the Mo-99 stays. In reality a little Tc-99m is left behind and Mo-99 also decays directly to Tc-99 (about 12% of the time), which is ignored. Tc-99 (T½ = 211 000 yr) is treated as stable.
  • The one-nucleus animation runs at a steady pace to show the order of the decays; real waiting times are random and follow the half-lives.
  • Left out: branching decays (some nuclides can decay two ways), the energy and range of the radiation, and the effect of chemistry (radon is a gas and can escape from a sample).
