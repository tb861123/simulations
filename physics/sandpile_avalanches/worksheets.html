Sandpile Avalanches · Teacher notessandpile-avalanches.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Build up to the edge

What to look for: At first nothing topples. As the table fills, small avalanches start, then bigger ones, until avalanches as wide as the table appear. From then on the mean height stays near 2.1 grains a square (the exact value for an infinite table is 17/8 = 2.125): the pile has organised itself to the critical state, with no-one adjusting anything.

Investigation 2: A straight line on a log–log graph

What to look for: A straight line on log–log axes is a power law: P(s) ∝ s^(−τ). Doubling the size always makes an avalanche the same factor rarer, whatever the size: there is no 'typical' avalanche. The fitted τ is about 1.1 here. The line bends down at the end because no avalanche can be bigger than the table allows.

Investigation 3: All in one place

What to look for: An intricate, symmetric fractal pattern (the 'identity' patterns of the abelian sandpile are cousins of it). Remarkably, the final picture does not depend on the order in which squares topple (the sandpile is 'abelian'), so the same pattern always grows.

Investigation 4: Where do avalanches start?

What to look for: Big avalanches are solid in the middle with ragged, fractal edges, and some squares inside topple several times. Their shape depends on the exact pattern of 2s and 3s already in the pile: a tiny change can turn a small avalanche into a huge one.

Investigation 5: The hourglass

What to look for: The flow through the neck is close to steady whatever the height of sand above it (unlike water, which slows as the level falls), in a real hourglass because the grains arch over the neck and take the weight, and in this cell model because grains can only slide in from the sides one cell a step. Coarse grains roll further down the heap's slopes, so they collect at the edges and bottom: the mixture separates (segregation), as in cereal packets and gravel heaps.

Investigation 6: Angle of repose

What to look for: It grows at a fixed angle: grains slide down until the slope is just shallow enough to stop them. In this cell model the angle is 45°, because a grain can only slide diagonally; real dry sand rests at about 30–35°. The slope stays the same as the heap grows: like the sandpile above, the heap keeps itself on the edge of sliding.

Batch experiment 1: The power law: τ against table size

The fitted τ comes out at about 1.07–1.08 for every size: the same power law, whatever the table, which is what 'critical' means. A simple straight-line fit is biased by the bend at the largest sizes; studies that allow for the edges of the table and use far bigger tables find values of about 1.1 to 1.3.
Result from a test run: 16: 1.07 ± 0.00547 · 32: 1.06 ± 0.00698 · 64: 1.07 ± 0.0085 · 128: 1.08 ± 0.00553 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Mean height of the critical pile

Small tables sit a little below 2.125 because grains near the edge are easily lost; the mean climbs towards 17/8 = 2.125 (worked out exactly by Priezzhev in 1994) as the table grows. Nothing sets this number from outside: the pile finds it.
Theory (infinite table: 17/8): 8: 2.13 · 16: 2.13 · 32: 2.13 · 64: 2.13 · 128: 2.13
Result from a test run: 8: 2.02 ± 0.0101 · 16: 2.04 ± 0.0221 · 32: 2.08 ± 0.0079 · 64: 2.11 ± 0.00304 · 128: 2.11 ± 0.00104 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Mean avalanche size

The mean is known exactly (Dhar 1990): it is the average number of times a grain added at random must topple on its way off the table, which grows roughly as L². The simulation matches it to within the scatter. This is unusual: for most critical systems nothing is known exactly.
Theory (exact (Dhar 1990)): 8: 3.46 · 16: 11.3 · 32: 40.6 · 48: 87.8 · 64: 153 · 96: 337 · 128: 594
Result from a test run: 8: 3.46 ± 0.00534 · 16: 11.4 ± 0.0183 · 32: 40.6 ± 0.11 · 48: 87.8 ± 0.19 · 64: 153 ± 0.139 · 96: 337 ± 0.248 · 128: 592 ± 1.59 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Pouring a mixture: do the grains separate?

With no extra rolling, both kinds of grain behave the same and the outer slopes hold about half coarse grains, like the mix poured in. The more readily coarse grains roll, the more of them end up at the foot of the heap, while fine grains stay near the middle: segregation. Real heaps separate for similar reasons (big, round grains roll over small ones), as you see in cereal packets and gravel heaps.
Theory (no separation): 0: 0.5 · 0.25: 0.5 · 0.5: 0.5 · 0.75: 0.5 · 1: 0.5
Result from a test run: 0: 0.496 ± 0.00232 · 0.25: 0.599 ± 0.00367 · 0.5: 0.673 ± 0.0114 · 0.75: 0.73 ± 0.00215 · 1: 0.762 ± 0.00329 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)
