Projectile Motion · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · How much do 200 shots scatter?

What this shows. Each run is one shot from a slightly imperfect launcher. The ranges form a bell-shaped spread around the formula R = v²sin2θ/g = 10.2 m. Almost all of the spread comes from the speed: R ∝ v², so a 2% speed error gives a 4% range error. At 45° a small angle error hardly matters, because sin 2θ is at its peak and the graph of R against θ is flat there. The mean sits a whisker below the formula: any angle error, either way, makes the range slightly shorter.

2 · Does the range grow as v²?

What this shows. On log–log axes a power law is a straight line whose gradient is the power. R = v²sin2θ/g gives a gradient of 2: double the speed and the ball goes four times as far. The small scatter at each speed is the launcher's random error; it grows with speed because it is a percentage.

3 · Which angle goes furthest?

What this shows. The points follow R = v²sin2θ/g: highest at 45°, and symmetrical about it, so 30° and 60° (or 20° and 70°) give the same range. Near 45° the curve is flat, so small angle errors cost almost nothing; at very low or very high angles the same error changes the range much more.

4 · Air resistance and the best angle

What this shows. The line is the no-air formula; the points are the RK4 model with quadratic drag. Every throw falls short of the formula, and high throws lose most (they stay in the air longer, so drag acts for longer). The best angle drops to about 40°. Heavier, denser balls (try a cannonball) are much closer to the formula.

5 · Faster sideways, longer in the air?

What this shows. However fast the ball is thrown sideways, it lands after the same time, t = √(2h/g) = 2.02 s, as a ball simply dropped. The horizontal motion does not affect the vertical motion. The scatter grows with speed only because a small error in the angle gives a fast ball a little upward or downward speed.

6 · Falling with air resistance

What this shows. With no air the landing speed keeps growing as √(2gh) (dashed grey line). With drag it levels off at the terminal velocity, v_T = √(2mg/ρC_DA) ≈ 22 m/s for a tennis ball, when drag balances the weight. The points sit on the exact drag formula (yellow), which checks the RK4 solver. There is no scatter: a dropped ball has no launch errors.
