📐 Physics notes

Spec reference. Advanced: WJEC Eduqas A level Physics, Component 1, Topic 2 (Kinematics), sections (f) and (g). Standard: Edexcel International GCSE Physics 4PH1, Section 1 Forces and motion, 1(b) Movement and position and 1(c) Forces, movement, shape and momentum.

Key equations:

x = v₀ cos θ · t
y = v₀ sin θ · t − ½ g t²
R = v₀² sin 2θ / g
H = v₀² sin² θ / (2g)

What this shows: projectile motion separates into two independent components. Horizontally there is no force (ignoring air resistance), so vₓ = v₀ cos θ stays the same throughout the flight. Vertically, gravity gives a constant downward acceleration g, so vᵧ falls steadily from v₀ sin θ to zero at the top, then grows downwards until landing. The result is a parabola. Range is greatest at θ = 45° with no air resistance.

Horizontal velocity is CONSTANT (no air resistance). This is the key idea: the x and y motions are completely independent.

Energy: at launch all the energy is kinetic (½mv₀²). At the top, KE = ½mv₀² cos²θ (only the horizontal motion is left) and GPE = mgH.

Advanced context. Quadratic drag arises because the projectile must push a column of air out of its way. The drag force is FD = ½ρCDAv², where ρ is the air density, CD the drag coefficient, A the cross-sectional area and v the speed. Because drag depends on v² it is negligible at low speeds but dominant at high speeds. The equations have no simple formula solution, so they are solved numerically (RK4 here). Terminal velocity is reached when drag exactly balances the weight: vT = √(2mg / ρCDA).

Magnus effect: a spinning ball drags the air next to it round with it. On one side this adds to the flow past the ball (faster, so lower pressure — Bernoulli); on the other side it opposes it (slower, higher pressure). The pressure difference gives a force at right angles to the velocity. Topspin (ω > 0) pushes the ball down (shorter range); backspin (ω < 0) pushes it up (longer range). Footballers, golfers, tennis players and cricketers all use it.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • No air resistance (Standard): exact formulas. Only the weight acts, so a = −g straight down and the horizontal speed never changes. The ball is a point: its size does not matter.
  • Flat, level ground at y = 0. A ball launched from a height h₀ lands when y = 0: T = (v₀ sin θ + √(v₀² sin²θ + 2gh₀)) / g.
  • g is constant (9.81 m/s² on Earth). In reality g falls very slightly with height, and the Earth is curved and spinning: for a ball these effects are far too small to see.
  • Air resistance (Advanced): FD = ½ρCDAv², with ρ = 1.225 kg/m³ and the velocity measured relative to the moving air (wind). CD is kept constant; for real balls it changes with speed (a golf ball's dimples make it drop sharply above about 15 m/s).
  • Magnus force: a = ½ρACLr ω v / m at right angles to the velocity, with lift coefficient CL = 0.5 and the radius r worked out from the area (A = πr²). The spin stays the same through the flight; really it slowly decays.
  • Numerical method: RK4 with 5 ms steps; the landing point is found by interpolating between the last two steps.
  • Left out: the ball bouncing or rolling after it lands, side-spin (swerve to the left or right), how a javelin or shuttlecock turns to point along its path, and changes of air density with height and temperature.
  • Batch runs: a real launcher is not perfect. Each run adds a random error to the launch speed (SD as a % of v₀) and to the angle (SD in degrees), drawn from normal distributions.
