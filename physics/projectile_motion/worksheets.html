Projectile Motion · Teacher notesprojectile-motion.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Two motions at once

What to look for: The ground shadow moves equal distances in equal times: the horizontal velocity never changes, because no force acts sideways. The wall shadow slows as it rises, stops at the top and speeds up as it falls: the weight gives a steady downward acceleration g. Look at the vₓ and vᵧ graph: vₓ is flat, vᵧ is a straight line sloping down at −9.81 m/s every second.

Investigation 2: Dropped or thrown?

What to look for: They land together, whatever the throwing speed. Throwing sideways adds horizontal velocity but no vertical velocity, so the time to fall is set only by the height: t = √(2h/g) = 2.02 s for 20 m. Faster throws just go further in the same time.

Investigation 3: Which angle goes furthest?

What to look for: 45° goes furthest on level ground with no air (22.9 m). 30° and 60° land in the same place: complementary angles give the same range, because the range is v² sin 2θ ÷ g and sin 60° = sin 120°. The high shot stays up longer but moves sideways more slowly.

Investigation 4: Hit the target

What to look for: Rearrange: v = √(R g ÷ sin 2θ). At 45°, sin 2θ = 1, so v = √(R g). For a target 25 m away, v = √(25 × 9.81) = 15.7 m/s. The aiming guide shows the path before you launch, if you want to check.

Investigation 5: On the Moon

What to look for: Range and time of flight both depend on 1/g, so on the Moon the ball goes 9.81 ÷ 1.62 ≈ 6 times further and stays up 6 times longer; the greatest height is also 6 times greater. There is no air on the Moon either, so the formulas are exact there.

Investigation 6: Energy on the way

What to look for: At the top the ball still moves sideways, so KE is not zero: it is ½ m vₓ². KE turns into GPE on the way up and back on the way down; with no air the total stays exactly constant. The speed at landing equals the launch speed (from the same height).

Investigation 7: Air resistance changes the best angle A level

What to look for: The best angle falls to about 40° and the range is about two-thirds of what it would be in a vacuum. The path is lopsided: it comes down more steeply than it went up, because drag has slowed the horizontal motion. Light objects with big areas (shuttlecocks) are worst affected: they fall almost vertically.

Investigation 8: Terminal velocity A level

What to look for: It levels off at about 22 m/s: the terminal velocity, where drag ½ρC_D A v² equals the weight, so the resultant force and the acceleration are zero. v_T = √(2mg ÷ ρC_D A). Without air it would land at √(2gh) = 44 m/s.

Investigation 9: Spin: the Magnus effect A level

What to look for: Backspin drags air down behind the ball, so the air pushes the ball up: a lift force at right angles to the velocity. The ball climbs and carries much further. Topspin pushes it down, making it dip: tennis players use it to hit hard and still land in the court.

Batch experiment 1: How much do shots scatter?

The ranges form a bell-shaped spread around the formula's value. Speed errors matter much more than angle errors at 45°, because the range is at its maximum there and barely changes with angle; but it goes as v², so a 2% speed error gives about a 4% range error.
Theory (theory): 10.2
Result from a test run: mean 10.2, SD 0.399, SE 0.0282 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 8: Backspin and the golf drive
