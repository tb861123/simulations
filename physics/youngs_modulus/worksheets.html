Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Measure E for steel (Searle's method)

What this shows. Each run is one student's experiment: 8 loads up to half the elastic limit, the extension read on a vernier scale (random error SD 0.01 mm) and the diameter measured once with a micrometer (SD 0.005 mm). The results form a bell-shaped spread around the true 200 GPa. Most of the spread comes from the diameter: a 1% error in d is a 2% error in A = πd²/4, because d is squared. That is why you measure d in several places and average.

2 · Why use a long, thin wire?

What this shows. Only the extension reading error this time (perfect micrometer). A longer wire stretches more for the same stress, so the same ±0.01 mm reading error is a smaller fraction of the extension: the error bars shrink roughly as 1/L₀. The means stay on the true value: a short wire is not wrong, just less precise. A thin wire helps in the same way.

3 · Is extension proportional to force?

What this shows. Log–log axes, because the extension grows by a factor of a thousand. The theory line is Hooke's law ΔL = FL₀/(AE), a straight line of gradient 1 here. Up to about 70 N (copper's elastic limit, 70 MPa on 1 mm²) the points lie on it: extension is proportional to force. Beyond it the extension grows much faster than the line, as the copper yields and stretches plastically, until it breaks at 220 N.

5 · Why is glass so unpredictable?

What this shows. Every glass fibre has tiny surface scratches, and it breaks at its worst one. So identical-looking fibres break at very different forces: here the SD is about 19% of the mean (the curve is a normal distribution with the same mean and SD; the real spread, a Weibull distribution, has a longer tail of weak fibres). Engineers therefore design glass with large safety factors. Try steel instead (record breaking force for your own wire): its strength varies by only about 4%.

6 · Breaking force against area

What this shows. The breaking force rises in proportion to the area (the theory line F = UTS × A), so the breaking STRESS is the same for every wire: that is why tables list the UTS in MPa rather than a breaking force. The small scatter at each step is the wire-to-wire variation of steel (about 4%).
