Materials Science · Teacher notesyoungs-modulus.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Searle's method

What to look for: Gradient = ΔL ÷ F = L ÷ (AE), so E = L ÷ (A × gradient), with L in mm and A in mm² to give E in N/mm² (MPa). Average several diameter readings: A = πd²/4, so a 1% error in d becomes a 2% error in E. The reference wire beside the test wire cancels out sagging of the support and changes in temperature.

Investigation 2: Stay below the elastic limit

What to look for: Copper's elastic limit is only about 60 MPa, about 0.6 kg on this wire. Beyond it the points curve upwards (plastic stretching), and on unloading the wire stays longer. Only the straight part gives E, so in the practical you keep the load well below the elastic limit.

Investigation 3: Hooke's law and the Young modulus

What to look for: On the straight part, stress ÷ strain = 200 GPa for steel whatever the wire's size: E is a property of the material. The spring constant k = F ÷ x does depend on the size: k = EA ÷ L, so a thicker or shorter wire is stiffer.

Investigation 4: Reading a ductile metal's curve

What to look for: Straight line to about 285 MPa (elastic: it would return to its original length). Then it yields: plastic flow at almost constant stress, then work hardening up to the UTS (about 415 MPa). After the UTS one part gets thinner (a neck), so the force it can carry falls; it breaks at the neck in a ductile 'cup and cone' fracture.

Investigation 5: Permanent stretch and work hardening

What to look for: Unloading follows a straight line of gradient E, not the loading curve, so it keeps a permanent (plastic) strain of nearly 10%. Reloaded, it stays elastic up to the stress it had reached before: work hardening has raised its elastic limit. The dislocations have tangled and block each other.

Investigation 6: Tough or brittle?

What to look for: Glass obeys Hooke's law almost to fracture and breaks at a tiny strain (under 0.1%): very little area, so very little energy absorbed. It is brittle. Copper's stress is not much higher, but it stretches by about 45%, so the area (energy absorbed) is thousands of times bigger: it is tough. Strong (high breaking stress) is not the same as tough (lots of energy absorbed).

Investigation 7: Rubber and hysteresis

What to look for: Stretching straightens the tangled chain molecules; on the way back the chains rub past each other, so less work comes back out than was put in. The area of the loop is the energy per unit volume turned into internal (thermal) energy: the rubber warms up. It obeys Hooke's law only roughly, and its Young modulus is very low.

Investigation 8: An edge dislocation

What to look for: The extra half-plane moves one row of atoms at a time: only the bonds along one line break and re-form at each step, like moving a ruck along a carpet. When it reaches the side it leaves a step one atom high: the top has slipped. The perfect crystal has to break a whole plane of bonds at once, so it needs many times more stress. This is why real metals are much weaker than the 'theoretical' strength, and why they are ductile.

Investigation 9: Foreign atoms

What to look for: The large atoms distort the lattice around them, so the dislocation gets stuck: it needs a larger shear stress to push past. That is why alloys (like steel: iron with carbon) are stronger and harder than pure metals, and less ductile.

Investigation 10: Grain boundaries

What to look for: At a boundary the slip planes change direction, so dislocations stop and pile up. A pile-up pushes hard on the boundary; when it pushes hard enough, slip breaks through and the metal yields. Small grains hold only short pile-ups, so a larger applied stress is needed: more grain boundaries, stronger metal. Each test adds a point to the graph on the right.

Investigation 11: Cracks concentrate stress

What to look for: The bonds at the tip of the notch are stretched several times more than the bonds far away (the 'stress concentration' readout). They reach their breaking strain first; then the next bond takes even more load, so the crack runs across: brittle fracture. A deeper or sharper crack concentrates the stress more, so the material breaks at a lower stress. Brittle materials are weak in tension because of surface scratches.

Investigation 12: Toughened glass

What to look for: In toughened glass the surface is squeezed (compressed) and the inside stretched. A surface crack cannot open until the applied pull has first cancelled the compression, so a much larger stress is needed to break it. Prestressed concrete uses the same idea: steel tendons keep the concrete in compression.

Investigation 13: Crystalline or amorphous?

What to look for: Yes: glass has no regular planes, so there is nothing for dislocations to glide on: no plastic flow at all. It is brittle like the ceramic, and a crack still concentrates the stress at its tip.

Investigation 14: Cross-links

What to look for: Cross-links tie the chains together. More cross-links: stiffer (higher Young modulus) and the chains run out of slack sooner, so it cannot stretch as far. Polythene has no cross-links, so its chains slide past each other: it stretches plastically, a neck travels along it ('cold drawing'), and it stays long when released.

Investigation 15: Theoretical strength A level

What to look for: Here the perfect crystal slips at roughly 1.5 model units: around one tenth of its shear modulus (its gradient on the graph). The dislocated crystal slips at a small fraction of that. Real metals yield at around G ÷ 1000 or less because they contain millions of dislocations per mm²; whiskers grown without dislocations come close to the theoretical strength.

Investigation 16: Hall–Petch A level

What to look for: σy = σ0 + k ÷ √d, so a graph of σy against d^(−½) is a straight line; the gradient is k (here about 1000 MPa √μm, i.e. about 1 MPa √m) and the intercept σ0 is the friction stress of a single crystal. The Batch run does this with repeats.

Batch experiment 1: Searle's method: measuring E for steel

A bell-shaped spread around 200 GPa. Most of it comes from the diameter: A = πd²/4, so a 1% error in d is a 2% error in E. That is why you measure d in several places and average.
Theory (true E): 200
Result from a test run: mean 200, SD 5.75, SE 0.407 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 2: Why use a long, thin wire?

Short wires give widely scattered answers: they stretch so little that the ±0.01 mm reading error is a big fraction of the extension. A longer (or thinner) wire stretches more, so the same reading error matters less. The mean is right either way; the precision is not.

Batch experiment 5: Perfect crystal or dislocation?

The perfect crystal must break a whole plane of bonds at once. A dislocation lets the slip happen one row at a time, at a small fraction of the stress (here about a sixth).

Batch experiment 6: Crack length and breaking stress

Deeper cracks concentrate the stress more, so the solid breaks at a lower applied stress. The Griffith line fits from about 4 atoms deep. Very shallow notches break at less than it predicts, because Griffith's equation assumes a crack far longer than the spacing of the atoms. Real glass has scratches thousands of atoms deep, which is why it breaks at about 50 MPa instead of several GPa.

Batch experiment 7: Scatter in the strength of glass

Brittle strength scatters a lot, because it depends on the worst scratch, which is a matter of chance. Engineers using glass or ceramics need big safety factors; metals are far more predictable.
Result from a test run: mean 57.4, SD 9.27, SE 0.535 from 300 runs

Batch experiment 8: Rubber: energy lost per cycle

The loop gets bigger, faster than in proportion, as the chains straighten and the stress climbs. This heat is why car tyres warm up as they roll.

Batch experiment 9: Toughness of materials

The brittle materials absorb almost nothing (well under 1 MJ per m³) because they break at a tiny strain. Ductile metals absorb thousands of times more because they stretch plastically, by tens of percent, before breaking.
