📐 Physics notes

Spec reference. Advanced: WJEC Eduqas A level Physics, Component 1, Topic 3: Deformation of solids — stress, strain and Young's modulus (and the specified practical: the Young modulus of a metal wire). Standard: Edexcel International GCSE Physics 4PH1, Section 1(c): the practical on extension against force for springs, metal wires and rubber bands, Hooke's law and elastic behaviour.

Key equations:

σ = F / A (Pa)
ε = ΔL / L₀ (no units)
E = σ / ε = F L₀ / (A ΔL) (Pa)
k = E A / L₀ (N m⁻¹)

What this shows: Young's modulus E measures a material's stiffness: how much stress is needed to produce a given strain in the elastic region. Below the elastic limit the material obeys Hooke's law and returns to its original shape when the load is removed. Beyond the yield point permanent (plastic) deformation occurs. The UTS is the maximum stress the material can withstand before necking leads to fracture.

Advanced context. The area under the elastic part of the stress–strain curve is the modulus of resilience (energy stored elastically per unit volume). The total area up to fracture is the modulus of toughness. The hysteresis loop shows energy dissipated as heat during a loading–unloading cycle in the plastic region (Bauschinger effect). Rubber's J-shaped curve comes from entropy-elastic (entropic) mechanisms in the polymer chains. The elastic energy stored in a wire is ½FΔL, or ½σε per unit volume.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Stress and strain: σ = F/A uses the original area (engineering stress), so the stress does not rise as the wire gets thinner while it necks.
  • Metals and bone (ductile): a straight line σ = Eε up to the yield point; then a parabola rising to the UTS, halfway between the yield strain and the fracture strain; then necking, where the stress falls to 5% of the UTS at the fracture strain.
  • Glass (brittle): a straight line up to fracture at the UTS (strain UTS/E ≈ 0.05%). No plastic region.
  • Rubber: a J-shaped curve σ ∝ ε + ε³/3 + ε⁵/5 reaching the UTS at 500% strain. All elastic: it goes back to its original length. (Real rubber unloads along a lower curve, so it gets warm when stretched and released.)
  • Hanging a load controls the force, not the extension. So once the force passes the UTS the wire cannot hold it and breaks; the falling part of the curve (necking) is only seen in a machine that controls the extension.
  • Unloading after plastic stretching follows a straight line of gradient E, so the wire ends up permanently longer (the "permanent set"). Loading again climbs back up that line to where it left the curve.
  • Batch runs: a Searle's-apparatus experiment with random reading errors (extension scale and micrometer, normal distributions), and breaking strengths that vary from wire to wire because of tiny flaws (a Weibull distribution: wide for glass, narrow for metals).
  • Left out: the wire's change in area while it stretches (true stress), temperature, how fast the load is applied (creep, strain rate), work hardening on reloading beyond the old maximum, the weight of the wire itself, and the elastic limit being slightly different from the limit of proportionality. Material values are typical; real alloys vary a lot.
