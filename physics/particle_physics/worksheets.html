Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Inside the atom

What to look for: 6 protons (+1, mass 1), 8 neutrons (0, mass 1), 6 electrons (−1, mass about 1/1840). Atomic number Z = 6 (protons), mass number A = 14 (protons + neutrons).

Investigation 2: Isotopes

What to look for: Isotopes have the same number of protons (same element, same chemistry) but different numbers of neutrons, so different mass numbers. Carbon-14 is radioactive (β⁻ decay to nitrogen-14).

Investigation 3: Beta-minus decay

What to look for: n → p + e⁻ (+ an antineutrino). The electron is the beta particle. Z goes up by 1, A stays the same: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + β⁻.

Investigation 4: Proton: uud A level

What to look for: u (+⅔) + u (+⅔) + d (−⅓) = +1; B = ⅓ × 3 = 1. Neutron udd: ⅔ − ⅓ − ⅓ = 0.

Investigation 5: Mesons A level

What to look for: π⁺ = ud̄: charge ⅔ + ⅓ = +1, B = ⅓ − ⅓ = 0. Mesons are a quark and an antiquark; two quarks (B = ⅔) is not allowed.

Investigation 6: Is it allowed? A level

What to look for: Charge balances (+1 → +1) but baryon number goes 1 → 0 and lepton number 0 → −1: forbidden. Proton decay has never been seen (lifetime > 10³⁴ years).

Investigation 7: Strangeness A level

What to look for: Production: S 0 → −1 + 1 = 0, a strong interaction (strange particles made in pairs). Decay: S −1 → 0, so only the weak interaction can do it: slow, about 10⁻¹⁰ s. (Strangeness is beyond the spec.)

Investigation 8: Feynman: β⁻ decay A level

What to look for: A W⁻ boson: a d quark becomes a u quark and emits a W⁻, which decays to an electron and an electron antineutrino. (Feynman diagrams are beyond the spec.)

Batch experiment 1: How often is charge conserved by chance?

About 30%: charge alone rules out most random reactions.
Theory (exact): 29.6
Result from a test run: mean 29.6, SD 2.02, SE 0.202 from 100 runs

Batch experiment 2: Q, B and L all at once?

Under 5%: the conservation laws together are very restrictive, which is why physicists use them to predict which reactions can happen.
Theory (exact): 4.5
Result from a test run: mean 4.49, SD 0.836, SE 0.0836 from 100 runs

Batch experiment 3: More products, less chance?

The spread of possible total charges widens with more particles, so hitting the right one exactly becomes less likely.
Theory (exact): 1: 35.5 · 2: 29.6 · 3: 25.9 · 4: 23.3 · 5: 21.3
Result from a test run: 1: 35 ± 0.372 · 2: 29.7 ± 0.652 · 3: 25.5 ± 0.57 · 4: 23 ± 0.7 · 5: 21.4 ± 0.628 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Does strangeness make it rarer?

Only about 2%: strangeness adds one more law that strong interactions must obey.
Theory (exact): 2.01
Result from a test run: mean 2.03, SD 0.672, SE 0.0672 from 100 runs

Batch experiment 5: Why are beta electrons spread out?

A spread from 0 to 0.78 MeV, not a single value: a third particle (the antineutrino) carries away the rest. This puzzle led Pauli to predict the neutrino in 1930.
Theory (mean of the spectrum): 0.303
Result from a test run: mean 0.301, SD 0.165, SE 0.00521 from 1000 runs
