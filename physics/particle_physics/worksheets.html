Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · How often is charge conserved by chance?

What this shows. Each run builds 500 random reactions of the form A → B + C and counts the ones where the charges balance. About 30% do. The exact chance comes from counting every one of the 20 × 20 × 20 combinations. Runs scatter around it with an SD of √(p(1 − p)/500) ≈ 2%, a binomial spread.

2 · Q, B and L all at once?

What this shows. Needing charge, baryon number and lepton number to balance together is much stricter: only about 4.5% of random reactions pass. Real particle reactions are a tiny, special set: nature only allows the ones that obey every law.

3 · More products, less chance?

What this shows. With more products there are more possible total charges, so a random set is less likely to land on exactly the right one. The chance falls from about 35% with one product to about 21% with five, matching the exact count at every step.

4 · Does strangeness make it rarer?

What this shows. For strong interactions strangeness must balance too. Only about 1 in 50 random two-to-two reactions pass all four checks. The spread of the runs is the binomial √(p(1 − p)/n), with n = 2000 reactions per run.

5 · Why are beta electrons spread out?

What this shows. If a neutron decayed into just a proton and an electron, every electron would get the same energy, 0.782 MeV. Instead the energies range from 0 up to 0.782 MeV with a mean of about 0.30 MeV, because a third particle, the antineutrino, takes a random share. This is how Pauli predicted the neutrino in 1930. (The spectrum is not a normal curve: the blue curve only shows its mean and SD.)
