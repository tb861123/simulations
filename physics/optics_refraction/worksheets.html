Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Bending light

What to look for: Going into glass the ray bends towards the normal (the dashed line at right angles to the surface); coming out it bends away again. sin i ÷ sin r comes to the same number every time, 1.5: the refractive index of the glass. That is Snell's law. Because the top and bottom faces are parallel, the ray leaves parallel to the way it came in, shifted sideways.

Investigation 2: Trapped inside: the critical angle

What to look for: As the angle inside grows, the escaping ray bends closer and closer to the surface and gets fainter while the reflected ray gets brighter. At the critical angle (sin c = 1/n, 41.8° for this glass) the escaping ray skims the surface; beyond it nothing gets out at all: total internal reflection. It only happens when light tries to leave a denser material.

Investigation 3: Light down a pipe

What to look for: Each time the ray meets the wall it does so at more than the critical angle, so it is totally internally reflected and none leaks out. Bend the fibre too tightly and the ray meets the outside of the bend too squarely: light escapes. Real fibres are hair-thin, so that even a tight-looking loop is a gentle bend for the light. They carry the internet as pulses of infrared.

Investigation 4: Splitting white light

What to look for: The glass has a slightly higher refractive index for violet than for red, so violet is bent most and red least: the colours are spread into a spectrum (dispersion). Newton showed in 1666 that the prism does not add colour: white light is a mixture of all the colours. A second prism the other way up puts them back together: try adding one.

Investigation 5: A lens is just curved glass

What to look for: Each ray is bent by Snell's law at both faces. Because the faces are curved, rays further from the middle meet steeper glass and are bent more, so they all cross at the principal focus. A more curved lens, or one with a higher refractive index, bends light more and has a shorter focal length. Look closely: rays from the edge cross slightly before those near the middle (spherical aberration).

Investigation 6: Making an image

What to look for: Rays from each lamp meet again at a point on the far side: a real image, upside down. A camera, a projector and your eye work like this. Bring the object closer and the image moves away and gets bigger. Closer than the focal length, the rays leaving the lens still spread out and never meet: they only appear to come from a point behind the lens (a virtual image). That is a magnifying glass.

Investigation 7: The diverging lens

What to look for: The rays spread out as if they had all come from a single point behind the lens, its principal focus. A diverging lens cannot form a real image of a real object. It is used in glasses for short-sighted people, to spread the light a little before the eye's own lens brings it to a focus.

Investigation 8: A rainbow in one raindrop

What to look for: The rays that reflect once inside the drop come back out bunched at about 42° to the incoming sunlight, red slightly more (42°) than violet (40°), because water bends violet more. Every drop sends you a different colour depending on its angle from your eye, so you see a bow: red on the outside, at 42° from the point opposite the Sun. Faint rays that reflect twice make the secondary bow.

Investigation 9: Why diamonds sparkle

What to look for: Diamond's critical angle is only 24°, so light inside it is totally internally reflected again and again before it finds a face it can leave through, and diamond's strong dispersion spreads the colours widely by then: flashes of colour ('fire'). Gem cutters choose the angles of the faces so that most of the light comes back out of the top.

Investigation 10: Why does light bend at all? A level

What to look for: Light travels more slowly in glass: v = c/n, so the pulses bunch up. If the beam meets the surface at an angle, one side of each line of pulses (a wavefront) enters the glass first and slows down while the other side is still in air, so the whole front swings round. The change of direction that keeps the fronts continuous is exactly Snell's law.

Batch experiment 5: The rainbow angle

Rays hit the drop at every height, but far more of them come out near 42° than at any other angle: the bars pile up at the right-hand edge. That bunching is the rainbow; no light at all comes out beyond it, which is why the sky is darker outside the bow.
Theory (rainbow angle): 42.3
Result from a test run: mean 25.7, SD 13.3, SE 0.542 from 600 runs

Batch experiment 6: How tightly can a fibre bend?

About 4% is always lost by reflection at each end face. Round a gentle bend, every reflection off the wall is total, so nothing more is lost. Below a certain radius rays start to meet the wall at less than the critical angle and leak out.
