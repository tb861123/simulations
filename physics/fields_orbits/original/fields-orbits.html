How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Point sources. Every charge and mass is a point. The field and potential at each pixel are the exact sums E = Σ kQ/r² (as vectors) and V = Σ kQ/r, or g = Σ GM/r² and Vg = Σ −GM/r. (The old app read them off a 60 × 60 grid; here nothing is interpolated.)
  • A flat slice. Real fields are 3-D; the map shows the plane the charges sit in. In this flat picture the spacing of the field lines is only a rough guide to strength: in 3-D the density of lines falls as 1/r², in a 2-D drawing as 1/r.
  • Colour map and equipotentials use a squashed (inverse-sinh) scale so both the strong field near a charge and the weak field far away show up. So equipotentials are drawn at equal steps on that scale, not at equal steps in volts. The potential is clipped very close to a source, where it heads to infinity.
  • Quality sets how many points the colour map and equipotentials use (Low 70 × 70 up to Ultra 340 × 340), how many field lines start from each source and the step along each line. It changes how sharp the picture is, not the physics. While you drag a source the map is drawn at Low quality, then redrawn.
  • Dropped test particles (click on the map) show the direction of the force only: they get a steady push along the field and their speed is not to scale. A positive test charge is used in the electric map.
  • Launched test particle (Advanced): real units. RK4 with a 5 ms time step for up to 8 s, stopping at the edge of the map. The charges and masses are held still and the test particle does not change the field. No magnetic effects, radiation or relativity, so total energy KE + PE should stay constant.
  • Gravitational presets put planets and stars a few metres apart, as the old app did. They show the shape of the field; the numbers at the probe are not realistic (real bodies would overlap).
  • Orbits: Newton's law of gravitation between point masses, nothing else: no other planets, no air drag near the Earth, no relativity. The size of the central body is ignored (and it is not drawn to scale), so a slow launch can pass "through" the Earth. RK4 with an adaptive time step (about 500 steps per orbit; shorter steps near the central body).
  • Time in the orbit view: 1× shows one orbit in about 10 s, whatever the real period. The clock shows the real time that has passed. Open orbits restart once the planet is 5 r₀ away, as in the old app.
  • Kepler's second law sectors are 8 slices covering equal times (one eighth of the period each), worked out from the simulated orbit. Their areas are measured, not assumed.
  • Launching your own planet by dragging: dragging a quarter of the picture's height gives the circular speed at that point. The path is worked out with RK4 (the old app used a simpler Euler method).
  • Two-body (Advanced): the star and planet pull each other equally and opposite (Newton's 3rd law), so their centre of mass never moves; the check shows any drift from rounding.
  • Hohmann transfer: two instant engine burns, circular start and target orbits in the same plane, the planet's own gravity ignored. Real missions (e.g. Earth to Mars) also have to climb out of the Earth's gravity well.
  • Galaxy rotation curve: all visible mass at the centre (real galaxies are discs, so near the centre the real curve rises), and the dark-matter halo simplified to a constant speed added in quadrature: v² = GM/r + vDM².
  • Binary stars: circular orbits only (many real binaries are elliptical), no mass transfer, tides or gravitational-wave decay. Radial velocity vr = v sin i cos φ. The Doppler wobble of a star with a planet is covered in the separate Exoplanet hunting simulation.
  • Batch runs use exact formulas and fixed integration settings (they do not depend on the Quality setting), with random reading errors where a real experiment would have them (a field probe: SD set in the panel, plus ±1 cm in where it is held; binary-star separation and period).
