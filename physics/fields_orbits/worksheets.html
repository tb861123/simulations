Fields and Orbits · Teacher notesfields-orbits.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The shape of a field

What to look for: The field lines spread out evenly in all directions, so the same number of lines pass through a bigger and bigger sphere: double the distance and the field strength falls to a quarter (an inverse-square law; see the right-hand graph). The equipotentials are circles, always at right angles to the field lines, and they are closer together where the field is stronger.

Investigation 2: Opposites attract

What to look for: Field lines start on positive charge and end on negative charge. An ion released from rest starts off along the field line, because that is the direction of the force on it. But once it is moving, its momentum carries it wide on the bends: a field line shows the direction of the force, not the path of a moving particle.

Investigation 3: The place with no field

What to look for: Exactly half-way between them the two pushes are equal and opposite, so the field is zero. The potential there is far from zero: it is the sum of two positive potentials. On the tilted view the point is a saddle (a pass between two hills): flat, so no force, but high up. Field strength is the slope of the potential, not its height.

Investigation 4: A uniform field

What to look for: Between the plates the field lines are parallel and evenly spaced: the field has the same strength and direction everywhere (a uniform field), and the equipotentials are evenly spaced parallel lines. An ion crossing it feels a constant sideways force, so it follows a parabola, exactly like a ball thrown sideways under gravity. This is how an oscilloscope and an inkjet printer steer charged particles.

Investigation 5: Falling without landing

What to look for: Speed. Too slow and the path meets the ground. At exactly the circular speed (the dotted path closes into a circle) gravity bends the path at just the rate the Earth curves away beneath it: the satellite is always falling but never gets nearer. Faster gives an ellipse, and above √2 times the circular speed (the escape speed) it never returns. The higher the orbit, the slower the satellite needs to go.

Investigation 6: Kepler's laws

What to look for: The nearer a planet is to the Sun, the stronger the pull and the faster it goes; and its orbit is shorter too, so its year is much shorter (T² is proportional to r³: Kepler's third law). The comet speeds up as it falls towards the Sun and slows as it climbs away, but the line joining it to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times (Kepler's second law). Gravitational potential energy turns into kinetic energy and back: see the left-hand graph.

Investigation 7: The gravity well

What to look for: The depth of the well at any point is the energy per kilogram needed to climb out from there. A satellite in orbit rolls round the inside wall without enough kinetic energy to reach the rim. Give it more than the escape speed and it has enough to climb all the way out. The slope of the wall is the field strength, steepest near the Earth. (The well is a graph of energy, not a picture of space itself.)

Investigation 8: Earth and Moon

What to look for: The Moon's well is a small dimple in the broad slope of the Earth's. To get there a spacecraft needs nearly the Earth's escape speed, aimed ahead of where the Moon is now, because the Moon moves on during the three-day journey. Between the two is a point where the pulls balance. Apollo used a path that looped round the back of the Moon and fell home again if the engine failed.

Investigation 9: Earth to Mars: a Hohmann transfer A level

What to look for: About 10%: theory says the speed must rise by a factor √(2r₂/(r₁+r₂)) = 1.099. The spacecraft then coasts along half an ellipse, taking about 8.5 months, with no further fuel. It only meets Mars if Mars is in the right place when it arrives, which is why launches wait for a 'window' every 26 months. A second burn on arrival is needed to match Mars's speed. This two-burn route uses the least fuel.

Investigation 10: Two suns

What to look for: Far from the pair, the planet feels them almost as a single mass and its orbit is stable. Close in, the pull changes from moment to moment as the stars swing round, and the planet is soon thrown out or swallowed: there is no stable orbit there, and tiny changes in the launch give completely different results (chaos). Real planets around double stars, such as Kepler-16b, all orbit well outside the pair.
