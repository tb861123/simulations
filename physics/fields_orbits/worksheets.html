Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Does E fall as 1/r²?

What this shows. Each point is a student holding a field probe at distance r (to ±1 cm) from a 1 nC charge; the meter has a 3% random error. On log–log axes E = kQ/r² is a straight line of gradient −2: double the distance, a quarter of the field. The measured gradient comes out close to −2. Close in, the ±1 cm position error matters more (a 1 cm error at 0.5 m is a 4% error in E), so the scatter is bigger there.

2 · Kepler's third law: T² ∝ r³?

What this shows. Each point is a planet launched at the circular speed and timed once round by the simulation (RK4). On log–log axes the gradient is 1.5: T ∝ r^1.5, which is the same as T² ∝ r³. At 1 AU the period is 1 year; at 30 AU (Neptune) about 164 years. The simulated periods sit on the theory line to better than 0.01%, because the only error is the tiny step error of RK4.

3 · Does Kepler's 3rd law hold for ellipses?

What this shows. Each run launches a planet at 1 AU with a random speed between 0.6 and 1.3 times the circular speed, so most orbits are ellipses of very different shapes. The simulation measures the semi-major axis a = (closest + furthest)/2 and the period T, in AU and years. a³/T² comes out as 1.000 for every orbit, the Sun's mass in solar masses: Kepler's third law uses the semi-major axis, whatever the eccentricity. The tiny spread is only the simulation's step error.

4 · Is energy conserved over orbits?

What this shows. In real gravity the total energy (KE + PE) of an orbit never changes. A computer has to step forward in time, and each step makes a small error, so the total energy drifts. RK4 is a 4th-order method: over a fixed time its errors shrink at least as fast as (time step)⁴, a gradient of −4 or steeper on log–log axes. For the energy of this orbit the gradient comes out steeper still, about −5: halve the time step and the drift falls about 30 times. With 25 steps an orbit the energy is out by about 1.5%; with 400 it is out by about one part in 60 million. The drift is a fault of the method, not of the physics. (The three runs at each step have slightly different launch speeds, between 0.79 and 0.81 of circular speed.)

5 · Where does escape speed start?

What this shows. How far out does the planet get? For a closed orbit the furthest point is r₀ × v²/(2v_c² − v²), which shoots up as the speed nears √2 × v_c = 1.414 v_c, the escape speed √(2GM/r). Beyond that the planet never comes back: the simulation stops it at 1000 r₀ (the flat top of the graph), and there is no theory line because the furthest point is at infinity.

6 · Weigh Sirius A and B

What this shows. Each run is one astronomer measuring the separation d (2% error) and the period T (1% error) of Sirius A and B, then using M₁ + M₂ = 4π²d³/(GT²). The results scatter round the true 3.0 M☉ with an SD of about 6%: because d is cubed, its 2% error becomes 6%, while the 1% error in T only becomes 2%. That is why measuring the distance well matters most when weighing stars.
