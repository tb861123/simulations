Special Relativity · Teacher notesspecial-relativity.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Moving clocks run slow

What to look for: Seen from the platform, the train's flash has to travel along a longer, zig-zag path. Light cannot go any faster to make up for it, so each tick takes longer: by the factor γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²) = 1.25 at 0.6c. Nothing is wrong with the clock: time itself runs slow.

Investigation 2: Who is really slow?

What to look for: From the train, it is the platform clock that zig-zags and runs slow. Each observer measures the other's clock as slow, and both are right: there is no experiment that says who is 'really' moving. The situation is symmetrical until someone turns round (see the twins).

Investigation 3: At the same time?

What to look for: From the platform the rear of the train rushes to meet the light while the front runs away from it, so the rear lights up first. Events that are simultaneous for one observer are not for another. On the diagram, 'now' is a horizontal line: boosting the frame tilts the events so that different ones lie on the same line.

Investigation 4: The train that should not fit

What to look for: From the train the doors do not shut at the same time: the exit door shuts and reopens first, well before the entrance door shuts behind the tail. The train is never inside with both doors closed. Both observers agree about every event (no door ever hits the train); they disagree only about which events are simultaneous. Below about 0.72c it genuinely does not fit.

Investigation 5: The twins

What to look for: Earth twin: 10 years. Ship twin: 6 years. It is not symmetrical because the ship changes frame at the turn-round: its worldline has a kink, and the bent path through spacetime is the shorter one in proper time. The flashes prove it: each twin literally counts the other's birthdays (slowly while separating, rapidly while approaching).

Investigation 6: Muons should not reach us

What to look for: Without relativity almost none arrive; with it, a good fraction do, as detectors confirm every day. From the ground: the muons' clocks run about 10 times slow, so they live long enough. From the muon: its lifetime is normal, but the atmosphere is contracted to about 1.5 km and the ground rushes up in time. Two explanations, one result.

Investigation 7: Is 300 km/h relativistic?

What to look for: At 0.05c, γ = 1.001: a tenth of a percent. For a 300 km/h train γ differs from 1 by about 4 parts in 10¹⁴. Relativity does not contradict everyday experience: it agrees with Newton whenever speeds are small. But GPS satellites must correct for it, or positions would drift by kilometres a day.

Batch experiment 1: Muons reaching the ground

Radioactive decay is random: each muon's lifetime is a matter of chance, so the number that survive out of 500 varies from run to run. Notice how sharply survival rises as v approaches c: the speed barely changes, but γ grows without limit.
