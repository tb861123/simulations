Lightning · Teacher noteslightning.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Where will it strike?

What to look for: No. Each step is a weighted gamble, so every bolt is different and the strike points scatter around the point below the start. The path is not planned: the leader only 'feels' the field at its own tip.

Investigation 2: Why does it branch?

What to look for: At η = 1 the bolt is a bushy fractal, at η = 5 nearly a straight spark. Real lightning sits in between. Branching happens because many cells near the tip have almost the same field, so chance decides, and sometimes two win.

Investigation 3: The field at a point

What to look for: Voltage lines have to squeeze round the tip of anything tall and thin, so the field there is many times stronger than over flat ground (see the right-hand chart). The leader grows fastest towards strong field, so it is drawn to the rod.

Investigation 4: Does the rod protect the houses?

What to look for: The rod takes most strikes near it, and the house beside it is rarely hit. The house further away gets little protection. A rule of thumb used by engineers is that a rod protects a cone about as wide as the rod is tall.

Investigation 5: Sheltering under a tree

What to look for: The tree is struck far more often than open ground. This model stops when the bolt arrives; in reality the current then jumps sideways from the trunk to anyone nearby (a 'side flash'), which is how most people sheltering under trees are hurt. In the open you are a small target, but the safest place is indoors or in a car.

Investigation 6: The tallest building

What to look for: The tallest takes the most, but not all: a leader that starts far to one side reaches a shorter, nearer building first. Height matters most, then how close the leader happens to start, then how pointed the top is.

Investigation 7: Blunt or pointed?

What to look for: The field at the thin tower's top is stronger than anywhere on the flat roof, though the wide roof is a bigger target. Sharper means stronger field: the same reason charge leaks from points, and why a rod has a pointed tip.

Batch experiment 1: How far off target?

The spread is the lesson: the same cloud, the same start, and strikes land tens of metres apart. The mean is close to zero because left and right are equally likely.
Theory (directly below): 0
Result from a test run: mean -16.1, SD 70.2, SE 5.73 from 150 runs

Batch experiment 2: How tall must a rod be?

Each run is a hit or a miss, so single runs sit at 0 or 1; the mean is the fraction caught. Taller rods catch more, with no sharp cut-off: protection is a matter of odds, not a guarantee.
Result from a test run: 0: 0 ± 0 · 18: 0.233 ± 0.0785 · 36: 0.4 ± 0.091 · 54: 0.6 ± 0.091 · 72: 0.833 ± 0.0692 · 96: 0.867 ± 0.0631 · 120: 1 ± 0 · 150: 1 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 30 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: How wide is the protected zone?

Leaders starting within about one rod-height are usually caught; beyond two rod-heights the rod barely helps. This is the idea behind the 'cone of protection' used to place lightning conductors.
Result from a test run: 0: 0.867 ± 0.0631 · 24: 0.8 ± 0.0743 · 48: 0.867 ± 0.0631 · 72: 0.667 ± 0.0875 · 96: 0.733 ± 0.0821 · 120: 0.633 ± 0.0895 · 156: 0.2 ± 0.0743 · 192: 0.0333 ± 0.0333 (mean ± SE, 30 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Branching and η

At η = 1 the bolt fills space with side branches (a fractal of dimension about 1.7), so it grows many times more cells than it needs. As η rises it wastes less and approaches a straight line. Real lightning corresponds to a moderate η.
Theory (a perfectly straight bolt): 1: 1 · 1.5: 1 · 2: 1 · 3: 1 · 4: 1 · 6: 1
Result from a test run: 1: 5.17 ± 0.271 · 1.5: 3.39 ± 0.281 · 2: 2.34 ± 0.13 · 3: 1.78 ± 0.0919 · 4: 1.37 ± 0.0378 · 6: 1.19 ± 0.0214 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)
