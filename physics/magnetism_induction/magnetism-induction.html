How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Uniform fields. The field B is the same everywhere in the region drawn and stops sharply at the edge. Real magnets have fields that spread out and weaken at the edges.
  • Force on a wire: F = BIL sin θ for the length of wire inside the field. The wire's own field and the force on the leads outside the field are ignored.
  • Charged particles move at constant speed in a circle (the magnetic force does no work). No relativity: at 3×10⁷ m/s (10% of c) an electron's mass is already 0.5% bigger. The circle is shown in slow motion: real periods are nanoseconds.
  • Induction: the field inside the coil is modelled as Bmax e−(x/1 m)² where x is how far the magnet is from the coil, as in the old app (so the flux pulse is 1 m wide whatever the speed). The coil's own induced current does not slow the magnet down (no braking force is included).
  • Transformer: an ideal transformer unless you set an efficiency (Advanced). All the flux from the primary links the secondary. Voltages are peak values of a 50 Hz sine wave; the animation is in slow motion (1 cycle every 2 s at 1×).
  • DC motor: the coil is a single rectangle of N turns in a uniform field; torque τ = BINA cos θ with θ the angle between the coil's plane and B. The spinning animation uses a simple model (torque minus a friction torque proportional to speed); its speed is not to scale. Back-EMF is ε = BNAω cos θ.
  • Fixes to the old app: the old motor page mixed up its angles (it said 0° gave maximum torque but calculated |sin θ|), and its back-EMF formula included the current I. Here both use the angle between the coil plane and B consistently, and ε = BNAω cos θ (no I).
  • Batch runs model class experiments: a top-pan balance (with a resolution and random error) for the force on a wire, a data logger with noise for induced EMFs, AC voltmeters with a % error, and hand-pushed magnets whose speed varies from run to run.
