📐 Physics notes

Spec reference: WJEC Eduqas A level Physics, Component 1, Topic 6 (Vibrations), sections (a)–(q).

Key equations:

x(t) = x₀ cos(ω₀t)
ω₀ = √(k/m) (spring)
ω₀ = √(g/L) (pendulum)
T = 2π/ω₀
v = −x₀ω₀ sin(ω₀t)
a = −ω₀²x

What this shows: Simple harmonic motion happens when the restoring force is proportional to, and directed opposite to, the displacement from equilibrium: F = −kx. The displacement, velocity and acceleration are all sinusoidal and 90° (or 180°) out of phase with each other. Energy is continuously exchanged between kinetic energy (½mv²) and potential energy (½kx² for a spring), with total mechanical energy conserved in the ideal (undamped) case. The phase portrait (v against x) is a perfect ellipse for ideal SHM.

Advanced context. The damping ratio ζ controls the energy lost per cycle. There are three regimes:

  • Underdamped (ζ < 1): the oscillation dies away exponentially, inside the envelope ±x₀e−ζω₀t.
  • Critically damped (ζ = 1): the fastest return to equilibrium without oscillating: ideal for instrument pointers and car shock absorbers.
  • Overdamped (ζ > 1): a slow exponential return, with no oscillation.

Resonance happens when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency (ω = ω₀). The steady-state amplitude is A = x₀ / √[(1 − (ω/ω₀)²)² + (2ζω/ω₀)²]. The quality factor Q = 1/(2ζ) measures how sharp the resonance is.

Real examples: the Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940) failed from wind-driven oscillation at the bridge's natural frequency. MRI scanners use tuned resonance of hydrogen nuclei in a magnetic field. Car suspension is designed close to critical damping for comfort and handling.

Musical instruments: a guitar string is a forced oscillating system. The fret position changes L, which changes f. A tighter string has more tension (a bigger "k"), so a higher f; a heavier string has more mass, so a lower f. The Q factor of a string decides its sustain: classical guitar strings have Q ≈ 1000, so the note rings for a long time.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Equation of motion: the computer solves q'' = −ω₀²q − 2ζω₀q' + ω₀²q₀cos(ωt), where q is the angle θ (pendulum) or the displacement x (spring). The first term is the restoring force, the second is damping, the third is the driving force (only when forced oscillation is on). Standard level uses only the first term: ideal SHM.
  • Natural angular frequency: ω₀ = √(g/L) for the pendulum, ω₀ = √(k/m) for the spring. So T = 2π√(L/g) and T = 2π√(m/k).
  • Solving: 4th-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) with 1000 steps per period, so it is accurate to better than one part in a million per swing.
  • Small-angle approximation: the ideal pendulum uses sin θ ≈ θ. With "Large-angle comparison" on, it solves the exact equation θ'' = −(g/L) sin θ instead, and the graphs show the small-angle answer as a dashed line. Big swings take longer: the exact period is T₀ ÷ AGM(1, cos(θ₀/2)) (an arithmetic–geometric mean).
  • Pendulum displacement is measured along the arc: x = Lθ (θ in radians), so speeds and accelerations are in m/s and m/s².
  • Energy: KE = ½mv². PE = ½kx² (spring) or ½mω₀²x² (pendulum, small angles; mgL(1 − cos θ) for the exact equation).
  • Damping is a force proportional to speed (like a shock absorber or slow movement through air). Real air resistance grows with speed², and friction at a pivot is roughly constant, so real swings die away a little differently.
  • Forcing: a sinusoidal driving force whose strength would hold the mass at x₀ if it were not moving. For the spring this is the same as the wall moving back and forth by x₀ (drawn on the picture). The response curve is the steady state, after the start-up wobble has died away.
  • Left out: the mass of the string or spring, the size of the bob, stretching of the string, the spring going beyond its limit of proportionality, and friction with the surface under the spring.
  • Batch runs use the same equations with no drawing, 800 steps per period. Timing experiments add a random human reaction time (normal distribution) at the start and the end of each stopwatch timing.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • The pendulum: two point masses on light, rigid rods, joined by frictionless hinges, swinging in a flat vertical plane. Angles θ₁ and θ₂ are measured from straight down.
  • Equations of motion (from Lagrange's method, with Δ = θ₁ − θ₂ and D = 2m₁ + m₂ − m₂cos2Δ):
    θ₁'' = [−g(2m₁+m₂)sinθ₁ − m₂g sin(θ₁−2θ₂) − 2 sinΔ · m₂(ω₂²L₂ + ω₁²L₁cosΔ)] ÷ (L₁D)
    θ₂'' = [2 sinΔ (ω₁²L₁(m₁+m₂) + g(m₁+m₂)cosθ₁ + ω₂²L₂m₂cosΔ)] ÷ (L₂D)
    Single pendulum: θ'' = −(g/L)sinθ.
  • Solving them: 4th-order Runge–Kutta with a fixed 1 ms step. Its error per step is tiny (about (step)⁵), so the energy drifts by less than a millionth of a per cent per minute. That is far smaller than the 0.001° differences we study, so the chaos is real, not a computer error.
  • Energy: KE = ½(m₁+m₂)L₁²ω₁² + ½m₂L₂²ω₂² + m₂L₁L₂ω₁ω₂cosΔ; PE = −(m₁+m₂)gL₁cosθ₁ − m₂gL₂cosθ₂. The drift is shown as a percentage of (m₁+m₂)gL₁ + m₂gL₂.
  • Separation is the distance between two pendulums in "phase space": √(Δθ₁² + Δθ₂² + τ²(Δω₁² + Δω₂²)) in radians, where τ = √(L₁/g) turns angular speeds into angles. For chaotic swings it grows like δeλt until the pendulums are completely different (about 1 radian), then stops growing.
  • Lyapunov exponent λ is found two ways: the gradient of ln(separation) against time on the graph, and a long-run average (the Benettin method: a shadow pendulum 10⁻⁸ rad away is pulled back every 0.05 s and the stretching is averaged).
  • Prediction horizon: the time until either angle differs by more than your chosen amount. Theory: about ln(X/δ) ÷ λ.
  • Small swings are not chaotic. A single pendulum has period 2π√(L/g) for small angles; bigger swings take longer (exact: 4√(L/g)·K(sin(θ₀/2)), where K is an elliptic integral). A double pendulum with small swings is a mix of two "normal modes" with fixed periods.
  • Damping is a simple drag on each angle, θ'' gets an extra −bω. Real air resistance depends on speed squared, and real hinges have friction.
  • Left out: rods with mass (real pendulums have heavy arms, which changes the numbers but not the chaos), the bobs' size, bending and stretching, air currents and the pendulums hitting each other. A real double pendulum can be predicted for only a few seconds, partly because you can never set it up more accurately than about 0.1°.
