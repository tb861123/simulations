Simple Harmonic Motion · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Why time 10 swings, not 1?

What this shows. A 1 m pendulum (T = 2.006 s) timed by hand. Each press of the stopwatch is late or early by a random reaction time (SD 0.15 s), at the start and at the end. That error is shared out over all the swings you time, so the spread of results (the error bars) shrinks like 1/n: from about √2 × 0.15 = 0.21 s for one swing to 0.02 s for ten. The mean stays on the theory line every time: timing more swings makes the result more precise, not more accurate.

2 · How much do stopwatch timings scatter?

What this shows. 200 students each time 10 swings of the same 1 m pendulum and divide by 10. The histogram is a bell shape (a normal distribution) centred on the true period 2π√(L/g) = 2.006 s, with SD close to the predicted √2 × 0.15 ÷ 10 = 0.021 s. The mean of all 200 is much closer than any single timing: its standard error is SD/√200 ≈ 0.0015 s. Results scatter around theory because each timing has its own random reaction errors.

3 · Is T = 2π√(L/g)? (timed by hand)

What this shows. Pendulums from 20 cm to 4 m, each timed over 10 swings by 5 students. On log–log axes T ∝ √L is a straight line with gradient 0.5, and the fitted gradient comes out very close to 0.5. A student would plot T² against L instead: a straight line through the origin with gradient 4π²/g, which is how g is measured in the lab.

4 · Where does the small-angle approximation fail?

What this shows. The exact equation θ'' = −(g/L) sin θ, timed perfectly (no reaction time). 2π√(L/g) (grey dashed) assumes sin θ ≈ θ. The measured period agrees with the exact theory (yellow) at every angle, but rises above the small-angle value: about 0.2% long at 10°, 0.8% at 20°, 1.7% at 30°, 3% at 40°, 7% at 60° and 14% at 80°. So SHM is a very good model for swings below about 15°.

5 · Resonance: how big do driven oscillations get?

What this shows. A 1 kg mass on a 20 N/m spring, damping ratio ζ = 0.1, driven at different frequencies. Each run waits for the start-up wobble to die away, then measures the steady amplitude. The points sit on the theory curve: a sharp peak of 1/(2ζ) = 5 times x₀ near ω = ω₀ (resonance), about 1× at low frequency, and almost nothing when driven much faster than ω₀ (the mass can't keep up).

6 · How quickly does damping shrink the swings?

What this shows. Release the mass, then measure how far it gets on the same side one swing later, as a fraction of the start. With no damping it comes all the way back (1.00). Each swing loses the same fraction, e^(−2πζ/√(1−ζ²)): about 53% is left at ζ = 0.1 and only 2% at ζ = 0.5. The model matches the formula to 4 decimal places, because it is solving exactly the equation the formula comes from.

7 · Is T = 2π√(m/k)? (spring timed by hand)

What this shows. The spring practical: masses from 0.1 kg to 3.2 kg (doubling each time) on a 20 N/m spring, each timed over 10 oscillations by 10 students with a stopwatch (reaction-time SD 0.1 s at each press). On log–log axes T ∝ √m is a straight line with gradient 0.5. A student would plot T² against m: a straight line through the origin with gradient 4π²/k, which gives the spring constant. Every mean sits about 0.004 s below the theory line: the same ten sets of random reaction times are used at every mass, so their small shared bias (about 1.4 standard errors) shows up at every step. The spread (SD ≈ 0.01 s, against √2 × 0.1 ÷ 10 = 0.014 s predicted) matters most for the lightest mass, whose period (0.44 s) is shortest.

9 · Does a 1000× smaller error give 1000× longer?

What this shows. No! Each 10× smaller starting error adds only the same fixed amount of time, ln(10)/λ, so the points fall on a straight line against log δ. Making the measurement a billion times better only buys a few times longer. The same random starts are used at every step, so the scatter at each step comes from how chaotic each start is: less chaotic starts stay predictable for longer. (Runs are stopped at 60 s.)

10 · When does chaos switch on?

What this shows. For small starting angles λ is close to 0 (the gap grows only slowly, so a 40 s run gives a small positive number rather than exactly 0). Somewhere between about 70° and 90° the pendulum gains enough energy for the lower arm to flip over and λ jumps up: chaos switches on. Near the boundary some starts are chaotic and some are not, so the scatter is large.

13 · Can we trust the computer?

What this shows. With no damping, energy should stay exactly constant. After 60 s of wild chaotic motion (60,000 RK4 steps) the drift is around a millionth of a per cent or less. The chaos is not caused by rounding errors in the computer: the starting difference of 0.001° is millions of times bigger than the numerical error.
