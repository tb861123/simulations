Simple Harmonic Motion · Teacher notessimple-harmonic-motion.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Does the size of the swing matter?

What to look for: Hardly at all: about 2.007 s at 5° and 2.02 s at 20°. A bigger swing travels further but moves faster, and the two almost cancel: the period is (nearly) independent of amplitude. Only at large angles (try 60°) does it grow noticeably, because sin θ is no longer close to θ.

Investigation 2: Length and period

What to look for: T = 2π√(L/g): 4 times the length doubles the period. On the Moon g is about a sixth, so the period is about 2.5 times longer. A pendulum clock taken to the Moon would run very slow.

Investigation 3: A mass on a spring

What to look for: T = 2π√(m/k): doubling the mass multiplies T by √2 (1.41); doubling k divides it by √2. A heavier mass is harder to accelerate; a stiffer spring pulls harder.

Investigation 4: Where is it fastest?

What to look for: Speed is greatest at the middle (displacement zero), where all the energy is kinetic. Acceleration is greatest at the ends, where the restoring force is biggest and the speed is zero; there all the energy is potential. Velocity is a quarter of a cycle ahead of displacement; acceleration is exactly opposite to displacement: a = −ω²x.

Investigation 5: Damping A level

What to look for: With light damping the amplitude falls by the same fraction each cycle (exponential decay), and the period is almost unchanged. With heavy damping the system returns slowly without oscillating; critical damping (car suspension) returns fastest without overshooting.

Investigation 6: Resonance A level

What to look for: Resonance: the amplitude is greatest when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency (here about 1 Hz). More damping makes the peak lower and broader and shifts it slightly lower in frequency. Bridges, buildings and car parts are designed so that resonance does not happen in use.

Investigation 7: Barton's pendulums A level

What to look for: The pendulum with the same length as the driver resonates: largest amplitude, and it lags the driver by a quarter of a cycle. Shorter ones (higher natural frequency) swing in phase with the driver; longer ones swing half a cycle out of phase.

Investigation 8: Chaos A level

What to look for: For a few seconds they move together; then the tiny difference grows exponentially and the motion becomes completely different. This is chaos: perfectly deterministic rules, but unpredictable in practice, because no measurement is perfect. Weather forecasts face the same limit.

Batch experiment 1: Period against length

A curve: T grows as √L. Plotting T² against L gives a straight line through the origin with gradient 4π² ÷ g, the usual way to measure g.
Theory (2π√(L/g)): 0.1: 0.634 · 0.25: 1 · 0.5: 1.42 · 0.75: 1.74 · 1: 2.01 · 1.25: 2.24 · 1.5: 2.46 · 2: 2.84
Result from a test run: 0.1: 0.635 ± 0.00267 · 0.25: 1 ± 0.00372 · 0.5: 1.43 ± 0.00823 · 0.75: 1.75 ± 0.0066 · 1: 2 ± 0.011 · 1.25: 2.24 ± 0.00865 · 1.5: 2.46 ± 0.00657 · 2: 2.84 ± 0.00981 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Timing one swing or ten?

Timing a single 2-second swing with a 0.15 s error gives g anywhere from about 8.5 to 11.5. Timing 10 swings divides the timing error by 10: try the next experiment.
Theory (true g): 9.81
Result from a test run: mean 9.96, SD 1.52, SE 0.108 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 3: Timing ten swings

The spread is about ten times smaller. Timing many oscillations is the best way to reduce the effect of reaction time.
Theory (true g): 9.81
Result from a test run: mean 9.81, SD 0.144, SE 0.0102 from 200 runs
