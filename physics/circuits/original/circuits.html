⚗️ Lissajous figure (oscilloscope)

The tilt of the Lissajous ellipse encodes the phase angle φ. Pure R → straight diagonal line (φ = 0). Pure L or C → an upright ellipse with no tilt (φ = ±90°). The area enclosed by the ellipse is proportional to the reactive (stored) power.

How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Ideal wires and meters. Connecting wires have no resistance. The voltmeter takes no current and the ammeter has no resistance.
  • The cell is an ideal EMF ε in series with a fixed internal resistance r. In a real cell r rises as it runs down and changes with temperature, and the EMF falls slowly as it is used.
  • Moving charges. The dots show the direction of the current and how fast it flows (faster dots = bigger current), not the real drift speed: electrons in a copper wire drift at well under a millimetre per second. Tick "Show electrons" to see the negative electrons, which move the opposite way to conventional current.
  • Filament lamp is a made-up power law I ∝ |V|^(1/1.8) that gives the right S-shape. A real lamp's resistance depends on its filament temperature, which takes a fraction of a second to change.
  • Diode follows the Shockley equation with VT = 25 mV and ideality factor n = 1.8. The current is capped at 100 mA (a real diode would need a series resistor or it would burn out). Reverse breakdown is not modelled.
  • Series, parallel and sensors tab: an ideal cell (no internal resistance) and ideal meters. The LDR follows R = 10 kΩ × (light/10 lux)−0.7, an illustrative law with the shape of a typical photocell; the thermistor is a typical 10 kΩ NTC part, R = 10 kΩ × e3950 K (1/T − 1/298 K), with T in kelvin. Neither heats itself up here.
  • Thermistor (I–V tab) uses I = V(1 + 0.6|V|)/R₀: the resistance falls as the power heats it. It ignores how long it takes to warm up and the temperature of the room.
  • Resistivity values are for about 20 °C; they change with temperature (up for metals, down for semiconductors). The wire is assumed uniform.
  • AC circuits show the steady state only: the switch-on transient has died away. Components are ideal (an inductor with no resistance, a capacitor with no leakage).
  • AC animation is in slow motion. One cycle takes 2 s at 1×, whatever the frequency; the clock shows the real time that has passed. At 50 Hz that is 100 times slower than reality.
  • Batch runs use exact formulas plus random meter errors (a percentage of the reading, SD set in the table) and, where set, the meter's resolution (rounding to the last digit). They model a class doing the experiment, not the animation.
