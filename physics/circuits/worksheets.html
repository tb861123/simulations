Electric Circuits · Teacher notescircuits.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: One loop: series

What to look for: The current is the same everywhere in a series circuit: charge cannot pile up anywhere, so the same amount passes every point each second. The voltages across the components add up to the 6 V of the cell: each coulomb gives up all the energy it was given. The bigger resistance takes the bigger share of the voltage.

Investigation 2: Two paths: parallel

What to look for: Each branch gets the full voltage of the cell. The currents in the branches add up to the current from the cell (charge splits at a junction and rejoins). Adding another branch draws more current from the cell, so the total resistance of the circuit falls. This is how the circuits in a house are wired: every appliance gets the full mains voltage and can be switched on its own.

Investigation 3: Is the lamp an ordinary resistor?

What to look for: Resistor: a straight line through the origin: current is proportional to voltage (Ohm's law), so its resistance is constant. Lamp: the line curves over: as the current rises the filament gets hotter, its atoms vibrate more and get in the way of the charges, so its resistance rises. Diode: almost no current backwards, and none forwards until about 0.6 V, then the current rises steeply. A diode lets current flow one way only.

Investigation 4: A street light: the LDR

What to look for: In bright light the LDR's resistance falls (from about 10 kΩ in the dark to a few hundred ohms), so more current flows and the LDR takes a smaller share of the voltage. The voltage across it is high in the dark and low in the light: that change can switch a street light on at dusk. This arrangement is a potential divider.

Investigation 5: A fire alarm: the thermistor

What to look for: This NTC thermistor's resistance falls as it gets hotter, from several kilohms when cold to under 200 Ω at 100 °C. So the current rises and the voltage across the fixed resistor rises: when it passes a set value it can trigger an alarm or switch off a heater. Thermistors are used in thermostats, car engines and digital thermometers.

Investigation 6: Why does a battery go flat?

What to look for: The terminal voltage falls as the current rises: V = ε − Ir. Some of each coulomb's energy is used up inside the cell itself, warming it. A cell that is being run hard gets warm and seems weaker. A car battery has a tiny internal resistance, so it can supply the hundreds of amps a starter motor needs.

Investigation 7: Energy and power

What to look for: Power = current × voltage. Energy = power × time, so in 60 s the lamp transfers P × 60 joules. Also, each coulomb passing through the lamp transfers energy equal to the voltage: E = QV. The cell's power is shared between the components.

Investigation 8: Measuring EMF and internal resistance A level

What to look for: A straight line: V = ε − Ir. The intercept on the V axis is the EMF ε (the terminal voltage when no current flows), and the gradient is −r. This is the standard A level practical. The batch run does it with realistic meter errors.

Investigation 9: The potential divider A level

What to look for: V_out = V_in × R₂ ÷ (R₁ + R₂) = 6 × 60 ÷ 80 = 4.5 V. Kirchhoff's second law: the e.m.f. equals the sum of the voltages round the loop. To get 3 V across the LDR, R₁ must equal the LDR's resistance at 200 lux, so the voltage is shared equally.
