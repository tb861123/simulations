Teacher notes

Investigation 1: A heater on the floor

What to look for: Air touching the heater warms, expands and becomes less dense, so it rises in a plume to the ceiling, spreads along it, cools, and sinks down the far wall. That loop is a convection current. A hot ceiling layer builds up over a cooler floor layer.

Investigation 2: A heater on the ceiling

What to look for: No. Warm air is less dense so it stays at the top: nothing makes it sink. The ceiling gets hot and the floor stays cold. This is why radiators are low down, and why a heater on the ceiling feels cold at your feet.

Investigation 3: An air conditioner on the ceiling

What to look for: Cold air is denser, so it sinks in a stream and spreads across the floor, pushing the warmer air up. The whole room mixes and cools. A cooler at the ceiling works with convection; put it on the floor and the cold air just sits there.

Investigation 4: Air conditioner on the floor

What to look for: The cold air pools at the bottom and does not move: there is no convection current. Only the air by the floor cools. Cold sinks, so cooling belongs at the top and heating at the bottom.

Investigation 5: Heater and cooler together

What to look for: They work together: warm air rises on the left, cold air sinks on the right, so one big loop forms and the air moves faster than with either alone. Good design for air conditioning of a room.

Investigation 6: A cold window

What to look for: Air touching the cold glass cools, becomes denser and sinks, so a cold downdraught flows down the window and spreads across the floor: that is why you feel cold feet near a window even when it is shut.

Investigation 7: Open the window

What to look for: Cold, dense air enters low down and sinks across the floor; warm, light air leaves high up. A single window makes a two-way convection exchange. You can click the window to open and close it.

Investigation 8: A hot room

What to look for: The cold air sinks from the unit and pushes the warm air up, so the whole room cools by convection. The colours are relative to the starting temperature.

Investigation 9: Conduction alone

What to look for: Air is a very poor conductor, so with no convection the heat hardly leaves the heater: after ten minutes it has spread a few centimetres. Convection is far faster than conduction in a fluid.

Investigation 10: Which rod conducts best?

What to look for: Copper first (about 26 s for the 5 cm pin), then aluminium, then steel (several minutes). Glass, wood and polystyrene never melt the wax in 20 min. Metals conduct best because their free electrons carry energy along as well as the vibrating atoms.

Investigation 11: Lag the rod

What to look for: Without lagging, energy leaks from the sides so the rod is cooler away from the heat. With lagging, the temperature falls in a straight line from the hot end to the cold end (all the energy goes down the rod).

Investigation 12: Fourier's law A level

What to look for: P is proportional to ΔT. For copper (k = 400), A = 7.85 × 10⁻⁵ m², L = 0.25 m and ΔT = 80 K, P = 400 × 7.85 × 10⁻⁵ × 80 ÷ 0.25 ≈ 10 W.

Batch experiment 1: How precise is one pin-drop time?

The spread comes from the wax's melting point and the stopwatch. Time for one rod: report the mean of several, and the standard error says how well you know it.
Theory (model at 60 °C): 118
Result from a test run: mean 124, SD 28.6, SE 1.66 from 297 runs

Batch experiment 2: Which material conducts best?

Copper first, then aluminium, then steel (much later). For glass, wood and polystyrene the wax never melts within the 20 minutes of the test, so they are plotted at the 1200 s limit: poor conductors (insulators). The real time for them would be far longer still.
Theory (model): 0: 26 · 1: 31 · 2: 283 · 3: 1200 · 4: 1200 · 5: 1200
Result from a test run: 0: 26.5 ± 0.847 · 1: 31.7 ± 0.685 · 2: 283 ± 0.459 · 3: 1200 ± 0 · 4: 1200 ± 0 · 5: 1200 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Fourier's law: heat flow against temperature difference

A straight line through the origin: double ΔT, double the power. The slope is kA/L ≈ 0.126 W/K for this rod.

Batch experiment 4: Heater height: does the room warm?

A heater on the floor warms the lower half most; above head height it does very little, and at the ceiling almost nothing. Warm air rises, so a floor heater stirs the whole room, while a high heater only warms the air above it and leaves the cold air at the bottom undisturbed. (A heater at 1.2 m sits inside the lower half, so it warms that band directly.) The model grid is coarse (32 × 20) so numbers are indicative.

Result from a test run: 0.15: 11.7 ± 0.0436 · 0.6: 7.32 ± 0.0275 · 1.2: 9.41 ± 0.0382 · 1.8: 4.3 ± 0.0697 · 2.35: 0.406 ± 0.0659 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Air conditioner height: does it cool?

A cooler near the ceiling lets cold air sink through the whole room, so the whole room cools. Near the floor the cold air collects at the bottom and the rest of the room is barely cooled.
Theory (noise-free run): 0.1: 1.01 · 0.6: 3.84 · 1.2: 7.22 · 1.8: 10.2 · 2.35: 9.27
Result from a test run: 0.1: 0.951 ± 0.0864 · 0.6: 3.82 ± 0.0176 · 1.2: 7.19 ± 0.0137 · 1.8: 10.2 ± 0.0134 · 2.35: 9.26 ± 0.0691 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: Convection or conduction only?

With convection the warm air circulates to the far side of the room and the temperature rises. With conduction alone nothing reaches it (air is a very poor conductor): convection is the key way heat moves in air.
Theory (noise-free run): 0: 0 · 1: 13.6
Result from a test run: 0: -0.0428 ± 0.0212 · 1: 13.6 ± 0.0568 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 7: Open the window: how fast does the room cool?

Shut, the room loses energy slowly through the walls and the cold glass. Open, cold outside air pours in low down and warm air leaves at the top, so the room cools several times faster and is close to the outside temperature within 8 minutes: a window lets convection carry the energy out.
Theory (noise-free run): 0: 6.86 · 1: 19.8
Result from a test run: 0: 6.82 ± 0.0195 · 1: 19.9 ± 0.0582 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)
