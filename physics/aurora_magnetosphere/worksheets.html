Teacher notes

Investigation 1: North or south?

What to look for: Northward: Earth's field lines stay closed (blue) and the particles drift round or are trapped; almost nothing reaches the poles. Southward: the fields cancel at a ring of null points and join up, so orange 'open' field lines appear, joining the polar regions to the solar wind; particles slide down them and the oval lights up. Aurora watchers check Bz first.

Investigation 2: A storm pushes the aurora south

What to look for: Stronger southward Bz opens more of Earth's field, so the polar cap and the auroral oval grow towards the equator: from about 76° to 69° magnetic latitude in this model. In real storms the oval reaches 50–55° and the aurora is seen across the UK (Britain is at about 52–58° magnetic latitude). The real oval sits a few degrees nearer the equator than this simple model's boundary.

Investigation 3: Kp: the aurora forecast

What to look for: Southward Bz and speed matter most (Kp rises with v^(4/3)|Bz|^(2/3) for southward fields); density matters much less. Kp 5 or more is a geomagnetic storm; northern Scotland often sees aurora at Kp 5, southern England needs about Kp 7–8. Kp here comes from a fitted formula (Newell 2008), not from the model.

Investigation 4: Spiralling down field lines

What to look for: They follow field lines (the spiral shows they are gyrating round them; it is drawn much bigger than real gyration, which is metres to kilometres). Charged particles can slide along a field line easily but hardly across it, so they can only reach the atmosphere where field lines connect to the solar wind: near the magnetic poles. That is why aurora happen at high latitudes, and in both hemispheres at once.

Investigation 5: Why green and red?

What to look for: Incoming electrons excite oxygen atoms. At 100–200 km oxygen gives out green light (557.7 nm); higher up, where the air is thin, oxygen has time to give out red light (630 nm) before collisions take its energy. Nitrogen gives blue and purple at the bottom edge. The picture shows this layering in a simple way.

Batch experiment 1: Where is the open–closed boundary?

The traced boundary matches the formula to within about 0.2° (the step size of the tracing). Stronger southward fields open more flux, so the boundary, and the aurora just poleward of it, move towards the equator: about 77° at −1 nT, 69° at −20 nT. Every longitude gives the same answer because this simple field is the same all round the axis.
Theory (cos²λ = 1.5(|Bz|/B_E)^⅓): -1: 77.4 · -2: 75.8 · -5: 73.4 · -10: 71.3 · -15: 69.9 · -20: 68.9 · -30: 67.3 · -40: 66.2
Result from a test run: -1: 77.2 ± 0 · -2: 75.6 ± 0 · -5: 73.2 ± 0 · -10: 71.1 ± 0 · -15: 69.7 ± 0 · -20: 68.7 ± 0 · -30: 67.1 ± 0 · -40: 65.9 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 2 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: How much gets in?

With northward Bz no particles get in: every field line near Earth is closed and shielded. With southward Bz a large share (about 25–45% here) slide down open field lines. Runs vary because the particles start at random places and with random speeds along the field.
Result from a test run: -20: 0.273 ± 0.0212 · -10: 0.32 ± 0.0171 · -5: 0.386 ± 0.0139 · -2: 0.316 ± 0.0128 · 2: 0 ± 0 · 5: 0 ± 0 · 10: 0 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Faster wind, more aurora?

In this model the fraction that gets in does not depend on speed (the drift and the sliding both scale with it), so the precipitation rate simply rises in proportion to the flux of particles arriving: a faster wind brings more. Real aurora also brighten because faster wind particles carry more energy each, and because reconnection runs faster.
Result from a test run: 300: 6.66 ± 0.533 · 400: 9.49 ± 0.429 · 500: 11.6 ± 0.418 · 600: 13.6 ± 0.288 · 700: 17.4 ± 0.168 · 800: 18.6 ± 0.697 · 900: 20.7 ± 1.26 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Where do they land?

Particles land on open field lines, so always poleward of the open–closed boundary (the theory line), a few degrees inside the polar cap. Both move towards the equator as southward Bz strengthens. In the real magnetosphere the brightest aurora are at the boundary itself, fed by particles from the magnetotail, which this model leaves out.
Theory (open–closed boundary): -20: 68.9 · -15: 69.9 · -10: 71.3 · -5: 73.4 · -2: 75.8
Result from a test run: -20: 76.3 ± 0.151 · -15: 79.3 ± 0.185 · -10: 79.3 ± 0.242 · -5: 79.9 ± 0.123 · -2: 80.5 ± 0.132 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)
