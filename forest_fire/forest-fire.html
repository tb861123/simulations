How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Grid: 240 × 160 cells, each 10 m × 10 m (2.4 km × 1.6 km). A cell is unburnt, burning, smouldering or burnt out.
  • Spreading: each burning cell can ignite its 8 neighbours. For each neighbour the model works out a rate of spread R (metres per minute) in that direction; in a short time step Δt the chance of ignition is 1 − e−RΔt/d, where d is the distance between cell centres. So on average the fire covers distance d in time d/R, with some randomness, like a real flickering fire front.
  • Base rate (no wind, flat, bone dry): grass 2 m/min, heather 1.5, bracken 1.0, leaf litter 0.5, scaled by how much undergrowth there is (30% of the rate with very little, 100% with lots). Trees add needle/leaf litter (1.0 m/min) and multiply the rate: pine ×1.3, eucalyptus ×1.5, birch ×0.8, broadleaf ×0.6.
  • Dryness: Rothermel's moisture-damping formula 1 − 2.59r + 5.11r² − 3.52r³ (r = moisture ÷ moisture of extinction, 25–30%), combined with the extra heat needed to boil off the water (250 + 1116 × moisture). Above the extinction moisture the factor is zero.
  • Wind: the head of the fire speeds up by 1 + 0.6V1.5 (V in m/s): ×8 at 5 m/s, ×20 at 10 m/s. Sideways spread is unaffected; spread into the wind is slowed. Under a closed canopy the wind near the ground is only about 40% as strong (60% for open woods).
  • Slope: fire runs uphill because flames lean over the fuel ahead: the rate is multiplied by e3.9 tan(slope) — about double for a 10° slope uphill — and reduced going downhill.
  • Intensity (Byram): I = 300 × fuel load (kg/m²) × rate (m/min), in kW per metre of fire edge.
  • Crown fire (Van Wagner): if I exceeds (0.010 × crown base height × 1,500)1.5, the fire climbs into the treetops. Undergrowth acts as ladder fuel, lowering the effective crown base height. Crown fires spread 3.3× faster, kill the trees and throw embers. Broadleaf canopies are too green to carry a crown fire in this model.
  • Embers (spotting): crown fires and very intense fires launch embers downwind, typically 12 m for every 1 m/s of wind (with a long random tail). An ember landing on dry fuel can start a new fire — this is how fires jump tracks and rivers. Eucalyptus bark throws the most embers.
  • Burn times: fine fuels flame only briefly — grass about 1.5 minutes, heather and litter 3, bracken 2, the fuel under trees 3–4 (crown flames about 2). Then the cell smoulders (logs and stumps for 40 minutes, litter 10, other fuels 3). Smouldering fuel can still set light to unburnt fuel next to it, at 20% of the flaming rate.
  • Trees: crown fires kill trees; after a surface fire, broadleaf and eucalyptus trees often survive (scorched), pines and birches less often.
  • Not modelled: fire making its own wind and weather, wind gusts and eddies around hills, day/night changes in humidity, fuel drying ahead of the fire, oxygen limits, firefighting other than the water-drop tool, and different moisture in living plants.

The fire triangle

A fire needs heat, fuel and oxygen. Firefighters win by removing one: firebreaks remove fuel, water removes heat. Wind brings oxygen and pushes the flames onto new fuel; slopes put the fuel above the flames.
