Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Which test?

What to look for: Ask three questions: difference or correlation? related or unrelated design? nominal, ordinal or interval data? Mann-Whitney is for an unrelated design with ordinal data, Wilcoxon for related ordinal, Spearman for ordinal correlation, chi-squared for nominal unrelated data or association, the sign test for nominal related data.

Investigation 2: The sign test

What to look for: The critical value of S for N = 12 at 0.05 one-tailed is 2: S must be 2 or fewer. Ties are left out, which lowers N.

Investigation 3: Type I errors

What to look for: About 5% or slightly fewer (the ranked tests can't hit 5% exactly). Each of those is a Type I error: a false positive. A stricter level (0.01) makes them rarer.

Investigation 4: Type II errors and sample size

What to look for: With N = 8 most studies miss the effect (Type II errors). Bigger samples give more power. A stricter significance level (0.01) also makes Type II errors MORE likely.

Investigation 5: One- or two-tailed?

What to look for: One-tailed tests have smaller critical thresholds so they find a real effect (in the predicted direction) more often. Use one only when previous research justifies predicting the direction (a directional hypothesis).

Investigation 6: Repeated measures v independent groups A level

What to look for: When a person's two scores are similar (strong individual differences), a related design removes those participant variables, so the same effect is detected far more often. With 'how alike' near 0 there is no advantage.

Investigation 7: Parametric power A level

What to look for: The t-test is slightly more powerful when its criteria are met (interval data, normal, similar variances), but only slightly: Mann-Whitney is about 95% as efficient on normal data and is safer when the data are skewed or only ordinal.

Investigation 8: Correlation A level

What to look for: With N = 10 the critical rho is about 0.56 (one-tailed 0.05) so a true 0.4 is usually missed. Around N = 40 the power reaches about 80%.

Batch experiment 1: Type I error rate

The false-positive rate equals the significance level: that is what p ≤ 0.05 means. Run 20 studies with no effect and you expect about one 'significant' result.
Theory (α): 0.01: 0.01 · 0.025: 0.025 · 0.05: 0.05 · 0.1: 0.1
Result from a test run: 0.01: 0.00667 ± 0.00471 · 0.025: 0.0267 ± 0.00932 · 0.05: 0.04 ± 0.0113 · 0.1: 0.123 ± 0.019 (mean ± SE, 300 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Power and sample size (Mann-Whitney)

Small studies miss real effects most of the time (Type II errors). Psychologists aim for power of about 0.8. Ratings out of 10 create many tied ranks, so the simulation runs a little below the smooth approximation.
Theory (t approximation): 5: 0.275 · 8: 0.423 · 12: 0.578 · 16: 0.694 · 20: 0.782 · 25: 0.859 · 30: 0.91
Result from a test run: 5: 0.247 ± 0.0353 · 8: 0.313 ± 0.038 · 12: 0.52 ± 0.0409 · 16: 0.647 ± 0.0392 · 20: 0.827 ± 0.031 · 25: 0.827 ± 0.031 · 30: 0.907 ± 0.0238 (mean ± SE, 150 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Related designs and participant variables (A level)

Using the same people removes individual differences from the comparison, so the more those differences matter, the bigger the advantage of a repeated measures design.
Theory (t approximation): 0: 0.281 · 0.3: 0.361 · 0.5: 0.46 · 0.7: 0.648 · 0.85: 0.889
Result from a test run: 0: 0.313 ± 0.038 · 0.3: 0.333 ± 0.0386 · 0.5: 0.48 ± 0.0409 · 0.7: 0.667 ± 0.0386 · 0.85: 0.893 ± 0.0253 (mean ± SE, 150 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Spearman's rho: how big a sample? (A level)

Moderate correlations need large samples. A small study that 'finds no correlation' may just have had too little power.
