Forgetting · Teacher notesforgetting-curve.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The forgetting curve

What to look for: Most is lost in the first day or two, then the curve flattens: Ebbinghaus's exponential-like decline. Real figures depend on the material: meaningful material is forgotten more slowly than nonsense syllables.

Investigation 2: Cram or space it out?

What to look for: Spaced wins easily: each review comes after some forgetting, and effortful retrieval strengthens the memory more (the spacing effect). Cramming feels effective on day 1 but fades fast.

Investigation 3: Retroactive interference

What to look for: Similar new learning disrupts recall of the old list most (McGeoch and McDonald, 1931: synonyms worst, numbers barely interfered).

Investigation 4: Proactive interference

What to look for: Both reduce recall. Proactive interference builds up over several similar lists (Underwood, 1957: recall of a list fell with the number of lists learned before).

Investigation 5: Divers: context-dependent forgetting

What to look for: Matching contexts (land–land, water–water) recall about 40% more than mismatched ones: the environment acts as a retrieval cue. But the contexts were extremely different, which limits how far the finding generalises (e.g. to exam halls).

Investigation 6: Design: independent groups A level

What to look for: IVs: learning context and recall context (2 × 2). DV: words recalled. Repeated measures would need different word lists and counterbalancing (order effects), but give better control of individual differences.

Batch experiment 1: Ebbinghaus forgetting curve

Steep then shallow. The class average falls slightly more slowly than the average person's curve, because people with very stable memories keep some for longer.
Theory ((1 + 3t/S)^−½ for the average person): 0: 1 · 0.5: 0.632 · 1: 0.5 · 2: 0.378 · 4: 0.277 · 7: 0.213 · 14: 0.152 · 30: 0.105
Result from a test run: 0: 1 ± 0 · 0.5: 0.63 ± 0.00154 · 1: 0.502 ± 0.00333 · 2: 0.38 ± 0.00249 · 4: 0.282 ± 0.0022 · 7: 0.212 ± 0.00304 · 14: 0.155 ± 0.00131 · 30: 0.106 ± 0.00118 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: How many spaced reviews?

Each well-timed review adds a lot of stability. In this model the gain is larger when the review comes after more forgetting (a 'desirable difficulty').
Result from a test run: 0: 0.106 ± 0.00134 · 1: 0.164 ± 0.00143 · 2: 0.232 ± 8.49e-4 · 3: 0.327 ± 0.00178 · 4: 0.472 ± 0.00134 · 5: 0.686 ± 9.33e-4 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Similarity and retroactive interference

Falls steadily with similarity, the pattern McGeoch and McDonald reported. The model is a straight line by assumption; the real relationship need not be.
Theory (model average): 0: 14 · 0.2: 12.2 · 0.4: 10.4 · 0.6: 8.6 · 0.8: 6.8 · 1: 5
Result from a test run: 0: 14.1 ± 0.217 · 0.2: 12.2 ± 0.22 · 0.4: 10.4 ± 0.132 · 0.6: 8.38 ± 0.201 · 0.8: 6.94 ± 0.179 · 1: 4.96 ± 0.146 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Godden and Baddeley: matched minus mismatched

About 4 extra words out of about 11, but with only 10 divers per group the difference varies from study to study: one reason replications matter.
Theory (model effect): 4.02
Result from a test run: mean 4.02, SD 0.779, SE 0.045 from 300 runs
