Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Identical v non-identical twins

What to look for: MZ twins share all their genes, DZ twins about half, but both share their home. r_MZ ≈ A + C = 0.7 and r_DZ ≈ ½A + C = 0.45.

Investigation 2: No genes at all

What to look for: Both correlations ≈ 0.4: twins are alike only because they share a home. Equal MZ and DZ correlations point to environment, not genes.

Investigation 3: Concordance for a disorder

What to look for: About 45% for MZ and 17% for DZ, close to Gottesman's (1991) schizophrenia figures (48% and 17%). Identical genes give the same vulnerability (diathesis), but whether someone passes the threshold also depends on their own environment (stress).

Investigation 4: Diathesis and stress

What to look for: More people pass the threshold and concordance rises: the same genetic diathesis is 'triggered' more often. Genes and environment interact (the interactionist approach).

Investigation 5: Falconer's formula A level

What to look for: On average yes (0.4), but with 100 pairs each estimate can easily be out by ±0.2: twin studies need hundreds or thousands of pairs.

Investigation 6: The equal environments assumption A level

What to look for: It overestimates: about 0.4 + 2 × 0.15 = 0.7. The extra similarity caused by environment is wrongly credited to genes. Critics of twin studies argue this happens in real life.

Investigation 7: Adoption and selective placement A level

What to look for: Without selective placement r(biological) ≈ ½A and r(adoptive) ≈ C. Selective placement puts children in homes like their birth families, so the adoptive home carries some of the genetic similarity: both correlations change and the two influences become confounded.

Batch experiment 1: Falconer's estimate v true heritability

The formula is unbiased when its assumptions hold, but each estimate scatters a lot: doubling a difference of two noisy correlations doubles the noise.
Theory (estimate = truth): 0: 0 · 0.2: 0.2 · 0.4: 0.4 · 0.6: 0.6 · 0.8: 0.8
Result from a test run: 0: 0.0877 ± 0.0676 · 0.2: 0.148 ± 0.0602 · 0.4: 0.363 ± 0.0345 · 0.6: 0.647 ± 0.0449 · 0.8: 0.807 ± 0.029 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: How many twin pairs?

Small twin studies give very uncertain heritabilities. The spread roughly halves each time the sample is multiplied by four (standard error ∝ 1/√n).
Theory (true h²): 25: 0.5 · 50: 0.5 · 100: 0.5 · 200: 0.5 · 400: 0.5 · 800: 0.5
Result from a test run: 25: 0.749 ± 0.108 · 50: 0.558 ± 0.0777 · 100: 0.503 ± 0.0505 · 200: 0.508 ± 0.025 · 400: 0.492 ± 0.0256 · 800: 0.499 ± 0.0185 (mean ± SE, 12 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Breaking the equal environments assumption (A level)

Every bit of extra MZ similarity counts double in the heritability estimate. This is the strongest criticism of twin studies.
Theory (0.4 + 2 × extra): 0: 0.4 · 0.05: 0.5 · 0.1: 0.6 · 0.15: 0.7 · 0.2: 0.8
Result from a test run: 0: 0.466 ± 0.0453 · 0.05: 0.495 ± 0.0399 · 0.1: 0.622 ± 0.0102 · 0.15: 0.642 ± 0.0263 · 0.2: 0.768 ± 0.0212 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: MZ concordance v heritability

Even with high heritability, concordance stays well below 100%: genes raise the risk but most MZ co-twins of someone with a rare disorder never pass the threshold.
Result from a test run: 0.2: 0.0216 ± 0.00721 · 0.4: 0.115 ± 0.00807 · 0.6: 0.199 ± 0.00902 · 0.8: 0.381 ± 0.0115 · 0.9: 0.564 ± 0.00788 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)
