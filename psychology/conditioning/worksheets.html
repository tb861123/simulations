Learning: Conditioning · Teacher notesconditioning.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Pavlov's dog

What to look for: Fast at first, then levelling off: each trial adds a fraction of what is left to learn (Rescorla–Wagner, ΔV = αβ(λ − V)). The bell has become a conditioned stimulus.

Investigation 2: Extinction and spontaneous recovery

What to look for: Salivation fades (extinction): the bell no longer predicts food. After a rest some response returns (spontaneous recovery), so extinction is new learning, not forgetting.

Investigation 3: Generalisation

What to look for: Yes, but less for bigger differences: a generalisation gradient. Little Albert's fear of the rat generalised to other white furry things (Watson and Rayner, 1920).

Investigation 4: A rat learns to press

What to look for: It gets steeper as pressing is reinforced (operant conditioning by positive reinforcement). The slope of a cumulative record is the response rate.

Investigation 5: Schedules of reinforcement

What to look for: Ratio schedules give fast pressing (more presses = more food), VR the steadiest. Fixed interval gives a scallop: little pressing just after food, speeding up as the time comes round again. Variable interval gives slow, steady pressing.

Investigation 6: Which is hardest to extinguish? A level

What to look for: The VR rat: it is used to many unrewarded presses, so non-reward is not surprising (the partial reinforcement extinction effect). That is why gambling (a VR schedule) is so persistent.

Batch experiment 1: Rescorla–Wagner learning curve

Negatively accelerated: big gains early, small gains later. Individual trials scatter because the response is noisy.
Theory (10(1 − 0.8ⁿ⁻¹)): 1: 0 · 2: 2 · 4: 4.88 · 6: 6.72 · 8: 7.9 · 12: 9.14 · 16: 9.65 · 24: 9.94
Result from a test run: 1: 0.25 ± 0.164 · 2: 2.13 ± 0.35 · 4: 5.13 ± 0.441 · 6: 7.63 ± 0.375 · 8: 7.63 ± 0.46 · 12: 8.5 ± 0.567 · 16: 9.75 ± 0.366 · 24: 9.75 ± 0.491 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Generalisation gradient

The more different the stimulus, the weaker the response. Discrimination training (food only with one tone) would make the gradient narrower.
Theory (bell-shaped gradient): 0: 9.95 · 0.5: 9.41 · 1: 7.97 · 1.5: 6.03 · 2: 4.09 · 3: 1.35 · 4: 0.284
Result from a test run: 0: 10.1 ± 0.515 · 0.5: 8.88 ± 0.441 · 1: 8.13 ± 0.398 · 1.5: 6.25 ± 0.453 · 2: 4.38 ± 0.42 · 3: 1.5 ± 0.267 · 4: 0.375 ± 0.183 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Partial reinforcement extinction effect (A level)

Rats trained with rarer, unpredictable rewards keep pressing much longer when rewards stop: non-reward is less of a surprise.
Result from a test run: 1: 51.8 ± 2.93 · 2: 65 ± 1.96 · 5: 101 ± 6.38 · 10: 151 ± 6.91 · 20: 241 ± 31.5 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Response rate under ratio schedules

Higher ratios keep the rate high only if enough rewards still arrive to sustain learning; very lean schedules learned from scratch lead to slow learning ('ratio strain').
Result from a test run: 1: 57.1 ± 0.0854 · 2: 57 ± 0.25 · 5: 55.8 ± 1.32 · 10: 49.6 ± 7.24 · 20: 52.3 ± 2.11 · 40: 34.8 ± 3.49 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)
