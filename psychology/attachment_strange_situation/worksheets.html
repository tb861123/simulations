Teacher notes

Investigation 1: A secure infant

What to look for: Happy exploration with the caregiver as a secure base, some stranger anxiety, clear separation anxiety, then on reunion the infant seeks contact, is comforted quickly and goes back to playing.

Investigation 2: Avoidant and resistant

What to look for: The avoidant infant shows little distress and ignores the caregiver at reunion, but its heart rate rises just like the others (Sroufe and Waters, 1977): it is hiding distress. The resistant infant is very distressed, seeks contact but pushes away, and is slow to settle.

Investigation 3: Caregiver sensitivity

What to look for: Less sensitive (but consistent) care produces more avoidant infants; inconsistent care produces more resistant infants. This is Ainsworth's caregiver sensitivity hypothesis (supported by De Wolff and van IJzendoorn's meta-analysis, though the correlation was only modest).

Investigation 4: Cultural variation A level

What to look for: Germany: more avoidant (encouraging independence). Japan: more resistant and almost no avoidant: Japanese infants were rarely separated from their mothers, so the Strange Situation was far more stressful for them. The test may not measure the same thing in every culture (an imposed etic).

Investigation 5: Small samples A level

What to look for: By several percentage points either way. Van IJzendoorn found variation WITHIN countries was 1.5 times bigger than BETWEEN countries, and many samples were small, so 'national' figures should be treated with caution.

Investigation 6: Stages of attachment

What to look for: Around 7–9 months (the specific stage, with separation and stranger anxiety), usually the mother (about 85% here; 65% in Schaffer and Emerson's study, with many more joint first attachments). Multiple attachments follow soon after for some, and most have several by 18 months (87% in Schaffer and Emerson's study).

Investigation 7: Responsive v present

What to look for: Responsiveness. Schaffer and Emerson found babies attached to the carers who responded most sensitively to their signals, not those who spent most time with them or fed them.

Batch experiment 1: Caregiver sensitivity and secure attachment

More sensitive care gives more secure attachment, but even at high sensitivity some infants are classed as insecure: temperament and inconsistency matter too.
Result from a test run: 0.4: 28.3 ± 2.4 · 0.5: 42.5 ± 1.73 · 0.6: 61.8 ± 2.14 · 0.7: 75.3 ± 2.6 · 0.8: 85 ± 1.93 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: How much do small samples vary? (A level)

Look at the SD: samples of 20 to 40 (common in the 1980s) can easily be 10 percentage points off. Differences between countries need large samples to be trusted.
Theory (population ≈ 75%): 20: 75 · 40: 75 · 80: 75 · 160: 75 · 320: 75
Result from a test run: 20: 74 ± 3.32 · 40: 76.5 ± 2.24 · 80: 72 ± 2.02 · 160: 73.2 ± 0.957 · 320: 74.3 ± 0.68 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: The father's responsiveness

A responsive father is very often a second attachment figure (or even the first).
Result from a test run: 0.2: 62.7 ± 2.33 · 0.4: 73.3 ± 1.83 · 0.55: 85 ± 1.9 · 0.7: 94 ± 1.25 · 0.9: 99.3 ± 0.408 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Time spent v responsiveness

Only a little: in this model, as in Schaffer and Emerson's findings, sensitive responding builds attachment far more than simply being there.
Result from a test run: 0.1: 21.7 ± 2.79 · 0.3: 26 ± 2.72 · 0.5: 31 ± 3.27 · 0.7: 27.3 ± 2.08 · 1: 33.7 ± 3.85 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)
