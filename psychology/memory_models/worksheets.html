Models of Memory · Teacher notesmemory-models.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: The serial position curve

What to look for: The first few (primacy) and the last few (recency). Early words were rehearsed more and reached LTM; the last words are still in STM at recall. The middle words got little rehearsal and were pushed out of STM.

Investigation 2: Counting backwards

What to look for: Recency vanishes: counting fills STM and displaces the last words. Primacy is unaffected because those words were already in LTM. This supports separate STM and LTM stores.

Investigation 3: Slower presentation

What to look for: Primacy (and the middle) rises: more rehearsal time means more transfer to LTM. Recency barely changes: the last words are in STM either way. Rehearsal affects LTM, not STM.

Investigation 4: How long does STM last?

What to look for: About 75–80% after 3 s falling to under 10% after 18 s: without rehearsal, STM lasts less than about 18–30 s. Counting stops rehearsal, but it may also cause interference rather than pure decay.

Investigation 5: Capacity: digit span

What to look for: About 7 letters and 9 digits (Jacobs, 1887; Miller's 'magical number 7 ± 2'). Chunking (grouping into meaningful units like FBI-BBC) lets more items fit because the capacity is in chunks.

Investigation 6: Dual tasks and working memory A level

What to look for: Two verbal tasks compete for the phonological loop and slow each other a lot; a visual task uses the visuo-spatial sketchpad and hardly interferes. Evidence that STM has separate components (Baddeley and Hitch).

Batch experiment 1: Serial position curve

U-shaped: primacy (first few) and recency (last four or so) with a trough in the middle.
Result from a test run: 1: 0.567 ± 0.0236 · 2: 0.5 ± 0.0304 · 3: 0.292 ± 0.0551 · 4: 0.2 ± 0.0609 · 5: 0.233 ± 0.0333 · 7: 0.3 ± 0.0653 · 9: 0.275 ± 0.016 · 11: 0.358 ± 0.021 · 13: 0.617 ± 0.0518 · 14: 0.717 ± 0.0397 · 15: 0.958 ± 0.016 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Glanzer and Cunitz: does a delay remove recency?

Any filled delay empties the STM buffer in this model, so the last word drops to LTM-only recall. Glanzer and Cunitz found recency had gone by 30 s.
Result from a test run: 0: 0.95 ± 0.00745 · 10: 0.05 ± 0.0143 · 30: 0.0833 ± 0.0167 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Peterson and Peterson: duration of STM

A steep fall: most information is lost within about 18 s without rehearsal. (The model is fitted to resemble the original findings.)
Theory (fitted decay 1.18e^(−t/6.5)): 3: 0.744 · 6: 0.469 · 9: 0.295 · 12: 0.186 · 15: 0.117 · 18: 0.074
Result from a test run: 3: 0.727 ± 0.0226 · 6: 0.501 ± 0.0155 · 9: 0.29 ± 0.0267 · 12: 0.164 ± 0.0015 · 15: 0.111 ± 0.0119 · 18: 0.0807 ± 0.0161 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Digit span (Jacobs)

Most spans are within about ±2 of the mean, echoing Miller's 7 ± 2 for letters and words. Individual differences (age, practice, chunking) matter.
Theory (Jacobs: 9.3 digits): 9.3
Result from a test run: mean 9.36, SD 1.33, SE 0.0664 from 400 runs

Batch experiment 5: Dual-task interference (A level)

Positive for almost everyone: tasks competing for one component of working memory interfere. The model's numbers are illustrative, not from a particular study.
Theory (model's average slow-down): 0.63
Result from a test run: mean 0.622, SD 0.282, SE 0.0163 from 300 runs
