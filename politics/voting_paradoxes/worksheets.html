Voting Paradoxes and the Median Voter · Teacher notesvoting-paradoxes.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Race to the centre

What to look for: Each move towards the middle wins the voters in between, so both parties converge on the median voter: very similar policies (consensus politics, 'Blatcherism' in the 1990s and 2000s).

Investigation 2: A polarised electorate

What to look for: Yes, in this simple model: the median is still in the middle even if few voters live there. Parties chase the median, not the crowd, so polarised voters can face centrist parties and feel unrepresented.

Investigation 3: Alienated voters stay at home

What to look for: Moving to the centre now loses your own extreme voters, who abstain. With polarised voters, parties stay apart. This helps explain adversarial politics and turnout.

Investigation 4: A third party

What to look for: With three parties there is no stable position: the centre party is squeezed from both sides and parties keep leapfrogging. Downs' theorem only works for two.

Investigation 5: Condorcet's paradox

What to look for: A beats B (67%), B beats C (68%), but C beats A (65%): a cycle. There is no 'will of the majority'. Each voter is rational, but the majority is not.

Investigation 6: Control the agenda

What to look for: Each agenda gives a different winner: whoever chooses the order of votes chooses the result. This is why control of the parliamentary timetable matters.

Investigation 7: The spoiler A level

What to look for: A wins on first choices with 40%, but B (the Condorcet winner) beats A and C one-to-one. C's voters 'split' the anti-A vote: a spoiler. AV doesn't fix it here: B has the fewest first choices, so is eliminated first and C wins (the 'centre squeeze'). Three rules, three winners.

Investigation 8: Tactical voting A level

What to look for: Its supporters switch to the less-bad of the top two, so the third party's vote collapses: one reason FPTP tends to produce two-party systems (Duverger's law).

Investigation 9: Primaries pull apart A level

What to look for: Each party is pulled towards the median of its own side, not of the whole electorate: a reason US politics has polarised as primaries became decisive.

Batch experiment 1: How common are cycles? (three candidates)

With 3 voters the exact answer is 1/18 = 5.6%; it rises towards 8.77% as the electorate grows. Real electorates are less random than this, so cycles are rarer, but they do happen.
Theory (Guilbaud limit 8.77%): 3: 8.77 · 5: 8.77 · 11: 8.77 · 25: 8.77 · 51: 8.77 · 101: 8.77 · 501: 8.77
Result from a test run: 3: 4.75 ± 1.02 · 5: 6 ± 0.928 · 11: 8.25 ± 0.99 · 25: 6.75 ± 0.99 · 51: 9 ± 1.94 · 101: 11 ± 1.76 · 501: 6.5 ± 1.4 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: More candidates, more cycles (A level)

With seven options there is no majority winner over a third of the time: 'the will of the people' is not always defined. This is the heart of Arrow's impossibility theorem.
Theory (large-electorate theory): 3: 8.77 · 4: 17.6 · 5: 25.1 · 6: 31.5 · 7: 36.9
Result from a test run: 3: 8.13 ± 1.62 · 4: 16.3 ± 0.472 · 5: 24.7 ± 1.6 · 6: 31.6 ± 2.87 · 7: 35.3 ± 2.08 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Do two parties converge?

The winner sits within a step or two of the median voter every time: Downs' prediction.
Theory (median voter): 0
Result from a test run: mean -0.00192, SD 0.0154, SE 0.00126 from 150 runs

Batch experiment 4: Abstention keeps parties apart

When voters stay at home unless a party is close, parties must stay near their own camp: polarisation. With no abstention (large distance) they converge.
Result from a test run: 0.2: 1.12 ± 0.0142 · 0.3: 1.1 ± 0.0159 · 0.4: 1.19 ± 0.0117 · 0.6: 0.828 ± 0.00477 · 0.8: 0.41 ± 0.00447 · 1.2: 0.01 ± 7.96e-18 · 2: 0.01 ± 1.19e-17 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Tactical voting squeezes the third party (A level)

A straight-line squeeze: every tactical voter is lost. Under FPTP a party that is third in the polls loses votes it would win under a proportional system, part of Duverger's law (FPTP tends to two parties).
Theory (sincere share × (1 − tactical fraction)): 0: 5.73 · 20: 4.59 · 40: 3.44 · 60: 2.29 · 80: 1.15 · 100: 0
Result from a test run: 0: 5.28 ± 0.0829 · 20: 4.66 ± 0.123 · 40: 3.69 ± 0.0851 · 60: 2.26 ± 0.114 · 80: 1.24 ± 0.0625 · 100: 0 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)
