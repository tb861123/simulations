Electoral Systems · Teacher noteselectoral-systems.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: A majority from a minority

What to look for: About 36% of the votes gives about half the seats, worth about 13 points more than its vote share; push Labour up a few points and it has a 'manufactured majority'. The winner's vote is spread efficiently, winning many seats by small margins, while Reform's 15% wins nothing.

Investigation 2: Spread thin or concentrated?

What to look for: Reform's vote is spread evenly, so it comes second in many places and wins few. The Lib Dems' vote is concentrated in some areas, so a smaller national share wins more seats.

Investigation 3: Wasted votes

What to look for: Often over half of all votes elect nobody. Deep-coloured seats are won with big majorities (safe); in marginals a small swing changes the winner.

Investigation 4: Proportional representation

What to look for: Seats ≈ votes, so the Gallagher index falls from about 15–20 to 1–3. Smaller parties gain; no party has a majority, so a coalition would be needed.

Investigation 5: Two-party politics

What to look for: With two big parties FPTP gives a clear winner and is reasonably proportional for them. With five similar-sized parties it becomes almost a lottery: small swings give huge seat changes.

Investigation 6: The supplementary vote A level

What to look for: No: SV only counts second choices for the top two, and ballots whose 2nd choice is someone else are exhausted, so winners can still have under half of all votes.

Investigation 7: STV and district size A level

What to look for: Bigger districts mean lower quotas (25% with 3 seats, 10% with 9), so more parties win seats and LSq falls. Voters can also rank candidates within a party.

Investigation 8: AMS: topping up A level

What to look for: List seats go mostly to parties under-represented in the constituencies (small parties, and the losers of FPTP); the big winner gets few or none. With enough list seats the result is close to proportional.

Investigation 9: Thresholds A level

What to look for: Parties under the threshold get no seats, and their votes are effectively wasted; their share goes to bigger parties. Thresholds keep out very small or extreme parties.

Batch experiment 1: FPTP: the cube law

A small lead in votes becomes a big lead in seats: the winner's bonus. The cube law (an old rule of thumb for UK elections) fits roughly; how steep the real curve is depends on how many seats are marginal. Here the curve also sits a little to the right: this country's geography (and where the Lib Dems win) slightly favours the Conservatives, an electoral bias like those in real UK elections.
Theory (cube law s/(1−s) = (v/(1−v))³): 40: 22.9 · 44: 32.7 · 46: 38.2 · 48: 44 · 50: 50 · 52: 56 · 54: 61.8 · 56: 67.3 · 60: 77.1
Result from a test run: 40: 26.8 ± 1.25 · 44: 34.5 ± 1.32 · 46: 38.3 ± 0.629 · 48: 42.8 ± 0.479 · 50: 44.5 ± 1.26 · 52: 50.8 ± 1.03 · 54: 54.5 ± 0.645 · 56: 60.3 ± 0.946 · 60: 71 ± 0.707 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: How proportional is each system?

FPTP, SV and AV (all single-member, majoritarian) are far less proportional than STV, AMS and list PR. AV is not a proportional system: it can even exaggerate landslides.
Result from a test run: 1: 17.1 ± 1.14 · 2: 16.4 ± 0.546 · 3: 15.8 ± 0.781 · 4: 7.07 ± 0.383 · 5: 0.486 ± 0.0897 · 6: 2.67 ± 0.056 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Concentration wins seats

Evenly spread, 12% wins almost nothing; concentrated, the same 12% wins seats where the party is strong. This is why FPTP is kinder to regional parties (like the SNP) than to nationally spread ones. The gain levels off: beyond a point, extra concentration just piles up bigger majorities in seats already won.
Result from a test run: 0: 0 ± 0 · 0.25: 0.25 ± 0.25 · 0.5: 4 ± 0.408 · 0.75: 10.5 ± 0.866 · 1: 13.5 ± 0.289 · 1.25: 15.3 ± 0.479 · 1.5: 14.8 ± 0.629 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: List PR: the threshold

Low until the threshold passes a party's share, then a jump: that party's votes win nothing and its seats go to others.
Result from a test run: 0: 0.312 ± 0.075 · 2: 0.207 ± 0.0478 · 3: 1.47 ± 0.563 · 4: 4.5 ± 0.0615 · 5: 4.64 ± 0.0307 · 7: 4.59 ± 0.042 · 10: 9.14 ± 0.0697 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: STV: seats per constituency (A level)

One seat per constituency is just AV. Each extra seat lowers the quota, letting smaller parties win; the Irish Dáil uses 3–5, the NI Assembly 5.
Result from a test run: 1: 14.8 ± 0.467 · 2: 16.8 ± 0.906 · 3: 12 ± 0.616 · 4: 8.04 ± 0.853 · 5: 6.92 ± 0.234 · 7: 6.39 ± 0.434 · 10: 3.38 ± 0.131 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)
