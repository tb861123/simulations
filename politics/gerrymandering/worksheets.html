Gerrymandering and District Maps · Teacher notesgerrymandering.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Same votes, different winners

What to look for: Each map gives a different split, from about 4–6 to 6–4, without any vote changing. Where the lines go matters as much as how people vote.

Investigation 2: Gerrymander for Red

What to look for: It packs Blue voters into a few districts Blue wins with huge margins and cracks the rest across districts Red wins narrowly: 7 or 8 seats from half the votes.

Investigation 3: Now for Blue

What to look for: The same trick works both ways: a party that draws the lines can turn 50% of the votes into most of the seats.

Investigation 4: Spot the packing

What to look for: The victim's votes are wasted in both kinds of district: surplus votes in packed ones, losing votes in cracked ones. The efficiency gap measures the difference.

Investigation 5: Draw your own

What to look for: It's easy if districts can have different numbers of voters (malapportionment), which is why courts require equal populations ('one person, one vote', Reynolds v Sims 1964).

Investigation 6: Is this map an outlier? A level

What to look for: Random fair maps mostly give Blue 4–6 seats. A map giving 2 or 8 is far outside the range: strong statistical evidence of deliberate gerrymandering (used in US state courts).

Investigation 7: Natural packing A level

What to look for: Blue voters packed into a dense city waste surplus votes even under neutral maps: 'unintentional gerrymandering' by geography, as with Democrats in cities. Similar efficiency effects create electoral bias in the UK.

Batch experiment 1: Random fair maps

Fair maps cluster around 4–6 seats. The spread itself is a warning: even neutral line-drawing changes results, so 'fair' needs defining.
Theory (proportional): 5
Result from a test run: mean 4.79, SD 0.862, SE 0.0703 from 150 runs

Batch experiment 2: How much can a gerrymander win?

A gerrymander can win a majority of seats from well under half of the votes: the dots lie far above the proportional line.
Theory (proportional): 35: 3.5 · 40: 4 · 45: 4.5 · 50: 5 · 55: 5.5 · 60: 6
Result from a test run: 35: 2.67 ± 0.333 · 40: 4.33 ± 0.333 · 45: 5.67 ± 0.333 · 50: 7 ± 0 · 55: 8.67 ± 0.333 · 60: 10 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Efficiency gap of fair maps

Centred near zero (with a lean from geography): one proposed legal test treated gaps above about 7% as suspicious. Compare with a gerrymandered map's gap.
Theory (no bias): 0
Result from a test run: mean 2.07, SD 8.62, SE 0.703 from 150 runs

Batch experiment 4: Seats and votes, rows as districts

A steep S-curve, as under any single-member system: a small swing flips several districts. Random noise in the precincts makes each run a little different.
Theory (proportional): 35: 3.5 · 40: 4 · 45: 4.5 · 50: 5 · 55: 5.5 · 60: 6 · 65: 6.5
Result from a test run: 35: 0.25 ± 0.25 · 40: 1.5 ± 0.289 · 45: 2.75 ± 0.25 · 50: 5 ± 0.408 · 55: 7 ± 0.408 · 60: 9 ± 0.408 · 65: 9.75 ± 0.25 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)
