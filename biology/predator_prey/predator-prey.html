How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Quality: the grass can be stored in 50 m squares (Low), or in finer cells of 25 m, 17 m or 12.5 m. A rabbit always grazes a 50 m × 50 m area centred on itself, the grass regrows at the same rate per week and a full field holds the same amount of grass, so populations behave the same at every setting; finer cells just let the grazed patches move smoothly with the animals. Batch runs always use Low, so their results can be repeated exactly.
  • Field: square patches, each 50 m × 50 m (0.25 ha). A field of 30 × 30 patches is 1.5 km × 1.5 km (225 ha). Animals can't leave the field and none arrive from outside.
  • Grass: each patch holds 0–1 "units" of grass. Each week it regrows by r × (1 − g): fastest when grazed bare, slowing as it fills up.
  • Rabbits: each week a rabbit hops up to 1.5 patches, to the grassier of two spots it tries. It eats up to 0.3 units of grass (8 units of energy per unit eaten) and uses 1 unit of energy. Energy is capped at 8. From 12 weeks old, a doe with at least 4 units of energy has a litter with the chance set by the breeding rate; about 3 young survive to leave the nest. Half the rabbits are does. Rabbits die if their energy runs out, and 1% die each week of other causes (most live about 2 years).
  • Foxes: each week a fox roams up to 3 patches and makes up to 5 attacks on random prey within 2 patches of where it ends up (its own patch and the 24 around it). Each attack succeeds with the hunting-success chance. A rabbit gives 1 unit of energy, a vole 0.25. A fox uses the "energy needs" amount each week, stores up to 6 units, and starves at 0. From 6 months old, a fox with at least 3 units of energy breeds with a 2% chance per week (half the foxes are vixens, each with a 4% chance), giving an average litter of 2.5 cubs that survive. 0.4% of foxes die each week of other causes.
  • Refuges: rabbits like to hop towards warren patches. In a warren patch up to 6 rabbits are safe from foxes; if more crowd in, the extras can be caught.
  • Voles (optional) eat less grass, breed faster and give a fox less food. Foxes eat whichever prey they meet, so voles can keep foxes alive when rabbits are scarce.
  • Culling: the chosen share of foxes is removed spread evenly through the year.
  • Lotka–Volterra rates from the counts: after the first year the model counts every birth and death. a = (rabbit births − rabbit deaths not caused by foxes) ÷ (rabbits summed over the weeks); b = rabbits eaten ÷ (rabbits × foxes summed over the weeks); c = fox births ÷ the same sum; d = fox deaths ÷ (foxes summed over the weeks). These give the theory period 2π/√(ad) and the equilibrium (d/c rabbits, a/b foxes). (The batch lab also reports an older fit from the shape of the cycles.)
  • Lotka–Volterra-like rules (choose under Rules) strip out the extra realism: grass is unlimited, rabbits don't starve, age or die except when caught, every doe breeds at the same rate from birth, each rabbit within 2 patches of a fox is caught with chance (hunting success ÷ 5) a week, each rabbit eaten gives a 10% chance of a cub, and foxes die with a fixed 4% chance a week. Then a = breeding rate × 3 ÷ 2 and d = 0.04 follow from the rules. Only the fox maturity delay (if you set one) and chance remain.
  • Why realistic cycles are slower: the equations assume a fox's birth rate responds instantly to food. Real foxes take months to grow up before they can breed, and starve only after their reserves run out; rabbits also need time to mature and are held back by grass. These delays stretch each cycle.
  • Differences from the equations: the equations have no grass limit, no ages, no space and no chance. Real cycles are more irregular, populations can die out by chance (the equations never reach zero), and crowding limits rabbits even without foxes.
  • Mark–release–recapture (Advanced). Each trapping catches every rabbit independently with the chance chosen (so the number caught varies at random, like real trapping). Caught rabbits are marked (a blue ring) and released; marks never fall off. A later trapping counts all rabbits caught and how many carry a mark. Lincoln index: N ≈ n₁ × n₂ ÷ m. The trapping uses its own random numbers, so it never changes what happens in the field. Births, deaths and foxes between the two trappings break the "closed population" assumption, as they would in a real field.
  • Not modelled: seasons (all breeding happens year round), other predators (buzzards, stoats), hunting by people other than culling, rabbits digging new warrens, and movement into or out of the field.
