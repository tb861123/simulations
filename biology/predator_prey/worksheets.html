Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Boom and bust

What to look for: The numbers rise and fall in cycles. Plenty of rabbits means foxes are well fed and breed, so fox numbers rise; more foxes eat more rabbits, so rabbits fall; with few rabbits, foxes starve and their numbers fall; with few foxes, rabbits recover, and round it goes. The fox peak always comes after the rabbit peak. The classic real example is the snowshoe hare and the lynx in Canada, recorded for over a century in fur-trapping records.

Investigation 2: Take away the foxes

What to look for: The rabbits multiply fast, then run out of food: the grass is eaten down and the population levels off, with rabbits starving as fast as they are born. The meadow can only support a certain number: its carrying capacity. Without predators the rabbits are limited by food instead, and the meadow is grazed bare. This is what happened when rabbits were introduced to Australia.

Investigation 3: A disease strikes

What to look for: Rabbits crash at once. The foxes do not die at once but go hungry: their numbers fall over the following months. With few foxes and plenty of grass, the surviving rabbits then recover quickly, often overshooting. A change to one species travels through the food chain, with a delay. In Britain, buzzards and foxes declined after myxomatosis, and the grassland changed too.

Investigation 4: Culling the predator

What to look for: With fewer foxes, more rabbits survive, and the average number of rabbits rises: more damage to crops and grass. Removing a predator often causes its prey to boom. This is why wolves were brought back to Yellowstone, where too many deer were stripping the vegetation.

Investigation 5: Somewhere to hide

What to look for: A few refuges make the system steadier: foxes can never catch the last rabbits, so the rabbit population never crashes as low and recovers sooner. With too many refuges the foxes can hardly find a meal and die out. In real ecosystems hiding places, difficult terrain and prey that are hard to catch all help predator and prey to coexist.

Investigation 6: A small island

What to look for: On a small patch the numbers are small, so when rabbits are scarce a run of bad luck can wipe out the last few foxes (or the last few rabbits), and once a species has gone it cannot come back. On a big meadow the animals in different places are out of step with each other, so somewhere there are always survivors to recolonise the rest. This is one reason small nature reserves lose species, and why wildlife corridors matter.

Investigation 7: The loop A level

What to look for: The path goes round anticlockwise: many rabbits and few foxes (bottom right) → foxes rise (top right) → rabbits fall (top left) → foxes fall (bottom left) → rabbits rise again. A steady ecosystem would be a single point. The Lotka–Volterra equations predict closed loops; here chance and the grass make the loops wander, shrinking and growing from cycle to cycle.

Investigation 8: Better hunters, fewer foxes? A level

What to look for: More efficient predators drive the prey lower, then starve: the swings get more violent and the risk of extinction rises. Predators that are 'too good' destroy their own food supply. The same thing can happen if the prey's food supply is enriched, which also makes the cycles more violent (the 'paradox of enrichment').

Batch experiment 1: Carrying capacity

The number of rabbits is set by how fast the grass can regrow: about double the regrowth, double the rabbits. That number is the meadow's carrying capacity.
