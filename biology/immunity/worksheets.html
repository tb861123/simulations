Teacher notes

Investigation 1: A first infection

What to look for: Phagocytes act at once but cannot keep up; specific lymphocytes take about a week to be activated and divide, so antibodies only rise after the pathogen has multiplied: several days of illness.

Investigation 2: The second time

What to look for: Memory cells respond within a day: the secondary response is faster, bigger (about 8 times here) and lasts longer, so the pathogen is destroyed before it causes illness. You are immune.

Investigation 3: Vaccination

What to look for: The vaccine's antigens trigger a primary response and memory cells without illness, so the real infection meets a secondary response.

Investigation 4: Antibiotics and viruses

What to look for: Antibiotics kill bacteria (or stop them growing), so the bacterial infection ends sooner; they do nothing to viruses, which live inside our cells. Overusing them selects for resistant bacteria.

Investigation 5: A big dose

What to look for: A bigger dose reaches the illness threshold sooner, before the lymphocytes have responded, so the illness is longer. Hygiene reduces the dose.

Investigation 6: Passive immunity A level

What to look for: Passive immunity: ready-made antibodies protect at once but are broken down within weeks, and no memory cells form, so a later infection is a primary response. (Active immunity, from infection or vaccination, makes memory cells.)

Investigation 7: A weakened immune system A level

What to look for: Fewer B cells are activated and they divide more slowly, so antibodies stay low and the infection lasts much longer: opportunistic infections.

Batch experiment 1: How much bigger is the secondary response?

Usually several times bigger, with a lopsided spread: people whose first response was weak gain most from their memory cells.
Theory (average person): 8
Result from a test run: mean 8.08, SD 1.57, SE 0.111 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 2: Time to the antibody peak

About a week, whatever the gap: memory cells last for years. The first peak takes about two weeks.
Theory (average person): 30: 8.4 · 40: 8.4 · 60: 8.4 · 120: 8.4 · 200: 8.4 · 300: 8.4
Result from a test run: 30: 8.43 ± 0.0333 · 40: 8.47 ± 0.0333 · 60: 8.43 ± 0.0333 · 120: 8.4 ± 0 · 200: 8.43 ± 0.0333 · 300: 8.43 ± 0.0333 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Bigger doses, more illness

More pathogens reach the illness threshold sooner, before the specific response arrives.
Theory (average person): -1: 6.22 · 0: 7.86 · 1: 10.1 · 2: 12.2 · 3: 12.2
Result from a test run: -1: 6.26 ± 0.134 · 0: 7.94 ± 0.114 · 1: 10.1 ± 0.0972 · 2: 12.2 ± 0.0857 · 3: 12.2 ± 0.0723 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Does vaccination protect against big doses?

Vaccinated people are protected against normal doses; only a huge dose can outrun the secondary response.
Theory (average person): -1: 0 · 0: 0 · 1: 0.88 · 2: 4.3 · 3: 4.4
Result from a test run: -1: 0 ± 0 · 0: 0 ± 0 · 1: 0.708 ± 0.148 · 2: 4.27 ± 0.0492 · 3: 4.46 ± 0.0657 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Antibiotics: bacteria or virus?

Antibiotics cut a bacterial illness short but make no difference to a virus.
Theory (average person): 1: 7.86 · 2: 1.06 · 3: 8.86 · 4: 8.86
Result from a test run: 1: 7.99 ± 0.124 · 2: 1.13 ± 0.128 · 3: 8.71 ± 0.1 · 4: 8.81 ± 0.096 (mean ± SE, 20 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: How long does passive immunity last?

Protection fades within weeks as the injected antibodies are broken down: no memory cells are made.
Theory (average person): 5: 4.24 · 10: 7.02 · 20: 7.8 · 30: 7.84 · 40: 7.86 · 60: 7.86
Result from a test run: 5: 3.8 ± 0.495 · 10: 6.82 ± 0.2 · 20: 7.68 ± 0.167 · 30: 7.94 ± 0.185 · 40: 7.83 ± 0.242 · 60: 7.69 ± 0.118 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)
