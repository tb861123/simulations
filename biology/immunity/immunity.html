How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • The tissue is a patch about 1 mm × 0.6 mm under the skin, with a capillary along the bottom. Every pathogen, phagocyte, killer T cell and tissue cell is followed one at a time in steps of 6 minutes, with random movement. An intact barrier (skin, mucus) stops about 95% of the pathogens that land on it; a cut lets them all in.
  • Pathogens: bacteria divide about every 2.3 hours (slowing as they crowd) and stay outside cells; fungi (yeast cells) grow more slowly and take longer to digest; viruses must enter a tissue cell, which bursts after 8 hours releasing 6 new viruses; the malaria parasite (a protoctist) bursts its cell after 24 hours. Free viruses break down with a half-life of about 3 hours. Growth rates are slowed so that a few days fit on screen.
  • Non-specific (innate) response: damaged cells and pathogens cause inflammation (histamine from mast cells → vasodilation and leaky capillaries), so more neutrophils leave the blood. Phagocytes move towards the nearest pathogen (chemotaxis), engulf it into a phagosome, and a lysosome fuses with it and digests it (1 hour; 3 hours for fungi). A neutrophil dies after about 8 meals or a day (pus); macrophages that have digested pathogens present the antigens to lymphocytes.
  • Specific response (the lymph nodes, drawn as a box at the side): when antigen-presenting cells arrive, the T helper and B cells with matching receptors are selected and divide (clonal expansion); plasma cells release antibodies and some cells become memory cells. It is a smooth pulse: with no memory, antibodies appear after about 5 days and peak at about 2 weeks; with memory cells, they rise within 1–2 days, peak at about 6 days and reach about 8 times the level. Antibodies (IgG) are removed with a half-life of about 3 weeks. Antibodies stick to pathogens (agglutination slows them; opsonised pathogens are engulfed about 10 times faster; bound viruses are neutralised and cannot enter cells). Killer T cells arrive in proportion to the response and destroy infected cells (or, in autoimmune disease, healthy cells carrying a self-antigen).
  • Antibody responses (the graph mode): the same pulses, plus a pathogen load L that grows 2.6× per day (fewer as it crowds), is cleared by phagocytes (which are overwhelmed by large numbers), by antibodies (0.25 × A per day) and by killer T cells. A person is "ill" while L is above 100 times the dose that got in. A vaccine is dead or weakened pathogen (or its antigens) that cannot multiply: it causes a primary response 60% as big, with no illness. Passive immunity adds 40 units of antibody and no memory cells.
  • Blood smear (PAG1): white cells are picked at random from typical proportions (healthy: neutrophils 60%, lymphocytes 30%, monocytes 6%, eosinophils 3%, basophils 1%; textbook values, approximate) and the proportions change with infection. Each field has 1–3 white cells among red cells. Sizes vary ±8%; the eyepiece graticule (calibrated: 1 division = 2.5 µm at ×400) is read to the nearest half division.
  • Antibiotic discs (PAG7): zone diameter = 6 mm (the disc) + 9√(ln(content ÷ critical content)) mm, a simplified diffusion rule; critical contents are illustrative (MRSA resists penicillin; E. coli, a Gram-negative bacterium, is little affected by penicillin and not at all by vancomycin). Plates differ by about ±0.8 mm; rulers read to ±0.5 mm. Antibiotics have no effect on viruses.
  • Quality only changes the picture (antibodies drawn, tissue texture, detail inside cells); the models and batch runs are the same at every level.
  • Left out: complement, interferons, natural killer cells, antibody classes (IgM first, then IgG) as separate curves, the thymus and bone marrow, cytokine signalling in detail, fever, the different routes into the body (gut, lungs), mutation of the pathogen (antigenic variation, as in flu), immunological memory fading over years, and plant defences.
