Turing Patterns · Teacher notesturing-patterns.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Spots from nothing

What to look for: Each speck of activator grows by making more of itself, but uses up the fuel around it, so nothing can grow close by. Spots end up about the same size and the same distance apart everywhere, yet their exact positions depend on the random start, so no two runs are identical. That is exactly what we see in animals: every leopard has the same kind of pattern, and no two have the same spots.

Investigation 2: Wound healing

What to look for: The gap fills itself in: neighbouring spots divide and move until the spacing is even again. Scribbled lines break up into spots. The pattern is not stored anywhere; it is continually remade by the reaction, so it repairs itself. Zebrafish stripes do the same when pigment cells are removed with a laser.

Investigation 3: Spots or stripes?

What to look for: A small change in one number turns spots into stripes, then into a maze, then into dark skin with pale holes. The same two chemicals, with slightly different rates, could give a leopard, a zebra or a giraffe. Evolution only has to tweak a rate, not invent a new mechanism. The map in the bottom left shows where you are.

Investigation 4: Turing's condition

What to look for: With equal diffusion the pattern never forms: a patch of activator either dies or takes over everywhere. Patterns need the activator to stay put while its effect on the surroundings (here, the hole it eats in the fuel) spreads further. This was Turing's key insight, and it is surprising, because diffusion normally smooths differences out. Here it creates them.

Investigation 5: Big animals, small animals

What to look for: The spacing is set by how far the chemicals diffuse before they react, not by the size of the animal. Slower spreading gives a finer pattern. With the same chemistry a bigger animal would simply have more spots, and a very small embryo might have room for none: one reason some small animals are plain.

Investigation 6: Spots that divide

What to look for: The spot grows until its middle runs short of fuel, pinches in two, and each half does the same, until the skin is full. It looks like dividing cells, though nothing here is alive: it is the same reaction and diffusion. Patterns that copy themselves like this were discovered in real chemical experiments in 1994.

Investigation 7: Running out of room

What to look for: The spots keep their size and spacing, so fewer and fewer fit across: several rows on the body, two, then a single file along the tail, and where the tail is too thin, nothing. A pattern needs room. In some other reaction–diffusion models a narrowing tail turns spots into bands across it, which the mathematician James Murray suggested is why spotted cats such as cheetahs have banded tails; this simple model does not show that step.

Investigation 8: The whole zoo at once

What to look for: Spots, stripes, mazes, holes, travelling waves and regions that stay blank all appear, each in its own zone, with sharp borders between some of them. To the right the activator is destroyed faster than it can make itself and dies out; to the lower left it burns everything. Interesting patterns live in the narrow band between.

Investigation 9: Spiral waves

What to look for: The wave burns through the fuel and leaves a used-up zone behind it that takes time to recover, so it cannot turn back. Its free ends curl round into a pair of spirals turning in opposite directions, which keep sending out waves for ever. Any break in a wave makes a new pair. Spiral waves like this are seen in real chemical reactions (the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction), in colonies of slime moulds, and in heart muscle, where a spiral wave causes a dangerous abnormal heart rhythm.

Batch experiment 1: How far apart are the spots?

The random start decides exactly where the spots sit, so the count on a small patch varies by a few. Theory: a molecule diffuses a distance proportional to √(diffusion × time) before it reacts.
Theory (∝ √diffusion): 0.3: 10.6 · 0.4: 12.2 · 0.5: 13.6 · 0.65: 15.6 · 0.8: 17.3 · 1: 19.3
Result from a test run: 0.3: 10.7 ± 0.464 · 0.4: 13.4 ± 0.619 · 0.5: 15.5 ± 1.16 · 0.65: 15.5 ± 0.13 · 0.8: 17.3 ± 0.297 · 1: 19.5 ± 0.408 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Turing's condition

Below a critical ratio no stable spots form at all. Above it, a larger ratio packs more, smaller spots into the same area.
Result from a test run: 1: 0 ± 0 · 1.2: 0 ± 0 · 1.4: 0 ± 0 · 1.6: 0 ± 0 · 1.8: 1.25 ± 0.25 · 2: 24.5 ± 0.866 · 2.5: 50.8 ± 0.25 · 3: 67.3 ± 0.479 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Same rules, different animals

The kind of pattern and the typical spacing are fixed by the chemistry; the exact number and positions are not. Identical twins, and cloned animals, have different fingerprints and coat markings for this reason.
Result from a test run: mean 24.1, SD 1.06, SE 0.118 from 80 runs

Batch experiment 4: From spots to stripes

Low kill rate: the activator covers most of the skin, leaving holes. Higher: stripes, then separate spots, then nothing.
Result from a test run: 0.056: 100 ± 0 · 0.058: 73.1 ± 0.0767 · 0.06: 55 ± 0.14 · 0.062: 44.9 ± 0.159 · 0.064: 29.9 ± 0.126 · 0.066: 6.71 ± 0.256 · 0.068: 1.3 ± 0.359 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Spots joining up

Near 100% means one connected network (a maze or stripes that join); a few per cent means separate spots. The change between the two is abrupt.
Result from a test run: 0.056: 100 ± 0 · 0.058: 100 ± 0 · 0.06: 88.6 ± 7.32 · 0.062: 18.2 ± 0.868 · 0.064: 3.45 ± 0.0403 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: Filling the skin

Growth is fast at first, as every spot divides, then stops when the skin is full: the same S-shaped curve as a population growing until it runs out of room.
Result from a test run: 500: 1 ± 0 · 1000: 4 ± 0 · 1500: 8 ± 0 · 2000: 16 ± 0 · 2500: 16 ± 0 · 3000: 24 ± 0 · 4000: 26.7 ± 2.67 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)
