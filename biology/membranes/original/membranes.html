How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Particles: each dot takes a random step every 1/60 s (Brownian motion). The step is bigger when it is warmer: the diffusion coefficient D ∝ T ÷ viscosity (Stokes–Einstein), using the real viscosity of water, so D roughly doubles between 5 °C and 45 °C. Time is slowed right down: real molecules cross a cell membrane in far less than a second.
  • The membrane is a line the particles can cross with a probability ∝ surface area ÷ thickness (the folds are drawn, the extra area is counted). Otherwise they bounce back. This gives Fick's law: rate ∝ surface area × concentration difference ÷ thickness (Fick's law is named in neither specification, but both list these factors).
  • Osmosis: the solute particles are too big to cross; water crosses both ways and moves net from where there is more water (dilute) to where there is less (concentrated). The membrane can slide: particles push on it, and a spring (like a stretched cell or the weight of water in a U-tube) pushes back, so the concentrated side swells until the push balances.
  • Active transport: carrier proteins load a particle on the outside only, use one ATP each trip and release it inside, so they move it against the gradient. ATP is made at a rate set by respiration (cyanide stops it). A small leak lets particles diffuse back. Facilitated diffusion (A level): ions cross only through channel proteins; glucose only with carrier proteins, which take a fixed time per trip, so the rate levels off when they are all busy.
  • Quality sets how many particles stand for each concentration (Low half, High 1.6×, Ultra 2.5× Medium) and the drawing detail. More particles: smoother graphs. The batch run always uses Medium.
  • Agar cubes: acid diffuses in and neutralises the alkali as it goes, so the colourless edge moves in as x = √(2 D t × c_acid ÷ c_alkali) (a moving reaction front; D for hydrochloric acid about 3 × 10⁻⁹ m²/s at 20 °C). A cube clears when the edge reaches the centre, half the side in, so time ∝ side² and the part still pink is (1 − 2x/L)³ of the cube. Timings include cutting errors (about ±0.3 mm), judging the colour (about ±4%) and reaction time.
  • Potato cylinders: water moves at a rate ∝ (water potential of the solution − water potential of the cells) × surface area ÷ volume. The cells' solute potential is about −950 kPa (it gets less negative as they swell); the cell walls push back with pressure potential once the cells are full (+250 kPa in fresh tissue). Half the cylinder's mass is water that can move by osmosis; the rest is starch, walls and water outside the cells. Each cylinder differs a little, masses are read to 0.01 g and blotting is never perfect. The sucrose water potentials come from a standard table (about 20 °C, approximate).
  • Beetroot: the red pigment (betalain) is inside the vacuole. Above about 45–50 °C membrane proteins denature and the bilayer becomes too fluid, so pigment leaks out; ethanol dissolves the lipids. Leakage follows smooth S-shaped curves (illustrative, typical of class results); colorimeter readings have about ±0.01 reading error and discs differ by about 8%.
  • Left out: the real number of molecules (a 0.1 mol/dm³ solution has millions of times more water than solute), charge and the membrane potential, aquaporins as separate channels, unstirred layers, the cell's shape in 3D, and changes to the potato's sugar content during the experiment.
