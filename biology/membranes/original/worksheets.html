Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Double the gradient, double the rate?

What this shows. Each run counts the particles crossing the membrane in the first 5 seconds. The rate rises in a straight line through the origin: double the concentration difference, double the rate (Fick's law). The theory line is very slightly below a straight line from the first points because the gradient starts to fall within the 5 seconds. Runs scatter because each particle moves at random; the scatter grows like the square root of the number crossing, so it is relatively smaller at high concentrations.

2 · How much faster when it is warmer?

What this shows. Warmer particles move faster, so more of them reach the membrane each second. The model uses the diffusion coefficient D ∝ T ÷ viscosity of water (Stokes–Einstein) and gives the particles a speed ∝ √D, so the rate about doubles from 0 °C to 60 °C. Most of the rise comes from water getting less viscous, not from the small rise in absolute temperature (273 K → 333 K is only 22%). Means sit on the line; runs scatter by a particle or two per second because crossing is random.

3 · Bigger cubes: how much longer?

What this shows. A student times each cube until the pink disappears. The acid moves in at the same speed in every cube, but the centre of a bigger cube is further from the surface: time ∝ (side ÷ 2)², so the gradient of the log–log graph is 2 (double the side, four times the time). SA:V = 6 ÷ side, so time ∝ 1 ÷ (SA:V)². Small cubes scatter more in relative terms: a 0.3 mm cutting error is a bigger fraction of a small cube, and reaction time matters more when the time is short.

4 · How good is a class's no-change concentration?

What this shows. Each run is a whole class practical: 24 potato cylinders weighed before and after (to 0.01 g), with real variation between cylinders and imperfect blotting. The no-change concentration is read where the line joining the means crosses zero. The estimates scatter by about ±0.02 mol/dm³ around the true value (about 0.26 mol/dm³, ψ ≈ −700 kPa). Try 1 or 10 repeats in the table: the SD shrinks roughly as 1 ÷ √(repeats). The mean of many classes lands very close to the true value; joining points with straight lines can shift a single estimate slightly, because the real curve bends.

5 · Do carrier proteins have a top speed?

What this shows. The cell uses glucose as soon as it arrives, so the inside stays at zero. At low concentrations the rate rises in proportion (like simple diffusion, dashed), but each carrier needs 1.5 s per trip, so the rate levels off towards 6 ÷ 1.5 = 4 per second when every carrier is busy: the carriers are saturated. This is the same shape as an enzyme's rate against substrate concentration. Runs scatter because molecules arrive at the carriers at random.

6 · Beetroot: when do membranes leak?

What this shows. Each run is one tube of beetroot discs read in a colorimeter. Little pigment leaks below about 40 °C (cut cells at the surface release a little). Between about 45 and 65 °C absorbance rises steeply as membrane proteins denature and the bilayer becomes very fluid; above about 70 °C almost all the pigment is out, so the curve levels off. Readings scatter by about 8% because discs differ in size and surface area. The curve is the model's (illustrative), typical of class results.
