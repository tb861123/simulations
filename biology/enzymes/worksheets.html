Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · More substrate: how much faster?

What this shows. Each run fills a box with 12 amylase molecules and a fixed number of substrate molecules (topped up as they are used) and counts products for 10 s. At low concentrations the rate rises almost in proportion; at high concentrations nearly every active site is busy and the rate levels off towards Vmax = 12 × kcat. The yellow line is the Michaelis–Menten equation for well-mixed molecules; the model sits a few per cent below it at high concentrations, because substrate is slightly used up close to busy enzymes. Runs scatter because collisions are random.

2 · A competitive inhibitor

What this shows. The inhibitor has a shape like the substrate and sits in active sites for 0.5 s at a time. At low substrate concentrations it blocks many active sites (well below the grey 'no inhibitor' line); as substrate is added, substrate molecules win more of the collisions and the rate climbs towards the same Vmax. Km rises; Vmax does not change. Runs scatter because collisions are random.

3 · A non-competitive inhibitor

What this shows. This inhibitor binds on the far side of the enzyme and changes the active site while it is there. Adding substrate cannot push it off, so the rate is the same fraction of the 'no inhibitor' rate at every substrate concentration: Vmax falls (here to about a third), Km stays the same. The model sits a little above the theory at high concentrations because inhibitor molecules, like substrate, get slightly used up close to the enzymes. Compare with the competitive inhibitor.

4 · Where is the optimum temperature?

What this shows. Each run measures the mean rate over 1 minute from when the enzyme is added. Up to about 40 °C the rate roughly doubles every 10 °C (Q10 ≈ 2: faster molecules, more energetic collisions). Above about 45 °C the enzymes denature during the minute, so the mean rate collapses: the optimum here is about 45 °C. The grey line is the rate if nothing denatured. Try a 5-minute assay (ta = 300 s): the optimum moves lower, because there is more time to denature. At the steep part, runs scatter a lot: with only 12 enzymes, it matters when each one happens to denature.

5 · Amylase practical: rate against temperature

What this shows. Each run is one timed amylase–iodine test; rate = 1000 ÷ time. The optimum is about 40 °C. Measured rates sit at or below the line because the time is rounded up to the next 30-s drop: at fast rates (short times) that rounding is a big fraction of the time, so the scatter is large and the peak is blunted. Testing every 10 s instead (iv = 10 in the table) gives much better agreement. Above about 50 °C the starch never disappears in 20 minutes (rate 0) as the amylase denatures; at 10 °C some runs do not finish in 20 minutes either, which pulls the mean down.

6 · Catalase: initial rate and Q10

What this shows. Each run is a gas-syringe experiment with five potato discs; the initial rate is the gradient of a tangent at t = 0 (from a curve through the first readings). On the log scale the line is nearly straight: the rate is multiplied by about 1.8 for every 10 °C (Q10 ≈ 1.8, activation energy 45 kJ/mol). Tangents sit a little below the true rate at low temperatures, where small volumes are hard to read to 0.5 cm³ and some oxygen dissolves; the mean rate over 30 s (grey) is lower, especially when warm, because the reaction slows as peroxide is used up (and the enzyme starts to denature). Discs differ by about 8%, so runs scatter.
