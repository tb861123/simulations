Enzymes · Teacher notesenzymes.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Lock and key

What to look for: The substrate fits the active site, forms an enzyme–substrate complex for a moment, and leaves as products (maltose). The enzyme is free again, unchanged, ready for the next one: a catalyst. Only the right-shaped substrate fits: enzymes are specific.

Investigation 2: Temperature

What to look for: Cold: the molecules move slowly, collide less often and react more slowly once bound. Warmer: more frequent, more successful collisions, up to the optimum (about 37 °C for human enzymes). Hotter still: the enzymes denature (grey): the bonds holding their shape break, the active site no longer fits, and the change is permanent. The batch run 'Rate against temperature' draws the whole curve.

Investigation 3: pH

What to look for: Pepsin works best near pH 2 (stomach acid); trypsin near pH 8. Away from the optimum the charges in the active site change, so its shape changes and fewer substrates fit. Moderate changes are reversible here: go back to the right pH and the rate recovers. Extreme pH can denature enzymes permanently.

Investigation 4: More substrate

What to look for: At first more substrate means more collisions and a faster rate. Eventually almost every enzyme is busy all the time: adding more substrate cannot help, because the enzymes are the bottleneck. The rate levels off at its maximum (Vmax). Adding more enzyme would raise it.

Investigation 5: A heat-loving enzyme

What to look for: Its optimum is around 72 °C and it survives brief heating to 95 °C, which would denature human enzymes in seconds. Its shape is held by extra bonds. PCR repeatedly heats DNA to separate the strands, so it needs an enzyme that is not destroyed by the heat.

Investigation 6: Competitive inhibition A level

What to look for: Inhibitors occupy active sites for a while, so fewer are free: the rate falls. But they compete: with lots of substrate, most active sites are found by substrate first, and the rate approaches the same maximum. Competitive inhibition raises Km but not Vmax.

Investigation 7: Non-competitive inhibition A level

What to look for: No. An enzyme with a non-competitive inhibitor attached cannot work however much substrate there is, so the maximum rate itself falls. Vmax goes down; Km is unchanged. Many poisons work like this, as do some drugs; and end-product inhibition uses the same idea to control metabolic pathways.

Investigation 8: Q10 A level

What to look for: About 2: a 10 °C rise roughly doubles the rate, because molecules collide more often and with more energy. In this model the conversion step has Q10 = 2 and collisions also speed up a little, so the measured value is near 2. Above the optimum, Q10 means nothing: denaturing takes over.

Batch experiment 1: Rate against temperature

Rises with temperature up to an optimum, then falls steeply as the enzymes denature. The optimum depends on how long you measure for: in a longer assay the enzymes have longer to denature, so the optimum appears lower.
