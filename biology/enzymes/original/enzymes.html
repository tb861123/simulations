How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Molecules: each moves in a straight line, turning a little at random, and bounces off the walls. Speed rises with temperature as √D, with D ∝ T ÷ viscosity of water. Time is slowed right down: real enzymes turn over thousands to millions of molecules a second (catalase about 10⁷).
  • Binding: when a substrate molecule moves into an enzyme's active site (a small circle in the notch) it binds, if the active site has the right shape. pH sets that chance (the active site's charged groups, a bell-shaped curve between two pK values); salivary amylase also needs chloride ions as a cofactor (A level). Molecules of the wrong shape never bind (specificity).
  • Turnover: an enzyme–substrate complex releases products with a chance kcat × Δt each step; kcat rises with temperature (Arrhenius, activation energy 45–50 kJ/mol, so Q10 ≈ 1.8–2.0 near room temperature).
  • Denaturation: each enzyme can lose its shape for good at random; the chance rises very steeply above about 40–50 °C (250 kJ/mol). So the "optimum temperature" depends on how long you measure for. Extremes of pH here only switch the enzyme off (reversibly); really they can denature it too.
  • Theory lines: for well-mixed molecules, each free enzyme catches substrate at f × 2 × r × v × n a second, which gives the Michaelis–Menten equation rate = Vmax [S] ÷ (Km + [S]). Substrate gets slightly used up close to busy enzymes, so measured rates sit a few per cent below the theory (more when enzymes are fast).
  • Inhibitors (A level): a competitive inhibitor sits in the active site for 0.5 s on average; a non-competitive inhibitor (or, for end-product inhibition, a product molecule) sits on the far side of the enzyme and stops it working while it is there. Both are reversible here.
  • Amylase practical: starch is broken down at a Michaelis–Menten rate with the same temperature, pH and denaturation rules; iodine is orange-brown below about 0.02% starch. The recorded time is the first drop that stays orange, so it is rounded up to the sampling interval. Water baths are ±0.5 °C; enzyme solutions differ by about ±5%.
  • Catalase practical: rate = k × discs × activity × [H₂O₂] ÷ (Km + [H₂O₂]), Km ≈ 80 mmol/dm³ (approximate: published values vary a lot). 1 mol of H₂O₂ gives 12 000 cm³ of oxygen. Discs differ by about ±8%, the syringe reads to 0.5 cm³, the bung goes in up to 1 s late and a little oxygen dissolves at first.
  • Quality only changes the picture (water molecules, bubbles, detail); the model and the batch runs are the same at every level.
  • Left out: the real 3D shapes, how the transition state is stabilised, substrate inhibition, enzyme concentrations far below substrate, product stopping the reaction going backwards (reversibility), and enzymes made as inactive precursors.
