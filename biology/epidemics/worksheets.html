Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Patient zero

What to look for: Inside a home it spreads quickly to the rest of the household, but a household is small. It reaches new households through the places where people from different homes mix: classes, workplaces, shops and cafés. Those links are what turn a few cases into an epidemic. The readings show where infections happened.

Investigation 2: What R0 means

What to look for: R0 is the average number of people each case infects when nobody is immune. Flu at 1.5 spreads slowly and many people never catch it. Measles at 15 tears through almost everyone in a few weeks. The epidemic turns over when enough people are immune that each case infects fewer than one other (R falls below 1), not when everyone has had it.

Investigation 3: Vaccination and herd immunity

What to look for: At 40% there is still an epidemic, though smaller. At about 70% it fizzles: each case meets so many immune people that it infects fewer than one other, so chains of infection die out. The unvaccinated are protected by the people around them. That is herd immunity, and the threshold is 1 − 1/R0 (67% for R0 = 3; about 93% for measles).

Investigation 4: Flatten the curve

What to look for: Cutting contacts lowers R. The peak is much lower and later, so fewer people need a bed at the same moment and the hospital may cope. The total infected falls too, but by less. If the measures are lifted while many are still susceptible, the epidemic takes off again: try switching distancing off part-way through.

Investigation 5: Close the schools?

What to look for: Classes link many households, so closing schools removes a major route and lowers the peak. But children still catch it at home and in shops, so it does not stop the outbreak alone. Real decisions weigh this against the harm of lost schooling, and it matters how much children actually spread the particular disease.

Investigation 6: Isolate the ill

What to look for: With the Ebola-like disease people are only infectious once they have symptoms, and everyone gets symptoms, so isolating the ill catches nearly all transmission. The COVID-like disease spreads for a day or two before symptoms start, and a third of cases never have symptoms: the 'silent' spreaders are missed. This is why COVID-19 needed testing and tracing, and Ebola outbreaks can be stopped by finding and isolating cases.

Investigation 7: Town or textbook?

What to look for: With equal mixing the model follows the equations closely. The town does not: the disease races through a household or a class, where people spend hours together, so the early rise is faster and comes in bursts as it reaches each new group. Real epidemics are lumpy like this, which is one reason forecasts based on simple equations go wrong.

Investigation 8: Luck

What to look for: Often it does not. With R0 = 1.5 the first case infects 0, 1, 2 or 3 people by chance, and if the first few happen to infect nobody the chain dies out. Simple theory says an outbreak with R0 = 1.5 takes off only about 6 times in 10. Many real introductions of a disease fizzle like this and are never noticed.
