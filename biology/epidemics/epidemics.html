How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • People: each person is S, E, I, R (or V) or D. Latent and infectious periods vary from person to person (gamma distribution, shape 3: most people are close to the average).
  • Contacts: an infectious person makes on average 10 contacts a day (random, Poisson). Each contact with a susceptible person infects them with chance τ = R0 ÷ (10 × infectious period). So in a fully susceptible population each case infects R0 others on average.
  • Mixing: "random" means any two people are equally likely to meet (the assumption behind the SEIR equations). "Local" means people only meet those within a short distance as they wander about. "Households…" puts people in homes of 1–5, school or work groups of about 25 and the wider community: 30% of contacts are at home, 40% at school/work and 30% in the community. Clustered contacts waste infections on people who already have it, so spread is slower than the equations predict.
  • Vaccination: "all-or-nothing": with 90% efficacy, 90% of vaccinated people are fully immune and 10% are not protected at all.
  • Distancing cuts contacts outside the home by the percentage shown. Masks cut the chance of passing it on in each contact. Isolation: people with symptoms who isolate have 90% fewer contacts from when symptoms start. Diseases that spread before symptoms (flu, COVID) or with no symptoms are hard to stop this way; Ebola and SARS spread mainly after symptoms.
  • Effective R: R at the start = R0 × (1 − distancing) × (1 − mask effect) × (isolation factor) × (share not immune). Rt (measured) is the average number of people infected by those who caught it on day t.
  • Theory: the dashed lines solve the SEIR equations for the same settings. The final size equation z = 1 − e−R z gives the share infected in a big outbreak (starting with some immune: z = s0(1 − e−R z)). The chance that one case starts a big outbreak is 1 − 1/R0 if illness lengths are very variable (exponential); with this model's more regular illness lengths it is a little higher.
  • Deaths happen at the end of the infectious period, with the chosen chance.
  • Not modelled: age groups, hospitals running out of beds, people changing behaviour by themselves, seasons, travel in and out, new variants, partial (leaky) vaccine protection, births and other deaths.
