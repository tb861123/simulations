Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Rediscover Tokyo's railways

What to look for: The mould's network is longer than the tree (the readout shows about 1.3–1.6 × its length) but it has loops, so most towns have two routes in: cut one tube and the towns stay joined. Real railways make the same trade: a little more track for shorter journeys and backup routes. The bays are walls, as bright light kept the real mould off the 'sea'.

Investigation 2: Where do three roads meet?

What to look for: Usually the mould keeps all three sides, a loop, which is longer but survives a cut anywhere. Watch for a side thinning and the tubes pulling inwards towards a junction; harsher settings (life without food 0.5–0.75) push it that way. The truly shortest network meets at the Steiner point, where three roads meet at 120° (13% shorter than two sides). Soap films find it exactly; the mould rarely does, which says something about what it is really optimising.

Investigation 3: Cut a vein

What to look for: The cut tube's ends fade. If the network had a loop, traffic simply switches to the other route at once. If not, the mould has to explore: shrinking means it has few spare agents, so press 'Grow again' to send new mould out from the food, and a new tube forms round the wall.

Investigation 4: Sensor angle: tangles or highways?

What to look for: Narrow sensors (10°) make agents follow trails very straight: long, smooth tubes but they miss food off to the side. Around 22–45° they find and join most of the food. At 90° the agents cannot follow a trail at all and the network breaks into clumps. The batch run measures it.

Investigation 5: Starve it or feed it

What to look for: Harsh: a lean tree-like network, but some food gets cut off. Never dies: a dense mesh over the whole dish, joining everything with near-straight routes but hugely long. Real Physarum, and real transport networks, sit in between: cost against efficiency against fault tolerance.

Investigation 6: No food at all

What to look for: Without food nothing dies, and the agents form a 'reticulated' network of polygonal cells that slowly merge and coarsen. Longer sensors make bigger cells; a turn angle smaller than the sensor angle makes the network unstable and wandering. Patterns like these grow when real Physarum spreads over an empty dish.

Investigation 7: England's motorways?

What to look for: London–Birmingham–Manchester–Leeds usually appear, a little like the M1/M6/M62 spine, and Bristol–Cardiff like the M4. Differences come from what the model leaves out: hills, rivers, the sea between Wales and Somerset (no walls here), and the fact that real roads were built where people and money were, not just where cities are.

Batch experiment 1: Sensor angle and joining up

Each run starts from a different random scatter of agents, so the network differs. Very narrow sensors make long straight tubes that miss food; very wide ones cannot follow a trail. Somewhere around 20–45° joins the most food.
Result from a test run: 10: 46.7 ± 13.4 · 22.5: 79.7 ± 3.49 · 45: 89.3 ± 3.57 · 67.5: 40.4 ± 2.3 · 90: 9.07 ± 0.275 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: How long a network?

Short lives give a lean network, sometimes shorter than the tree because some food is not joined at all. Long lives keep a mesh of loops over much of the dish, several times the tree's length. The spanning tree is not the true minimum (a Steiner tree, with extra junctions, is up to 13% shorter), but it is easy to work out.
Theory (minimum spanning tree): 0.5: 1 · 0.75: 1 · 1: 1 · 1.5: 1 · 2: 1
Result from a test run: 0.5: 0.663 ± 0.0262 · 0.75: 1.24 ± 0.0858 · 1: 1.59 ± 0.0512 · 1.5: 3.94 ± 0.0937 · 2: 4.97 ± 0.0296 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: How direct are the routes?

Only joined pairs count, so harsh settings can look direct simply because only nearby pairs are joined. Richer networks have more loops and short-cuts, so routes get closer to the straight line: the cost–efficiency trade-off Tero and colleagues measured for real Physarum and the Tokyo railway (about 1.1–1.2 for both).
Theory (straight line): 0.5: 1 · 0.75: 1 · 1: 1 · 1.5: 1 · 2: 1
Result from a test run: 0.5: 1.09 ± 0.0108 · 0.75: 1.23 ± 0.0448 · 1: 1.21 ± 0.0281 · 1.5: 1.11 ± 0.00589 · 2: 1.11 ± 0.0013 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Trail decay

A slow-fading trail spreads further and keeps more tubes and loops alive; a fast-fading one keeps only the busiest tubes, so the network is shorter at high decay (about 1.6 × the tree up to 10% decay, about 1.25 × at 20–30%).
Theory (minimum spanning tree): 0.03: 1 · 0.05: 1 · 0.1: 1 · 0.2: 1 · 0.3: 1
Result from a test run: 0.03: 1.57 ± 0.115 · 0.05: 1.7 ± 0.0772 · 0.1: 1.59 ± 0.0512 · 0.2: 1.23 ± 0.0265 · 0.3: 1.31 ± 0.12 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Tokyo, again and again

Every run differs in detail but the length lands in a narrow band, a little above the tree: like Tero's real moulds, which made networks about 1.4–1.8 times the spanning tree, close to the real railway. This model, with its settings on load, comes out at about 1.3.

Result from a test run: mean 1.27, SD 0.0522, SE 0.0117 from 20 runs
