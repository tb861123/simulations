Murmuration · Teacher notesmurmuration.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Order from nothing

What to look for: Within a few seconds small groups agree locally, then merge. Nobody decided the direction: it grew from small chance agreements that spread, and a different one wins each time you scatter. A big flock often ends up wheeling like a whirlpool, so 'flying the same way' stays well below 100% even though it is clearly one flock; try 200 birds to see near-perfect alignment.

Investigation 2: Which rule does what?

What to look for: No alignment: a milling swarm, like midges, that stays together but goes nowhere. No cohesion: aligned streams that drift apart. No separation: birds pile into impossible dense clumps. A murmuration needs all three.

Investigation 3: How many neighbours?

What to look for: With one neighbour, birds pair off and the flock shatters into many small groups. With two or three it mostly holds; from about four it reliably stays as one. Tracking more adds little here, so a handful is enough. Real starlings use six or seven, which may give a safety margin in turbulent air and under attack.

Investigation 4: The falcon

What to look for: Only birds within 30 m react to the falcon itself. The rest react to their neighbours turning, so a wave of turning sweeps across the flock faster than any bird flies. Where the wave passes, birds bank together and bunch up, which shows as a dark band. The flock splits around the falcon and re-forms behind it.

Investigation 5: Safety in numbers?

What to look for: The falcon catches about the same number per minute either way, because it can only chase one bird at a time. In the big flock that loss is shared among ten times as many birds, so each bird's risk is roughly ten times lower: the 'dilution effect'. The batch run measures it properly.

Investigation 6: Too much wobble

What to look for: In a small flock (try 200 birds) order hangs on for a long while and then collapses quite suddenly: a transition a little like melting. Below the critical wobble, alignment with neighbours corrects errors faster than they build up; above it, it cannot.

Investigation 7: One bird's view

What to look for: It knows nothing beyond its seven neighbours, a few metres away. The flock's shape exists only at the level of the whole. That is what 'emergent' means: a pattern of the group that no individual holds or controls.

Batch experiment 1: Order against wobble

At low wobble every run ends up well aligned. At high wobble none does. In between, runs disagree with one another most strongly: large scatter near a transition is typical of this kind of collapse.
Result from a test run: 0: 0.904 ± 0.0608 · 1: 0.811 ± 0.106 · 2: 0.976 ± 0.00741 · 3: 0.884 ± 0.0507 · 4: 0.914 ± 0.00888 · 5: 0.836 ± 0.0217 · 6: 0.693 ± 0.0441 · 7: 0.601 ± 0.0353 · 8: 0.484 ± 0.0366 · 9: 0.321 ± 0.0355 · 10: 0.23 ± 0.0222 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: How many neighbours are enough?

Chance decides how the scattered birds first pair up, so runs differ. With k = 1 the flock stays in fragments; a few neighbours are enough to join it into one. Field measurements on real starlings found 6 to 7: more than the bare minimum this model needs.
Result from a test run: 1: 17.2 ± 1.11 · 2: 1.33 ± 0.333 · 3: 1.33 ± 0.333 · 4: 1.17 ± 0.167 · 5: 1.67 ± 0.333 · 6: 1.5 ± 0.5 · 7: 1.33 ± 0.211 · 9: 1.17 ± 0.167 · 12: 1 ± 0 · 15: 1.17 ± 0.167 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Safety in numbers

Catches are rare events, so single runs vary a lot (0, 1 or 2 birds). The falcon takes roughly the same number of birds whatever the flock size, so the risk per bird falls steeply as the flock grows: about halving each time the flock doubles.
Result from a test run: 25: 8.89 ± 1.78 · 50: 5.33 ± 0.974 · 100: 2.67 ± 0.77 · 200: 1.22 ± 0.318 · 400: 0.611 ± 0.102 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Time to agree

Sometimes the first local groups happen to agree and the flock lines up in a few seconds; sometimes two large groups form heading opposite ways and must meet and merge first. Hence the long tail.
Result from a test run: mean 6.24, SD 4.78, SE 0.535 from 80 runs
