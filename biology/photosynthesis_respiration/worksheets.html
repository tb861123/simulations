Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · The inverse square law

What this shows. Each run counts the bubbles from a fresh piece of pondweed for one minute. Far from the lamp, light is the limiting factor and the count follows the inverse square law (grey dashed line: rate ∝ 1/d², minus respiration). Close to the lamp the count levels off below that line: carbon dioxide (1% sodium hydrogencarbonate) now limits the rate, so more light does not help. Beyond about 40 cm photosynthesis barely beats respiration and there are almost no bubbles. Counts scatter because bubbles come out at random and pieces of pondweed differ.

3 · Temperature and rate

What this shows. In bright light with plenty of CO₂, temperature limits the rate: it roughly doubles every 10 °C up to an optimum of about 30–35 °C (enzymes such as rubisco), then falls steeply as the enzymes denature. Try the same sweep at 40 cm: in dim light, temperature hardly matters, because the light-dependent reactions limit the rate.

4 · How much do counts scatter?

What this shows. The same set-up 200 times. Bubbles come out at random, so even a perfect plant gives counts that scatter by about √(mean); different pieces of pondweed add about ±10%. The histogram matches the predicted spread (blue curve). This is why each count is repeated and a mean is taken: the standard error of a mean of n counts is SD ÷ √n.

5 · The compensation point

What this shows. A gas analyser measures a leaf's net CO₂ uptake. In the dark the leaf only respires (uptake negative). As light increases, photosynthesis rises until it equals respiration: the net uptake crosses zero at the light compensation point, about 1100 lux here at 20 °C. Repeat at 30 °C (TL2 = 30): respiration is faster, so the compensation point moves up to about 2200 lux.

6 · Respirometer: temperature and Q10

What this shows. Each run is a 10-minute respirometer reading with soda lime and a control tube; rate = gradient × πr² ÷ mass. The rate roughly doubles every 10 °C (Q10 ≈ 2) because respiration is controlled by enzymes, then falls above about 40 °C as they denature (Q10 from the table: rate at 30 °C ÷ rate at 20 °C ≈ 2). Runs scatter because seeds differ (±8%), the drop can only be read to ±0.5 mm and the water bath temperature wanders, which the control tube only partly cancels.

7 · RQ of sunflower seeds (lipid)

What this shows. Each RQ comes from two 10-minute runs with the same seeds: with soda lime (O₂ used) and with water (O₂ used − CO₂ made). The mean is close to 0.7, the RQ of lipid. Single RQs scatter by a few hundredths: the water run moves much less than the soda-lime run, so reading errors, temperature drift the control does not cancel, and seeds respiring a little differently on the second run all matter more. Change the organisms to germinating peas (RQ 1.0): the water run hardly moves at all.
