Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Light and bubbles

What to look for: Light intensity follows the inverse square law: double the distance and the light falls to a quarter. At 20 cm the count is about a third of the 10 cm count (not a quarter), because close to the lamp CO₂ is already limiting. At 30 cm it is small: light is the limiting factor there. The graph of rate against 1/d² is straight at low light and levels off at high light.

Investigation 2: What is limiting?

What to look for: Close to the lamp, light is no longer limiting: CO₂ is. Adding sodium hydrogencarbonate raises the plateau; then temperature (the enzymes) may limit. The limiting factor is whichever is in shortest supply; improving anything else makes no difference. Growers add CO₂ and heat to greenhouses for the same reason.

Investigation 3: A fair test?

What to look for: A filament bulb gives out a lot of heat, which warms the water and the plant's enzymes. The temperature is no longer controlled, so you cannot tell which variable caused the change. Use an LED, or put a beaker of water (heat shield) between lamp and tube, and keep the tube in a water bath.

Investigation 4: Day and night in a leaf

What to look for: In bright light photosynthesis is faster than respiration, so the leaf takes in CO₂ and gives out O₂. At night only respiration happens: it takes in O₂ and gives out CO₂. Respiration goes on all the time, day and night. Around dawn and dusk the two balance: the compensation point.

Investigation 5: Indicator tubes

What to look for: Indicator is red at normal CO₂, yellow when CO₂ rises and purple when it falls. Dark: respiration only, CO₂ rises: yellow. Full light: photosynthesis is faster than respiration, CO₂ falls: purple. Gauze: about balanced, stays red (near the compensation point). No leaf: the control, stays red.

Investigation 6: Respiring seeds

What to look for: The peas take in oxygen; the CO₂ they give out is absorbed by the soda lime. So the volume of gas falls and the drop is pulled in. Volume = πr² × distance: with a 1 mm bore, r = 0.5 mm and every mm the drop moves is 0.785 mm³. The control tube of glass beads shows changes caused by temperature or pressure, not by living things.

Investigation 7: Temperature and respiration

What to look for: Respiration is controlled by enzymes, so the rate roughly doubles every 10 °C (Q10 ≈ 2) up to about 40 °C, then falls as the enzymes denature. Boiled peas are dead: their enzymes have been denatured, so they do not respire. They make a better control than nothing at all.

Investigation 8: The compensation point A level

What to look for: About 1100 lux at 20 °C: photosynthesis then exactly uses the CO₂ that respiration makes. Warmer leaves respire faster, so they need more light to break even: the compensation point rises. A plant must spend enough time above it each day to grow.

Investigation 9: Light off: the Calvin cycle A level

What to look for: In the dark the light-dependent stage stops, so there is no ATP or reduced NADP. GP cannot be reduced to TP, so GP piles up; RuBP cannot be regenerated from TP, but rubisco keeps using it up, so RuBP falls. TP falls too. Classic experiments (Calvin and Benson) found exactly this.

Investigation 10: Less CO₂: RuBP piles up A level

What to look for: Without CO₂, rubisco cannot fix it onto RuBP. RuBP is still made from TP, so it builds up; GP is still turned into TP but no new GP is made, so GP falls. The opposite of switching the light off.

Investigation 11: Respiratory quotient A level

What to look for: With soda lime the drop measures O₂ used. With water it measures O₂ used − CO₂ made. So CO₂ = soda − water, and RQ = (soda − water) ÷ soda. Sunflower seeds give about 0.7: they store oil, a lipid. Carbohydrate gives 1.0, protein about 0.9.
