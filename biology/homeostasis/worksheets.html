Homeostasis · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Glucose tolerance test: type 2

What this shows. Each run is a new person with type 2 diabetes (insulin secretion and sensitivity each differ by about ±15%) given 75 g of glucose; the 2-hour reading is taken with a glucose meter. The mean is very close to the average person's value, well above the 11.1 mmol/L threshold, and the SD (about 1.4 mmol/L) comes from people differing (the meter adds only a few tenths). The standard error of the mean is under 0.1 mmol/L, so the mean is known far more precisely than any one person's result. Change Person to 'no diabetes': readings are around 5–6 mmol/L; try 'type 1' too.

2 · Bigger meals, higher peaks

What this shows. The highest glucose of the day rises only slowly with the size of the meals in someone without diabetes: more glucose absorbed means more insulin, so negative feedback keeps the peak below about 8 mmol/L. The grey dashed line is someone with untreated type 2 diabetes, whose peak rises steeply with carbohydrate. Runs scatter because people differ in how much insulin they make and how well they respond to it.

3 · Insulin dose and hypos

What this shows. Each run is a day for someone with type 1 diabetes who goes for a run in the afternoon. With little rapid-acting insulin the lowest glucose stays above 4 mmol/L (but the highest is far too high); as the dose rises the lowest value falls, and from about 2 units per 10 g (1.5 for some people) it drops below 3.5 mmol/L during exercise: hypoglycaemia (the injected insulin cannot be switched off, and in type 1 the glucagon response is weak). The scatter is larger at high doses because people differ in their sensitivity to insulin: the same dose can be fine for one person and cause a hypo in another, which is why doses are set for each person.

4 · Keeping 37 °C in any weather

What this shows. With the hypothalamus controlling sweating, shivering and skin blood flow, the core stays within a few tenths of a degree of 37 °C from −5 to 45 °C (pink: each run; gold: the average person). The grey dashed line is the same body with no control: its temperature follows the air. That is negative feedback working. Try humidity 90%: above about 35 °C the sweat cannot evaporate and the core rises.

5 · Drinking and urine: ADH

What this shows. With no drink the kidney makes just under 1 mL a minute (about 140 mL in 3 hours). After a large drink the blood becomes dilute, ADH falls and a lot of dilute urine is made: about two-thirds of a litre drink is passed within 3 hours (best-fit gradient about 0.67 mL per mL drunk; the rest follows later, and very large drinks hit the kidney's maximum flow). Grey: someone whose kidneys do not respond to ADH (diabetes insipidus) makes lots of urine whatever they drink. Runs scatter because people have different amounts of body water.

6 · The renal threshold for glucose

What this shows. Below about 10 mmol/L all filtered glucose is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule, so none reaches the urine; above it the carrier proteins are saturated (a maximum rate of reabsorption) and the extra is lost in urine. The gold line has a sharp corner at 10, but the means curve smoothly round it because each person (and each nephron) has a slightly different threshold ('splay'). This is why glucose in urine suggests diabetes.

7 · How good is the colorimeter? (PAG5)

What this shows. Each run makes a new calibration line from six standards (0–10 mmol/L), each absorbance read to about ±0.015, then reads one unknown. The estimates centre on the true value (6 mmol/L) with an SD of about 0.27 mmol/L, which matches the theory for reading a value off a fitted line (blue curve). Repeating the unknown reading and taking a mean, or using more standards, makes the estimate more precise; it is least precise at the ends of the line.

8 · Kidney failure: blood urea

What this shows. Urea is made at the same rate however well the kidneys work, so it builds up until removal (∝ GFR × urea concentration) balances production: steady urea ∝ 1 ÷ GFR. On log–log axes this is a straight line of gradient −1. At 10% of normal function urea is about 10 times its normal level (uraemia), which is when dialysis or a transplant is needed. Runs scatter by about 10% because protein intake (and so urea production) varies from day to day.
