Homeostasis · Teacher noteshomeostasis.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: A day of meals

What to look for: It rises to about 7–8 mmol/L after each meal and returns to about 5 within two hours. Beta cells detect the rise and release insulin; liver and muscle cells take up glucose and the liver stores it as glycogen. That is negative feedback.

Investigation 2: Type 1 diabetes

What to look for: No insulin is made, so glucose climbs above 15–20 mmol/L and spills into the urine. Injecting insulin before meals (matched to the carbohydrate) brings it back into a safer range: treatment, not a cure.

Investigation 3: Type 2 diabetes

What to look for: Cells respond poorly to insulin (insulin resistance), so glucose stays high for longer. Exercise uses glucose and improves sensitivity; metformin reduces glucose release by the liver. Diet and weight loss are the first treatment.

Investigation 4: Glucose tolerance test

What to look for: No diabetes: under 7.8 mmol/L at 2 hours. Type 2: often above 11.1. Type 1 untreated: very high. The test shows how well the body brings glucose back down.

Investigation 5: A hot day

What to look for: Sweat glands release sweat, which cools the skin as it evaporates; arterioles to the skin dilate (vasodilation), so more blood flows near the surface and loses heat. In humid air sweat evaporates less, so it works less well.

Investigation 6: Out in the cold

What to look for: Shivering (muscles contract, releasing heat), vasoconstriction (less blood to the skin, so less heat lost), hairs stand up. With no control, the core temperature keeps falling: hypothermia.

Investigation 7: Drinking and ADH

What to look for: The blood becomes more dilute, so the pituitary releases less ADH; the collecting ducts become less permeable, less water is reabsorbed, and lots of dilute urine is made. On a hot day sweating concentrates the blood, ADH rises and a little concentrated urine is made.

Investigation 8: Glucose in the urine

What to look for: All glucose is normally reabsorbed in the proximal tubule, but the carriers can only take up so much. Above about 10 mmol/L (the renal threshold) some is left in the urine: a sign of untreated diabetes.

Investigation 9: Endotherm and ectotherm A level

What to look for: Mammals (endotherms) make heat internally and keep their core temperature constant over a wide range of air temperatures. The lizard (an ectotherm) depends on outside heat and controls its temperature by behaviour: basking in the sun, moving to the shade.

Investigation 10: Positive feedback A level

What to look for: Positive feedback increases the change instead of reversing it, so it runs away. It is useful only for things that must finish quickly: oxytocin in childbirth, blood clotting, the action potential (Na⁺ channels). Homeostasis uses negative feedback.

Batch experiment 1: Glucose tolerance test

People without diabetes are back near normal; type 2 is usually above 11.1 mmol/L; type 1 untreated is very high.
Result from a test run: 1: 4.91 ± 0.0707 · 2: 10.3 ± 0.205 · 3: 18.6 ± 0.0736 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Insulin sensitivity and the peak after a meal

The less sensitive the cells, the higher and longer the peak: insulin resistance is the start of type 2 diabetes.
Result from a test run: 0.2: 7.94 ± 0.159 · 0.4: 7.11 ± 0.129 · 0.6: 6.64 ± 0.0866 · 0.8: 6.41 ± 0.105 · 1: 6.4 ± 0.14 · 1.2: 6.32 ± 0.14 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Sweating and air temperature

Walking makes enough heat that some sweating is needed even in cool air; it doubles by 40 °C, because the hotter the air, the less heat the skin can lose by radiation and convection.

Batch experiment 5: Drinking and urine

More water drunk → more dilute blood → less ADH → more urine. The kidneys get rid of the extra water within a couple of hours.
