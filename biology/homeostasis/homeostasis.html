How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Blood glucose (mmol/L) is one well-mixed pool of about 14 L. Food passes from the stomach (at up to 1.0 g of carbohydrate a minute for sugar, 0.6 for a mixed meal, 0.35 for starchy high-fibre food) into the small intestine and is absorbed. The brain uses glucose all the time without insulin; muscle and fat take it up faster with more insulin; the liver stores it as glycogen (up to about 100 g) when insulin is high and releases it (glycogenolysis), and makes new glucose (gluconeogenesis), when glucagon is high. Above about 10 mmol/L the kidney cannot reabsorb it all and glucose appears in urine.
  • Hormones: insulin (beta cells) rises steeply once glucose is above about 5 mmol/L, is boosted a little while food is absorbed (gut hormones) and has a half-life of about 6 minutes. Glucagon (alpha cells) rises when glucose falls below about 4.6 mmol/L and is suppressed by insulin. Exercise adds adrenaline-like extra release from the liver and glucose uptake by working muscle that needs no insulin.
  • Diabetes: type 1: almost no insulin is made (the beta cells have been destroyed by the immune system). Type 2: the beta cells make about 40% as much, cells respond to insulin about 30% as well (insulin resistance) and the liver releases more glucose. Injected insulin goes into a store under the skin and reaches the blood over 1–3 hours (rapid-acting, dosed by counting carbohydrate) or steadily (long-acting). Metformin is modelled as less glucose release by the liver and a little better insulin sensitivity. Stem-cell beta cells (A level, still experimental) restore about 70% of normal insulin secretion.
  • Glucose tolerance test (clinical enrichment, not named in 4BI1 or H420): 75 g glucose after an overnight fast; the glucose meter reads to about ±(0.2 mmol/L + 4%). Diagnosis (WHO/NICE): fasting ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or 2-hour ≥ 11.1 mmol/L means diabetes; a 2-hour value of 7.8–11.0 is impaired glucose tolerance. Each "new trial" is a new person whose insulin sensitivity and secretion differ by about ±15%.
  • Temperature: a core (45 kg) and a skin shell (15 kg). Heat is made by metabolism (80 W resting) and shivering (up to 250 W extra); blood carries it to the skin (10 W/°C with the skin arterioles constricted, up to 90 W/°C dilated); the skin loses it to the air (convection and radiation through clothing) and by evaporating sweat (2.4 kJ per gram; humid air evaporates less). The hypothalamus compares the core with 37.0 °C and senses the skin. Tick off "control working" to see what happens without negative feedback. The data logger reads every minute with probes good to about ±0.1 °C.
  • The lizard (A level): an ectotherm whose body temperature moves towards that of its surroundings (time constant 10 minutes), plus about 9 °C in full sun. It moves into the sun below 32 °C and into the shade above 36 °C (behaviour). It cannot cool below the air temperature.
  • The kidney: 125 mL of filtrate a minute (180 L a day). The solute to be excreted (about 0.45 mOsm a minute) sets the urine volume: urine flow = solute ÷ urine concentration. ADH rises once the blood's solute concentration is above about 283 mOsm/kg and lets the collecting duct reabsorb water, so urine concentration goes from about 60 (no ADH) to about 1200 mOsm/kg (maximum ADH). Drinks are absorbed over about 20 minutes; you lose about 0.6 mL a minute through breath and skin and 10 mL a minute sweating in the heat. Urea (about 400 mmol a day) is cleared at about half the filtration rate; dialysis cleans the blood for 4 hours every other day.
  • Along the nephron (A level): the proximal convoluted tubule reabsorbs about 65% of the water and salts and all the glucose (if blood glucose is below the threshold); the loop of Henle makes the medulla salty (up to about 1200 mOsm/kg) by pumping ions out of the ascending limb, which is impermeable to water (the countercurrent multiplier: a name beyond the H420 wording); the collecting duct reabsorbs water by osmosis into the salty medulla when ADH has added aquaporins. Values are typical textbook figures, simplified.
  • Urine tests: Benedict's colour bands (blue → green → yellow → orange → brick red) are semi-quantitative. The colorimeter (PAG5) uses a straight calibration line: absorbance of the blue that is left = 0.85 − 0.06 × glucose (mmol/L) (an illustrative line), read to about ±0.015. Pregnancy tests use monoclonal antibodies that bind hCG.
  • Beta cell and cAMP (A level): smooth switch-like curves, not measured values. ATP closes K⁺ channels between about 5 and 8 mmol/L glucose, the membrane depolarises from −70 mV, Ca²⁺ channels open and insulin is secreted. In the liver cell each step of the adrenaline → cAMP → protein kinase → phosphorylase cascade multiplies the signal; the numbers show the idea of amplification, not real molecule counts.
  • Quality only changes the picture (particles and small details); the models and the batch runs are the same at every level.
  • Left out: the circadian rhythm of hormones, cortisol and growth hormone, fat and protein metabolism (ketones in untreated type 1 diabetes are only mentioned), how fast glucose moves between blood and tissue fluid, sodium and potassium balance and aldosterone, the thirst mechanism, the renin–angiotensin system, the countercurrent heat exchange in limbs, fever (a raised set point), sweat salts and dehydration over long times.
