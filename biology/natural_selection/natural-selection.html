How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Genes: each animal has two alleles of each gene. Moths, beetles, guppies, snails: one colour gene. Hares: a winter-coat gene and a running-speed gene. Finches: beak depth is controlled by 6 genes, each "+" allele adding 0.4 mm, plus random variation from the environment (SD 0.45 mm), so it varies smoothly.
  • Dominance: dark moths (C), green or brown beetles, white winter coats, bright guppies and unbanded snails are dominant (as in the real animals). You can switch to "no dominance": then heterozygotes are halfway between.
  • Hunting by sight: how easy an animal is to spot depends on the colour difference between it and the background: 0.1 (perfectly camouflaged) up to 1 (stands out). A patchy background counts each patch by its share of the area. For snails, a striped shell matches stripy grass but not plain leaf litter.
  • Chasers (foxes, lynx) use smell as well as sight, so camouflage helps less; fast hares (FF) are caught 45% as often as slow ones (ff); Ff hares 70%.
  • Hunting risk h = predators × 0.07 × (how easy to spot) × (how easy to catch). Chance of escaping all season = e−h. Each animal's risk is independent of how many prey there are (in reality predators eat more when prey are common).
  • Snow: snow covers a share of the ground that depends on mean winter temperature: all of it at −7 °C or colder, none at +1 °C or warmer. Hares moult at a fixed time of year, so in a warm winter white hares sit on bare brown ground.
  • Heat (snails): above 18 °C, dark shells absorb more sunlight and more snails die of overheating.
  • Drought (finches): in a drought only large, hard seeds are left. A finch with a beak 10.8 mm deep has a 50% chance of cracking enough of them; the chance rises steadily with beak depth (an S-shaped curve), and 5% find enough small seeds anyway. In a wet year every finch has a 95% chance of surviving.
  • Breeding: survivors pair at random (mothers and fathers picked at random, so some have many babies and some none). Each baby gets one allele from each parent at random. With sexual selection on, bright guppy males are picked as fathers 1.5× or 3× as often. Each surviving adult can raise up to 3 young, up to the population limit.
  • Generations do not overlap: parents breed once and die. Moths, beetles and snails really are close to this; hares, finches and guppies are not.
  • Theory: the dashed line is the deterministic selection equation (infinite population, random mating, Hardy–Weinberg proportions): p′ = (p²wAA + pq·wAa)/w̄. With no dominance this is p′ = p(1+s)/(1+ps). For finches the breeder's equation R = h²S is used. Real runs wander around it because of genetic drift (chance), which is strongest in small populations.
  • Not modelled: migration between populations, linkage between genes, learning by predators (search images), predators choosing the commonest type, differences in fertility, age structure, and the costs of being fast or of a thick beak.
