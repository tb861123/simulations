How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Energy: sunlight on the UK is about 3.6 million kJ per m² a year (approximate). Producers fix 0.5–3% of it (GPP) and use half in respiration (NPP = GPP − R). At each step a chosen fraction (the transfer efficiency) becomes new biomass in the next level; of the rest, half is not eaten (it goes to decomposers when it dies), and of what is eaten 30% is egested in faeces; the remainder is released by respiration as heat. Real efficiencies vary (about 2–20%) and are lower for mammals and birds, which use energy keeping warm.
  • Food web: removing a species is followed qualitatively: its prey lose a predator, and feeders that ate only it lose their food. Real webs are larger and numbers change over time (see Predator and Prey).
  • Nutrient cycles: each dot is a packet of atoms. Every time step each packet moves along one arrow out of its store at random, with a chance set by that process's rate (rates per month for nitrogen, per year for carbon; illustrative). Nitrifying bacteria need oxygen; denitrifying bacteria work without it; decomposition and bacteria speed up about ×2 for every 10 °C. The steady state is the solution of the averaged equations. Fossil fuels start with 30% of the carbon, because they formed over millions of years.
  • The field: 40 m × 20 m beside a wood; light rises with distance from the wood. Each species grows in clumps (seeds fall near the parent) and is most common at its preferred light. Counts are exact; the light meter has ±4% error. % cover counts the small squares (out of 100) that are at least half covered by grass. Real fields also vary with soil, water, trampling and grazing.
  • Statistics: Simpson's index D = 1 − Σ(n/N)²; Spearman's rank with tied ranks averaged; Student's t-test for two independent samples (pooled variance). Critical values (p = 0.05, two-tailed) from standard tables.
  • Succession: a 60 × 30 grid of patches; each year a patch can move up a stage if the soil is deep enough, more likely if its neighbours are already there; soil deepens as plants die and decompose. Grazing removes shrubs and young trees; burning resets to grassland. Real succession also depends on climate, seeds arriving from elsewhere and chance events; times are illustrative.
  • The lake: nitrate, algae, rooted plants, dead matter, oxygen and fish in days; algae shade the plants; decomposers use 0.8 mg of oxygen for every mg of dead matter broken down; fish die below about 4 mg/dm³ of oxygen. Phosphate (often the real limit in lakes) is left out.
  • The forest: 800 plots in 100 ha; about 250 t of carbon per hectare of mature trees; bare ground loses about 40 t of soil per hectare a year; plant species by a species–area rule. All approximate. The fishery (A level): logistic growth (r = 0.5 a year, K = 100 thousand tonnes) with random good and bad years; maximum sustainable yield rK ÷ 4 when the catch rate is r ÷ 2.
  • Quality only changes the picture (moving dots, plant drawings, trees and fish); the model and the batch runs are the same at every level.
