Ecosystems and Sampling · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · How much energy reaches each level?

What this shows. Each run sends a million packets of the producers' energy up a chain; at each step a packet passes on with a 10% chance. 10% reaches primary consumers, 1% secondary, 0.1% tertiary and 0.01% the fifth level: on the log scale the points fall on a straight line. That is why food chains rarely have more than four or five levels: there is too little energy left to support a population at the top. With a million packets the runs barely scatter; with fewer packets (try 1000) the top level is very uncertain.

2 · Waterlogged soil: where does the nitrate go?

What this shows. Each run follows about 270 packets of nitrogen atoms in a soil for 10 years. With no oxygen, nitrifying bacteria (Nitrosomonas, Nitrobacter) cannot work and denitrifying bacteria turn what nitrate there is into nitrogen gas: almost no nitrate for plants (ammonium builds up instead). As the soil is aerated the nitrate rises steeply. The yellow line is the steady state of the averaged equations; runs scatter by roughly √(number of atoms), because each atom moves at random.

3 · Random quadrats: estimating a population

What this shows. Each run places 10 random 1 m² quadrats in the 800 m² field, counts daisies and estimates the population as mean per m² × 800. The estimates are centred on the true number (random sampling is unbiased), but they scatter widely, because daisies grow in clumps and most of them are out in the open: one quadrat may land in a clump of 30, another on bare ground. The predicted spread is the SD of single quadrats ÷ √10.

4 · More quadrats, better estimates

What this shows. Each step repeats the estimate 30 times. The mean stays near the true population at every sample size, but the spread (± 1 SD bars) shrinks roughly as 1 ÷ √(number of quadrats): four times as many quadrats halves the uncertainty. This is why a minimum number of quadrats is needed, and why a running mean that has levelled off is a good sign that you have enough.

5 · Simpson's index from quadrats

What this shows. Each run pools the plants counted in random quadrats in zone A (near the wood) and works out D = 1 − Σ(n/N)². With one or two quadrats D is often too low and very variable, because a single quadrat can be dominated by one clump of one species. With more quadrats it settles on the true value for the zone (yellow). Compare zone B in the live model: the open field is dominated by daisies, so its D is lower.

6 · Two zones: is the difference significant?

What this shows. Each run places 10 random quadrats in zone A (near the wood) and 10 in zone B (open field), counts buttercups and works out Student's t. Buttercups really are more common in zone A (they prefer part shade), but with 10 quadrats a clumped plant gives t values scattered from about 0 to 5: many runs fall below the critical value 2.10 (grey) even though the difference is real. More quadrats make a real difference far more likely to show up as significant. The yellow line is an approximate expected t (difference in true means ÷ its standard error); the runs average about 10% above it, because with clumped, skewed counts a sample of 10 often misses the big clumps and underestimates the spread, which makes t larger.

7 · Grazing and deflected succession

What this shows. Each run leaves an abandoned field for 150 years with some grazing. Without grazing most of it becomes woodland; grazing eats shrub and tree seedlings, so succession is held back at grassland (a plagioclimax, as on chalk downland grazed by sheep). The yellow line is the well-mixed prediction: it differs from the grid because real trees spread from neighbours, in patches. Runs scatter little because the grid has 1800 patches.

8 · Fishing: catch against fishing rate

What this shows. Each run fishes a stock for 100 years and averages the catch over the last 50. The catch rises at first, peaks near h = r ÷ 2 = 0.25 (the maximum sustainable yield, about 12.5 thousand t a year), then falls as the stock is fished down; at h ≥ 0.5 the stock collapses and the catch goes to nearly 0. Random good and bad breeding years scatter the runs, most of all near the collapse; at h = 0.5 the line reaches 0, but runs of good years keep a small stock (and catch) going for a while.
