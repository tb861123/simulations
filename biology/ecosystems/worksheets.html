Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Remove a species

What to look for: Caterpillars and aphids have fewer predators, so they increase (and eat more oak). Sparrowhawks lose their only food here, so they decrease or move away. In a food web, one change spreads to many species.

Investigation 2: Why only 10%?

What to look for: About 10% passes on. The rest is used in respiration (released as heat), lost in waste and faeces, or in parts that are never eaten (roots, bones). That is why there are rarely more than four or five levels.

Investigation 3: Pyramids

What to look for: One oak tree feeds thousands of caterpillars, so the pyramid of numbers is narrow at the bottom. Pyramids of biomass and energy are the right way up, because the oak is huge. A pyramid of energy is always wider at the bottom.

Investigation 4: Nitrogen in waterlogged soil

What to look for: With little oxygen, nitrifying bacteria cannot make nitrate, and denitrifying bacteria turn nitrate back into nitrogen gas: the nitrate falls and plants grow poorly. Draining and ploughing let air in.

Investigation 5: Peas and beans

What to look for: Legumes have root nodules containing nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) that turn nitrogen gas into ammonia the plant can use. When they die, the nitrogen enters the soil: crop rotation uses this.

Investigation 6: Estimate a population

What to look for: Population ≈ mean number per m² × 800. More quadrats give a more reliable estimate (the standard error falls as 1 ÷ √n). Quadrats must be placed at random, or the estimate is biased.

Investigation 7: Transect

What to look for: Bluebells are commonest in the shade near the wood; daisies in the open, where there is more light. A transect shows how the distribution of a species changes with an abiotic factor (light).

Investigation 8: Eutrophication

What to look for: Nitrate and phosphate → algae grow fast (algal bloom) → they block light so plants below die → algae and plants die → bacteria decomposing them respire and use up the oxygen → fish suffocate. It is the bacteria, not the algae, that use the oxygen.

Investigation 9: Succession and grazing A level

What to look for: Pioneers (lichens, mosses) break rock and add humus; soil deepens; grasses, then shrubs, birch and finally oak (the climax community). Grazing stops woody plants establishing: deflected succession (a plagioclimax), as on moorland or chalk grassland.

Investigation 10: Simpson's index A level

What to look for: D = 1 − Σn(n − 1) ÷ N(N − 1). Higher D (closer to 1) means more diverse: more species and more even numbers. Compare the two zones' values in the readings.

Batch experiment 1: How many quadrats are enough?

Clumped plants vary a lot from quadrat to quadrat, so a few quadrats can be far out. The error falls roughly as 1 ÷ √n: four times as many quadrats halve it.
Theory (∝ 1 ÷ √n): 5: 35.8 · 10: 25.3 · 20: 17.9 · 40: 12.6 · 80: 8.94
Result from a test run: 5: 26.8 ± 4.03 · 10: 12.3 ± 1.88 · 20: 11.8 ± 3.59 · 40: 10.9 ± 3.24 · 80: 9 ± 2.67 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Random or chosen?

Choosing 'good' spots overestimates the population: random placement (using random numbers for coordinates) avoids this bias.
Result from a test run: 1: 100 ± 5.92 · 2: 168 ± 6.65 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Transfer efficiency and the top of the chain

Energy falls as the efficiency cubed over three steps: at 10%, only 10 kJ of every 10 000 kJ in the plants reaches the top predators. Too little energy is left for a 6th or 7th level.
Theory (10 000 × (e/100)³): 5: 1.25 · 8: 5.12 · 10: 10 · 12: 17.3 · 15: 33.7 · 20: 80
Result from a test run: 5: 1.34 ± 0.119 · 8: 5.03 ± 0.576 · 10: 10.6 ± 2.39 · 12: 16 ± 1.25 · 15: 38.4 ± 11 · 20: 90.3 ± 14.7 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Soil oxygen and nitrate

Waterlogged soil: little nitrification and lots of denitrification, so very little nitrate. Aerated soil: much more.
Theory (model): 0.05: 0.489 · 0.2: 0.645 · 0.4: 1.08 · 0.6: 2.25 · 0.8: 6.57 · 1: 12.2
Result from a test run: 0.05: 0.495 ± 0.0194 · 0.2: 0.661 ± 0.0207 · 0.4: 1.08 ± 0.0235 · 0.6: 2.21 ± 0.0591 · 0.8: 6.66 ± 0.23 · 1: 12.6 ± 0.428 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Fertiliser runoff and the fish

A little fertiliser is fine; beyond a threshold the algal bloom and its decay drive the oxygen below about 3 mg/L, where most fish die.
Theory (model): 0: 5.92 · 0.2: 3.02 · 0.4: 2.02 · 0.6: 0.796 · 0.8: 0.556 · 1: 0.456 · 1.2: 0.408 · 1.5: 0.371
Result from a test run: 0: 5.9 ± 0.0314 · 0.2: 2.96 ± 0.0584 · 0.4: 2.1 ± 0.151 · 0.6: 0.982 ± 0.102 · 0.8: 0.638 ± 0.134 · 1: 0.445 ± 0.15 · 1.2: 0.44 ± 0.114 · 1.5: 0.442 ± 0.172 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 6: Grazing and succession

Without grazing, woodland takes over; grazing holds the community at grassland (deflected succession).
Result from a test run: 0: 87.2 ± 0.487 · 0.01: 77.4 ± 0.645 · 0.02: 67.2 ± 2.13 · 0.04: 47.1 ± 0.456 · 0.06: 30.2 ± 1.93 · 0.1: 12.9 ± 0.414 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)
