Teacher notes

Investigation 1: A 3 : 1 ratio

What to look for: Each parent's gametes are half V, half v. Offspring: ¼ VV, ½ Vv, ¼ vv, so ¾ have long wings (V is dominant) and ¼ vestigial: 3 : 1. With 100 flies you might get 71 : 29 or 78 : 22. Breed 1000 and the ratio gets closer: chance evens out over larger numbers.

Investigation 2: A test cross

What to look for: If it is VV, every offspring gets a V: all long wings. If it is Vv, half the offspring get v from it and v from the vv parent: half vestigial (1 : 1). A test cross with the homozygous recessive reveals the unknown genotype.

Investigation 3: Punnett squares

What to look for: The square gives the probabilities: each offspring has a 3 in 4 chance of being tall. A family of 4 could have 4 tall, or 2 dwarf, by chance. Only over many offspring does the ratio approach 3 : 1.

Investigation 4: Codominance

What to look for: Neither allele is dominant: RW cattle show both (red and white hairs mixed: roan). The ratio is 1 red : 2 roan : 1 white, and every genotype has its own phenotype.

Investigation 5: Carriers in a family

What to look for: Cystic fibrosis is recessive: a person needs two copies of the allele (ff) to be affected. Carriers (Ff) are unaffected. If both parents are carriers, each child has a 1 in 4 chance of cystic fibrosis. Polydactyly is dominant: an affected child must have an affected parent.

Investigation 6: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 A level

What to look for: The genes are on different chromosomes, so they assort independently: 9 long grey : 3 long ebony : 3 vestigial grey : 1 vestigial ebony. χ² with 3 degrees of freedom: if it is below 7.81 (p > 0.05), any difference is likely due to chance.

Investigation 7: Morgan's white eyes A level

What to look for: The F1 all have red eyes. In the F2, a quarter of the flies have white eyes, and all of them are males: the gene is on the X chromosome, and males have only one X. Thomas Morgan found exactly this in 1910, the first evidence that genes are on chromosomes.

Investigation 8: Linked genes A level

What to look for: Most offspring are the parental types (long grey, vestigial black): vg and b are on the same chromosome. The recombinants (long black, vestigial grey) come from crossing over in meiosis. Recombination frequency = recombinants ÷ total ≈ 17%: the genes are about 17 map units apart.

Batch experiment 1: How close to 3 : 1?

Most crosses land within about ±9 of 25 (two standard deviations: 2 × √(100 × 0.25 × 0.75) ≈ 8.7). Chance alone makes each vial different.
Theory (Mendel: ¼): 25
Result from a test run: mean 25.3, SD 4.44, SE 0.314 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 2: Bigger samples, closer ratios

Four times the sample halves the typical error (it falls as 1 ÷ √n). That is why Mendel counted thousands of peas.
Theory (chance alone): 10: 11 · 20: 7.75 · 50: 4.9 · 100: 3.46 · 200: 2.45 · 500: 1.55 · 1000: 1.1
Result from a test run: 10: 12.5 ± 2.5 · 20: 6.25 ± 1.57 · 50: 4.5 ± 1.5 · 100: 2.88 ± 1.03 · 200: 2.69 ± 0.472 · 500: 2.18 ± 0.76 · 1000: 1.28 ± 0.318 (mean ± SE, 8 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: A test cross

Centred on 50%: the heterozygous parent gives V or v equally often.
Theory (1 : 1): 50
Result from a test run: mean 49.4, SD 4.64, SE 0.328 from 200 runs

Batch experiment 4: Chi-squared under the null hypothesis

Even when the hypothesis is true, χ² is sometimes large by chance: about 1 cross in 20 would wrongly be 'rejected' at p = 0.05.
Theory (mean = df = 3): 3
Result from a test run: mean 3.19, SD 2.45, SE 0.141 from 300 runs

Batch experiment 5: Measuring recombination

Recombination frequency measures distance along the chromosome; it can never exceed 50%, which looks the same as genes on different chromosomes.
Theory (= distance): 0: 0 · 5: 5 · 10: 10 · 17: 17 · 25: 25 · 35: 35 · 50: 50
Result from a test run: 0: 0 ± 0 · 5: 4.63 ± 0.375 · 10: 9.63 ± 1.52 · 17: 18.3 ± 0.924 · 25: 25.1 ± 0.718 · 35: 35.6 ± 1.6 · 50: 51.5 ± 1.84 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)
