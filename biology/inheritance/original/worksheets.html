Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Ratios emerge from many offspring

What this shows. Each run breeds a family of Aa × Aa offspring and records the proportion that are aa (recessive phenotype). With 10 offspring anything from 0 to 0.6 happens; with thousands the proportion is very close to ¼. The spread (SD) shrinks as 1 ÷ √n: four times as many offspring halves the scatter. This is why Mendel counted thousands of peas.

2 · How far from 3 : 1 is normal?

What this shows. 500 families of 100 offspring each. The proportion recessive averages 0.25 but individual families range from about 0.15 to 0.35; the SD matches √(pq/n) = √(0.25 × 0.75 / 100) = 0.043. A result of 30 dwarf out of 100 is not evidence against 3 : 1: this is what the χ² test is for.

3 · χ² when the hypothesis is true

What this shows. Each run is a true 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 cross of 160 offspring, tested with χ² against 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. χ² is never exactly 0: chance always makes some difference. Its mean is about 3 (the degrees of freedom) and about 5% of runs exceed the critical value 7.815, so even a correct hypothesis is wrongly rejected 1 time in 20 at p = 0.05.

4 · Does χ² spot linkage?

What this shows. Each run is a test cross of 100 offspring tested against 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. The y-axis is the fraction of runs where χ² was significant. Tightly linked genes (close together) are almost always detected; genes 35–40 map units apart often are not, because recombinants are then nearly as common as parental types; at 50 (not linked) only about 5% of tests are significant, by chance. The yellow line is the exact chance from the non-central χ² distribution. Count more offspring (n) to detect looser linkage.

5 · Measuring recombination frequency

What this shows. Recombination frequency = recombinant offspring ÷ total × 100. The measured values follow the true distance (one map unit = 1% recombination) with a scatter of about √(r(1 − r)/n). Real maps measured this way are only additive for short distances, because two crossovers between distant genes cancel out (not modelled here).

6 · Sons of a carrier mother

What this shows. A carrier mother (XᴴXʰ) passes either X to each son with chance ½, so on average half her sons are affected; the father's X goes only to daughters. In families of 40 sons the proportion scatters by √(0.25/40) = 0.079. Real families are far smaller, so whole families of affected or unaffected sons are common.

7 · Hardy–Weinberg: counting CF carriers

What this shows. You cannot see carriers, so they are estimated: count affected people (q² = 1 in 2500), take the square root to get q, then 2pq. In a sample of 1000 people there may be no affected people at all, giving an estimate of 0, or one, giving 6% carriers. Only samples of hundreds of thousands pin the carrier frequency (about 1 in 25) down well. This assumes Hardy–Weinberg conditions (random mating, no selection).
