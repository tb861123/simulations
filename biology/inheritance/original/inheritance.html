How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Meiosis and fertilisation: every gamete gets one allele of each gene at random (each with chance ½); genes on different chromosomes are passed on independently (independent assortment). Fertilisation joins one gamete from each parent at random. So every offspring is a fresh random draw, and ratios such as 3 : 1 only appear on average, over many offspring.
  • Fruit flies: vestigial wings (vg) and black body (b) are both on chromosome 2, about 17 map units apart (real flies: about 17–18; approximate); ebony body (e) is on chromosome 3; white eyes (w) is on the X chromosome. A crossover between b and vg happens in a female's meiosis with chance = the recombination frequency. Fruit-fly males do not cross over at all (true of Drosophila), so test crosses for linkage use a heterozygous female. All flies survive and are counted; really some mutants (e.g. vestigial) survive less well, which skews ratios.
  • Punnett squares: the square lists every gamete of each parent with its probability; each box is one possible offspring, equally likely in a monohybrid or dihybrid square. Epistasis: in labradors ee hides the B gene (recessive epistasis, 9 : 3 : 4); in squash W hides the Y gene (dominant epistasis, 12 : 3 : 1).
  • Chi-squared: χ² = Σ(O − E)² ÷ E, with E from the Mendelian hypothesis (for two genes: independent assortment). Degrees of freedom = classes − 1. If χ² is above the critical value at p = 0.05, the difference is unlikely to be due to chance alone, so the hypothesis is rejected. Critical values and p-values are calculated exactly here, not read from a table.
  • Pedigrees: one gene, with the disease allele recessive, dominant or X-linked recessive. To make the condition show up often, the families' grandparents are drawn with a high allele frequency (15–20%); the probabilities use the real population frequency for anyone who married in. The chance for the next child is worked out exactly from who is and is not affected in the tree (adding up over every genotype the key people could have), as you would in an exam; the "true" chance uses the genotypes the model knows.
  • Variation: height is controlled by many genes with equal, additive effects (each allele 50% common) plus a normal environmental effect. With one gene and no environment you get three separate classes (discontinuous); with many genes and the environment the distribution becomes a smooth bell curve (continuous). Real height involves thousands of genetic variants with small effects; heritability is about 80% (approximate).
  • Hardy–Weinberg (A level): p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1, assuming a large population, random mating, no mutation, migration or selection. For an X-linked allele, the frequency in men is q.
  • Quality only changes how many flies, offspring and people are drawn and their detail; every offspring is still counted, and batch runs are the same at every level.
  • Left out: mutation, incomplete penetrance and variable expression, lethal genotypes (e.g. in yellow mice), genes on the Y chromosome, more than one crossover, genomic imprinting, and selection against some phenotypes.
