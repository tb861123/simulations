Ant Colony Foraging · Teacher notesant-colony.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: From wandering to highways

What to look for: At first every ant wanders, laying home scent. The few that stumble on food walk back laying food scent, strongest near the food. Other ants following that scent find the food sooner and add more, so one route wins (positive feedback). Wiggly bits of a trail are slower to walk, get less traffic, fade, and are short-cut: the trail straightens.

Investigation 2: The double bridge

What to look for: Ants taking the short route get back sooner, so the short route is marked first and more often. That small head start grows: within a few minutes nearly all the traffic takes the short route (about 95% here). No ant compares the lengths. Real ants did the same, though not every colony picked the short branch every time.

Investigation 3: Block the trail

What to look for: Ants pile up at the wall and spread along it (random turns). The ones that get round find the old scent beyond and lay new scent on the detour. The old trail behind the wall fades, the detour is reinforced, and within a minute or two traffic flows round the end of the wall.

Investigation 4: Move the food

What to look for: Long-lasting scent keeps ants marching to the old spot for minutes after the food has gone: the colony is stubborn. Very short-lived scent never builds a trail at all. In between, the old trail fades soon enough to free the ants to find the new spot. Evaporation is not a flaw: it is how the colony forgets.

Investigation 5: How much randomness?

What to look for: Up to about 30° the colony copes well: walls, the edge and the scent itself already scatter the ants enough to find new food. Beyond that, ants cannot hold a trail and foraging falls off. Real ants are 'noisy' on purpose: some always leave the trail, and those are the ones that find new food and short-cuts. Try it with 'New food appears' on.

Investigation 6: Too few ants

What to look for: With few ants, scent evaporates before anyone else walks the same way, so no trail builds up and each ant forages alone. Above a critical number trails form and the whole colony speeds up. Small colonies of real species often forage alone or recruit nestmates one at a time ('tandem running') instead.

Investigation 7: Near or far?

What to look for: The near patch nearly always wins, because round trips are quicker and its trail is reinforced faster. Usually the colony concentrates on it and only switches to the far patch once the near one is empty and its trail fades.

Batch experiment 1: Evaporation: an optimum?

Where the next patch appears is random, so runs vary a lot. Very short-lived scent (1 s) cannot hold a trail. Very long-lived scent keeps ants on trails to food that has run out. The best is in between: about 3–5 s here, falling slowly for longer half-lives.
Result from a test run: 1: 78.7 ± 9.8 · 3: 183 ± 22.2 · 5: 167 ± 16.4 · 10: 141 ± 11.1 · 20: 144 ± 17.1 · 40: 113 ± 12.1 · 80: 89.6 ± 19 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Bigger colonies: better ants?

Small colonies lay too little scent to keep a trail, so each ant forages alone and brings home about 0.2–0.3 crumbs a minute. From about 200 ants trails hold and each ant brings home about twice as much; beyond that it levels off, because the patches empty and new ones must be found. In this model ants never block each other; real crowding would cut it further.
Result from a test run: 25: 0.253 ± 0.126 · 50: 0.198 ± 0.0345 · 100: 0.32 ± 0.0656 · 200: 0.608 ± 0.196 · 400: 0.578 ± 0.0838 · 800: 0.554 ± 0.0572 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: How much randomness?

In this arena the colony does about as well anywhere from 0 to about 30° (walls, the edge and the scent itself already scatter the ants), but at 45° ants can no longer hold a trail and the food brought home roughly halves. Runs vary a lot because of where new food appears.
Result from a test run: 0: 143 ± 16.2 · 5: 119 ± 4.21 · 10: 113 ± 9.57 · 20: 132 ± 21.7 · 30: 108 ± 10.7 · 45: 61.1 ± 5.81 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: The double bridge

Nearly every run picks the short branch, most with over 90% of the traffic. Goss et al. (1989) found Argentine ants chose the short branch in most trials when the long one was twice as long. Early random choices matter: in a few runs the long branch keeps a share for a while.
Result from a test run: mean 0.923, SD 0.186, SE 0.0339 from 30 runs
