Plant Transport and Responses · Teacher notesAnswers from a test run (fixed seed)

Teacher notes

The results below come from one test run with the default seed, so a class should get the same numbers. A different seed gives slightly different values within the scatter.

1 · Light and transpiration

What this shows. Each run sets up a new leafy shoot, lets it adjust, then takes three 2-minute readings and averages them. In the dark the stomata are almost shut and only a little water escapes through the cuticle; as light increases the stomata open and the rate rises steeply, then levels off once they are fully open (the still air next to the leaf then limits the rate). Runs scatter by about 15% because every shoot has a different leaf area and number of stomata: compare shoots by dividing by leaf area, or use the same shoot for every light level.

2 · Humidity and transpiration

What this shows. The rate falls in a straight line as the humidity rises, because the rate is proportional to how much drier the air is than the air spaces in the leaf (the water vapour concentration gradient). At 100% humidity transpiration would stop. The best-fit line is the model's rule: rate ∝ (1 − humidity).

3 · Wind and transpiration

What this shows. Wind blows away the layer of humid air next to the leaf. The first bit of wind has the biggest effect (still air to a breeze about doubles the rate); after that the stomata themselves limit the rate, so the curve levels off.

4 · How precise is one potometer result?

What this shows. The same conditions, 500 different shoots. The mean of all of them is very close to the model's true rate (the standard error is tiny), but single shoots spread with an SD of about 15% of the mean, mostly because shoots differ, not because of reading the scale. That is why you repeat with several shoots and why a difference of 10% between two single shoots means very little. The blue curve is the predicted spread from the error model.

5 · Capillary radius and bubble speed (A level)

What this shows. The same volume of water a minute moves the bubble further in a narrower tube: distance = volume ÷ (πr²), so doubling the radius quarters the distance. On log–log axes the points lie on a line of gradient −2. A narrow capillary gives a longer, more precise distance to measure, but the bubble runs off the scale sooner.

6 · Phototropism: brighter light, bigger bend?

What this shows. Each run grows six oat coleoptiles for 4 hours with light from one side and averages their protractor angles. With no light they grow straight; dim light already causes a clear bend and brighter light more, levelling off as most of the auxin is already on the shaded side (and gravity pulls the other way as the shoot tilts). Seedlings differ by ±15% in growth and ±20% in sensitivity, so means of six still scatter by a few degrees.

7 · Went's agar blocks (A level)

What this shows. Agar blocks with more auxin, placed on one side of decapitated coleoptiles in the dark, cause more curvature: nearly in proportion at low concentrations, levelling off at high ones. Went used exactly this to measure auxin (the Avena curvature test). With no auxin there is no bending, which shows that a chemical from the tip causes the response. The same 48 coleoptiles are used at every step (so only the auxin changes); if they happen to be a little less sensitive than average, every point sits a little below the line.

8 · Translocation: which sink wins? (A level)

What this shows. Each plant is fed ¹⁴CO₂ and the radioactivity reaching its fruit and roots is counted a day later. The stronger the fruit sink (the faster it unloads and uses sucrose), the lower the pressure at that end of the phloem, so more sap flows towards it and it gets a bigger share. Runs scatter because plants differ (±15% in each sink) and because radioactive counts are random (Poisson); the counting error alone would be about ±1%.

9 · Apical dominance: how much auxin? (A level)

What this shows. Pea shoots with the apex removed and auxin paste on the stump. With no auxin (plain paste) the side buds grow tens of millimetres in two weeks; a little auxin already holds them back and the normal amount from an apex (1) keeps them almost dormant. The same auxin that makes stems grow inhibits buds: different tissues respond differently to the same concentration.
