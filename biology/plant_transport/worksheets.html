Plant Transport and Responses · Teacher notesplant-transport.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: A day in the life of a plant

What to look for: Fastest in the early afternoon: bright light (stomata open), warm and dry air. The leaves lose water first; the tension it creates then pulls water up the stem and into the roots, so uptake lags.

Investigation 2: Light and the potometer

What to look for: In the dark the stomata close, so little water vapour escapes (about 5 mm/min); in bright light they open (about 18 mm/min). Above about 40% light the stomata are fully open, so more light adds little.

Investigation 3: Humidity

What to look for: Humid air already holds lots of water vapour, so the diffusion gradient from the leaf is shallow: transpiration falls in a straight line as humidity rises.

Investigation 4: Wind

What to look for: Wind blows away the layer of moist air around the leaf, keeping the gradient steep: a big rise at first, then it levels off.

Investigation 5: Phloem: sources and sinks

What to look for: From the leaves (sources) to the flower and growing tip above and to the roots below (sinks). Unlike xylem, phloem flow can go both ways.

Investigation 6: Phototropism

What to look for: Towards the light. Auxin moves to the shaded side, where it makes cells elongate more, so the shoot bends towards the light: more light for photosynthesis.

Investigation 7: Geotropism

What to look for: Shoot up (negative geotropism), root down (positive geotropism). Auxin collects on the lower side: in shoots it speeds up elongation, in roots it slows it.

Investigation 8: Cohesion–tension A level

What to look for: Water evaporates from the spongy mesophyll cell walls and diffuses out through the stomata. This lowers the water potential in the leaf; water moves in from the xylem; hydrogen bonds hold the water molecules together (cohesion) and to the xylem walls (adhesion), so the whole column is pulled up under tension.

Investigation 9: Capillary radius (PAG5) A level

What to look for: Speed ∝ 1/r² (the same volume per minute spread over a cross-section πr²): halve the radius, four times the speed. Convert speed to volume: rate = speed × πr².

Batch experiment 1: Light and transpiration

A steep rise as the stomata open, then a plateau once they are fully open.
Theory (stomatal model): 0: 5.63 · 20: 16.2 · 40: 17.6 · 60: 18.2 · 80: 18.4 · 100: 18.6
Result from a test run: 0: 5.77 ± 0.205 · 20: 16.4 ± 0.514 · 40: 18 ± 0.441 · 60: 17.6 ± 0.937 · 80: 18.5 ± 0.836 · 100: 19.9 ± 0.793 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Humidity and transpiration

A straight line down: the diffusion gradient is proportional to how far the air is from saturation.
Theory (∝ (1 − humidity)): 20: 29.5 · 35: 24 · 50: 18.4 · 65: 12.9 · 80: 7.38 · 95: 1.85
Result from a test run: 20: 30.3 ± 1.13 · 35: 24.3 ± 0.767 · 50: 18.9 ± 0.463 · 65: 12.5 ± 0.669 · 80: 7.37 ± 0.331 · 95: 1.92 ± 0.0735 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Wind and transpiration

A big jump from still air to a breeze, then smaller gains: the moist layer is already mostly blown away.
Theory (boundary-layer model): 0: 18.4 · 1: 34.8 · 2: 37.3 · 3: 38.3 · 4: 38.8
Result from a test run: 0: 18.7 ± 0.821 · 1: 33.6 ± 1.39 · 2: 39.1 ± 1.16 · 3: 39.6 ± 1.69 · 4: 38.2 ± 1.4 (mean ± SE, 10 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: How precise is one potometer result?

Mostly within about ±15%: shoots differ in leaf area and stomata, so repeat with several shoots or measure leaf area.
Theory (model): 18.4
Result from a test run: mean 18.4, SD 2.47, SE 0.11 from 500 runs

A straight line of gradient −2: speed ∝ 1/r², because the same volume of water moves through a cross-section of area πr².
