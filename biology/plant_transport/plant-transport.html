How the model works (and what it leaves out)
  • Transpiration: water vapour diffuses out through the stomata, then through a layer of still air next to the leaf. Rate = 0.4 × leaf area × conductance × (saturation vapour pressure at the temperature × (1 − humidity)). Stomatal conductance rises with light (stomata open), falls in drought (abscisic acid) and in very hot leaves; the still-air layer gets thinner as wind rises (conductance 0.5 + 1.5√wind). A waxy cuticle lets a little water through (3% of open stomata). Leaf temperature is taken as air temperature. All illustrative.
  • Potometer: measures water uptake, which is almost the same as transpiration (about 1% is used in photosynthesis and keeping cells turgid). The bubble moves rate ÷ (πr²) mm a minute. Stomata take about 3 minutes (time constant) to respond to a change. Each shoot differs (leaf area ±12%, stomata ±10%); the rate wanders ±4%; the scale is read to ±0.4 mm and the stopwatch to ±0.25 s. A shoot not cut under water may have air in its xylem (30–70% slower).
  • The day: sunshine and temperature follow the sun; the air holds the same water all day, so humidity falls as it warms. Uptake by the roots lags behind transpiration because the stem stores water (time constant about 1.2 hours); the stem shrinks a little as its water comes under tension. Soil water potential falls steeply as the soil dries; below about −0.8 MPa in the stem, abscisic acid closes the stomata.
  • Roots: water enters the root hair by osmosis when the soil water potential is higher than the root's (−0.35 MPa). Mineral ions are taken up by active transport (needs ATP from aerobic respiration; ×2 per 10 °C, stopped by cyanide). At A level: about 70% of the water crosses the cortex in the cell walls (apoplast; illustrative); the Casparian strip forces it into the cytoplasm (symplast) at the endodermis. Ions pumped into the xylem lower its water potential: root pressure up to about 0.2 MPa.
  • Translocation: the phloem is 20 sections from the roots to the fruit. Sucrose is loaded at the source leaf by companion cells (active, needs ATP and light for photosynthesis); its high concentration draws water in from the xylem by osmosis, raising the pressure; sinks unload sucrose, so the pressure is lower there; sap flows from high to low pressure (mass flow), carrying the sucrose. Pressure = 0.07 MPa per % sucrose + xylem water potential (taken as −0.1 MPa). Sieve plates, starch storage and the details of unloading are left out.
  • Tropisms: each coleoptile tip makes auxin. Light from one side moves auxin to the shaded side (up to 75:25); gravity moves it to the lower side. Auxin diffuses down; in shoots more auxin means more cell elongation, in roots (at these concentrations) less. The difference between the two sides bends the organ; as it turns towards the stimulus the difference shrinks. Seedlings vary (growth ±15%); protractor ±1.5°.
  • Hormones: lateral buds grow at a rate inhibited by auxin from the apex (or paste); dwarf peas make too little gibberellin and grow towards tall height as GA is sprayed; drought raises abscisic acid, which closes stomata; ethene speeds ripening and ripening fruit make more ethene (positive feedback). All rates illustrative; cytokinins, strigolactones and other interactions are left out.
  • Quality only changes the picture (particles, vapour, cell detail); the model and the batch runs are the same at every level.
