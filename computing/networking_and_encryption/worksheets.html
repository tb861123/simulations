Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Packets take different routes

What to look for: Each packet is routed separately using the current delays, so routes differ and packets can overtake each other. The sequence number in each header lets the receiver reassemble the message in the right order.

Investigation 2: Lost packets

What to look for: Some packets vanish (red ✕). The receiver does not acknowledge them, so after a timeout the sender re-sends them. The message arrives complete, just later: TCP gives reliable delivery over an unreliable network.

Investigation 3: Circuit switching A level

What to look for: One path is reserved for the whole message, so packets arrive in order with a steady delay (good for live calls). But the path is tied up even when idle and one broken link stops everything; packet switching shares links and routes round failures.

Investigation 4: Caesar cipher

What to look for: Only 25 useful keys, so a computer (or a patient person) can try them all at once: brute force.

Investigation 5: Frequency analysis

What to look for: E is the most common English letter, so the tallest ciphertext bar is probably E shifted by the key. The 'crack' reading tries all 26 shifts and picks the best match to English.

Investigation 6: Vigenère

What to look for: Much flatter: each letter is shifted by a different amount depending on its position, so the same plaintext letter becomes different ciphertext letters. Longer keys hide the pattern better.

Investigation 7: Public and private keys (toy RSA) A level

What to look for: n = 3233, φ = 3120, d = 2753 (17 × 2753 = 46 801 = 15 × 3120 + 1). c = 65¹⁷ mod 3233 = 2790 and 2790²⁷⁵³ mod 3233 = 65. Anyone can encrypt with (e, n); only the holder of d can decrypt. Real keys use primes hundreds of digits long.

Investigation 8: A digital signature A level

What to look for: Decrypting the signature with the sender's PUBLIC key gives back the hash. If it matches the hash of the message received, it was signed by the private key's owner and has not been changed. A certificate from a trusted authority vouches that the public key really is theirs.

Batch experiment 1: Re-sending lost packets

A packet must survive every hop: probability (1 − p)⁴. Small losses per link add up over many hops.
Theory (1/(1 − p)⁴): 0: 1 · 0.02: 1.08 · 0.05: 1.23 · 0.1: 1.52 · 0.15: 1.92 · 0.2: 2.44 · 0.3: 4.16
Result from a test run: 0: 1 ± 0 · 0.02: 1.08 ± 0.005 · 0.05: 1.24 ± 0.0176 · 0.1: 1.49 ± 0.0261 · 0.15: 1.88 ± 0.0125 · 0.2: 2.45 ± 0.0307 · 0.3: 4.13 ± 0.0589 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Out-of-order arrival

With steady delays (no congestion) every packet takes the same route and order is kept. Varying delays make packets overtake, which is why TCP numbers them.
Result from a test run: 0: 0 ± 0 · 0.2: 0.02 ± 0.00707 · 0.4: 0.688 ± 0.035 · 0.6: 1.61 ± 0.0685 · 0.8: 2.41 ± 0.059 · 1: 3.03 ± 0.053 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Brute-forcing a Caesar cipher

On average half the keys. A real cipher needs so many keys (2¹²⁸ for AES) that trying half of them is impossible.
Theory ((1 + 25)/2): 13
Result from a test run: mean 12.7, SD 7, SE 0.313 from 500 runs

Batch experiment 4: How much text does frequency analysis need?

A sentence or two is enough: the longer the text, the more its letter frequencies look like English. Caesar offers no real security.
Result from a test run: 5: 0.463 ± 0.0318 · 10: 0.763 ± 0.0333 · 20: 0.94 ± 0.00577 · 40: 1 ± 0 · 80: 1 ± 0 · 160: 1 ± 0 (mean ± SE, 3 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Does RSA always decrypt? (A level)

Always, because ed ≡ 1 (mod φ(n)) (Euler's theorem). Its security comes from the difficulty of factorising n into p and q when they are huge.
Theory (always): 1
Result from a test run: mean 1, SD 0, SE 0 from 100 runs
