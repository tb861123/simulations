Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Linear search

What to look for: 1 for the first, 31 for the last, and 31 (every item) to be sure it is absent. On average about (n + 1)/2 = 16 for an item that is present. Linear search does not need sorted data.

Investigation 2: Binary search

What to look for: Each comparison halves the part still to search: 31 → 15 → 7 → 3 → 1, so at most 5 comparisons (log₂ 32 = 5). It only works because the list is sorted.

Investigation 3: Searching for something that isn't there

What to look for: When low passes high there is nothing left to search: the item is not present. That took about log₂ n comparisons, far fewer than a linear search's n.

Investigation 4: A hash table A level

What to look for: When two keys give the same address, linear probing puts the second in the next free slot (wrapping round to 0). Searching for it must then follow the same path.

Investigation 5: Chaining A level

What to look for: Each address holds a linked list (an overflow area); colliding keys join the list. Yes: the load factor can exceed 1, but long chains make searching slower.

Investigation 6: A nearly full table A level

What to look for: Clusters: a run of filled slots catches any key that hashes into it and makes the run longer. The average successful search needs about ½(1 + 1/(1 − α)) probes, about 6.5 when α = 0.92.

Batch experiment 1: Linear search: average comparisons

Each position is equally likely, so on average half way. O(n): ten times the data, ten times the work.
Theory ((n + 1)/2): 10: 5.5 · 20: 10.5 · 40: 20.5 · 60: 30.5 · 80: 40.5 · 100: 50.5
Result from a test run: 10: 5.54 ± 0.161 · 20: 10.2 ± 0.3 · 40: 21.5 ± 1.02 · 60: 30.3 ± 0.557 · 80: 41.3 ± 0.397 · 100: 49.3 ± 1.1 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Binary search: average comparisons

Each doubling of n adds only ONE comparison: O(log n). For 1023 items, about 9 on average and 10 at most.
Theory (exact average (≈ log₂ n − 1)): 7: 2.43 · 15: 3.27 · 31: 4.16 · 63: 5.1 · 127: 6.06 · 255: 7.03 · 511: 8.02 · 1023: 9.01
Result from a test run: 7: 2.42 ± 0.0347 · 15: 3.26 ± 0.0594 · 31: 4.17 ± 0.0762 · 63: 5.12 ± 0.0323 · 127: 6.04 ± 0.0571 · 255: 6.98 ± 0.0712 · 511: 8.01 ± 0.0796 · 1023: 9.03 ± 0.0416 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Linear probing and the load factor (A level)

Gentle up to about α = 0.7, then it shoots up: clusters of full slots grow and merge. Keep a probed table well under 70% full.
Theory (½(1 + 1/(1 − α))): 0.1: 1.06 · 0.3: 1.21 · 0.5: 1.5 · 0.6: 1.75 · 0.7: 2.17 · 0.8: 3 · 0.9: 5.5
Result from a test run: 0.1: 1.07 ± 0.0146 · 0.3: 1.22 ± 0.0124 · 0.5: 1.41 ± 0.0277 · 0.6: 1.69 ± 0.0639 · 0.7: 2.07 ± 0.0677 · 0.8: 3.2 ± 0.19 · 0.9: 6.21 ± 0.513 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Chaining and the load factor (A level)

Grows only slowly and steadily, even beyond α = 1. The cost is the extra memory for the linked lists.
Theory (1 + α/2): 0.25: 1.13 · 0.5: 1.25 · 1: 1.5 · 1.5: 1.75 · 2: 2 · 3: 2.5
Result from a test run: 0.25: 1.13 ± 0.0105 · 0.5: 1.23 ± 0.00502 · 1: 1.5 ± 0.0171 · 1.5: 1.76 ± 0.00879 · 2: 1.99 ± 0.0173 · 3: 2.51 ± 0.01 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: When is the first collision? (A level)

The birthday problem: collisions start long before the table is full (half of the time by the 23rd key). That is why every hash table needs a collision policy.
Theory (expected ≈ 24.6): 23.6
Result from a test run: mean 25.5, SD 12.8, SE 0.641 from 400 runs
