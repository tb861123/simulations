Logic Gates and Circuits · Teacher noteslogic-gates.html

Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Meet the gates

What to look for: XOR. AND needs both, OR needs at least one, NAND and NOR are AND and OR followed by NOT. Every row of the truth table lights up as you try it.

Investigation 2: From expression to truth table

What to look for: X = 1 whenever C = 0 (four rows), and also when A = B = 1 with C = 1. So X = 0 only for C = 1 and not (A AND B): three rows.

Investigation 3: Adding two bits

What to look for: 1 + 1 = 10 in binary: Sum 0, Carry 1. One bit cannot hold the answer 2, so the carry goes to the next column.

Investigation 4: A full adder

What to look for: 1 + 1 + 1 = 11 in binary: Sum 1, Carry 1. Every column except the first must also add the carry from the column to its right.

Investigation 5: NAND is universal

What to look for: It matches A XOR B in every row. Any circuit can be built from NAND gates alone, so a whole chip can use one simple, fast gate design.

Investigation 6: A circuit that remembers A level

What to look for: S = 1 sets Q = 1, and Q STAYS 1 after S returns to 0 (feedback holds it). R resets Q to 0. S = R = 0 keeps the last value: one bit of memory. S = R = 1 is not allowed (both outputs 0).

Investigation 7: Ripple-carry adder A level

What to look for: 0111 + 1001 = 10000: the carry ripples through every column. 9 + 9 = 18 needs 5 bits: the carry out of the last column is overflow for a 4-bit result.

Investigation 8: Karnaugh map A level

What to look for: Three pairs: AB, A·Cin and B·Cin, so Cout = AB + A·Cin + B·Cin (the 'majority' function: 1 when at least two inputs are 1).

Batch experiment 1: Does the adder add? (A level)

Always correct, because each full adder implements the column-addition rule exactly. This is a check of the logic, like testing a program.
Theory (always): 1
Result from a test run: mean 1, SD 0, SE 0 from 100 runs

Batch experiment 2: How far does the carry ripple? (A level)

Usually short (about log₂ n − 1), but a ripple-carry adder must wait for the worst case (n columns), which is why fast processors use carry-lookahead adders.
Theory (≈ log₂ n − 1): 4: 1 · 8: 2 · 12: 2.58 · 16: 3 · 20: 3.32 · 24: 3.58 · 30: 3.91
Result from a test run: 4: 1.19 ± 0.0656 · 8: 2.13 ± 0.0442 · 12: 2.68 ± 0.017 · 16: 3.15 ± 0.101 · 20: 3.62 ± 0.0288 · 24: 3.82 ± 0.0994 · 30: 4.15 ± 0.0826 (mean ± SE, 4 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Does the flip-flop remember? (A level)

The feedback loop keeps Q steady when S = R = 0: a 1-bit memory cell, the basis of registers and static RAM.
Theory (always): 1
Result from a test run: mean 1, SD 0, SE 0 from 100 runs
