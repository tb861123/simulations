Teacher notes

Investigation 1: Bubble sort, pass by pass

What to look for: After pass 1 the largest value has 'bubbled' to the end; after pass k the last k items are in place (shaded green), so each pass can stop one place earlier (Last ← Last − 1).

Investigation 2: When can bubble sort stop early?

What to look for: If a whole pass makes no swaps, the list is sorted and the REPEAT…UNTIL NOT Swapped loop stops. On already-sorted data that is after one pass: n − 1 comparisons, O(n).

Investigation 3: Insertion sort

What to look for: Each larger item is copied one place to the right to make a gap, and the saved Item drops into the gap. The left part (i items) is always sorted.

Investigation 4: The worst case

What to look for: Both need 190 comparisons for reversed data: every pair is the wrong way round, so every pair must be compared. That is the worst case, O(n²).

Investigation 5: Race them A level

What to look for: Random: merge and quick sort win easily (about 550 and 650 comparisons against about 2500 for insertion and 4900 for bubble). Nearly sorted: insertion sort wins, while plain quick sort (last item as pivot) gets slower.

Investigation 6: Big O: doubling n A level

What to look for: Bubble/insertion: ×4 (O(n²)). Merge: a little over ×2 (O(n log n)). For n = 1 000 000, n² is 10¹², but n log₂ n is only about 2 × 10⁷.

Batch experiment 1: Swaps in bubble sort

Each swap fixes exactly one pair that is the wrong way round (an inversion). A random list has, on average, half of all n(n − 1)/2 pairs inverted.
Theory (n(n − 1)/4): 5: 5 · 10: 22.5 · 20: 95 · 40: 390 · 60: 885 · 80: 1580 · 100: 2480
Result from a test run: 5: 6.5 ± 1.18 · 10: 21.8 ± 0.946 · 20: 92.5 ± 4.3 · 40: 377 ± 13.8 · 60: 899 ± 17.2 · 80: 1590 ± 29.7 · 100: 2450 ± 82 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 2: Comparisons in insertion sort

About half of bubble sort's: O(n²) still, but insertion sort stops each WHILE loop as soon as it finds the item's place.
Theory (n(n − 1)/4 + n − Hₙ): 5: 7.72 · 10: 29.6 · 20: 111 · 40: 426 · 60: 940 · 80: 1660 · 100: 2570
Result from a test run: 5: 8.33 ± 0.615 · 10: 28.5 ± 0.957 · 20: 108 ± 3.97 · 40: 412 ± 13.2 · 60: 953 ± 16.9 · 80: 1670 ± 29.4 · 100: 2540 ± 81.9 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)

Batch experiment 3: Comparisons as n grows (A level)

O(n log n): doubling n a little more than doubles the work. Merge sort's count hardly varies from list to list.
Theory (≈ n log₂ n − 1.25n): 8: 14 · 16: 44 · 32: 120 · 64: 304 · 128: 736 · 256: 1730
Result from a test run: 8: 15.8 ± 0.374 · 16: 46 ± 1.14 · 32: 124 ± 1.24 · 64: 305 ± 2.6 · 128: 741 ± 1.36 · 256: 1710 ± 8.73 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 4: Quick sort on random data (beyond)

On average about 1.39 n log₂ n, but it varies more than merge sort, and on already-sorted data this version degrades to n(n − 1)/2.
Theory (2(n + 1)Hₙ − 4n): 8: 16.9 · 16: 50.9 · 32: 140 · 64: 361 · 128: 890 · 256: 2120
Result from a test run: 8: 17.6 ± 1.89 · 16: 49.6 ± 2.93 · 32: 134 ± 11.7 · 64: 358 ± 15.6 · 128: 907 ± 44 · 256: 2070 ± 73.9 (mean ± SE, 5 runs each)

Batch experiment 5: Insertion sort loves nearly sorted data

One comparison per item plus one per inversion: close to O(n). (Overlapping swaps can cancel or add inversions, so the points scatter a little.)
Theory (≈ (n − 1) + k): 0: 99 · 2: 101 · 5: 104 · 10: 109 · 20: 119 · 40: 139
Result from a test run: 0: 99 ± 0 · 2: 101 ± 0.167 · 5: 104 ± 0 · 10: 107 ± 0.615 · 20: 116 ± 0.989 · 40: 127 ± 1.29 (mean ± SE, 6 runs each)
